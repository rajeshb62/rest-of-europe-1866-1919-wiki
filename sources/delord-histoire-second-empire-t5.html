Delord — Histoire du second empire, Tome V

Taxile Delord (1815–1877) — Histoire du second empire (1848–1869). — Tome cinquième. — Paris: Librairie Germer Baillière, rue de l’École-de-Médecine, 17, 1874. Printer: Imprimerie de E. Martinet, rue Mignon, 2.

Republican journalist and deputy (Siècle), Delord writes a contemporary opposition history of the Second Empire as a continuous Histoire, not memoir. The series band on the half-title is 1848–1870. This volume carries the running title L’Empire (suite) and is the fifth tome of the set. Body text runs through printed p. 632 (Fin du cinquième volume); table of contents follows (to Fin de la table des matières du cinquième volume).

Core chronological span is 1867–1869: from the letter of 19 January 1867 and the legislative session through the Luxembourg crisis, the Exposition, Maximilian’s death, Mentana, Rochefort’s Lanterne, the Baudin subscription and trial, the elections of 1869, the extraordinary session, the formation of the Ollivier ministry (Préface de l’Empire libéral), and the preliminaries of the Vatican Council (opening 8 December 1869).

OCR from local Anna’s Archive scan set (OCLC 6266334; Baillière 1869–75). Cleaned raw keeps title-page through end of table; internal double-spaces collapsed; front blank library leaves dropped. Tome II (authoritarian Empire through early 1860) sits in the 1800–1865 wiki.

Chapters

Chapitre I — La Session (1867)

Letter of 19 January, legislative session, press and meetings bills, foreign affairs and Luxembourg, fall of Walewski.

Opening of 1867: Café de la Renaissance students’ trial; Jules Favre’s candidacy at the Academy; death of Victor Cousin. The imperial letter of 19 January replaces the address with a regulated right of interpellation and announces press and meetings laws. Senate: Troplong, senatus-consult of 7 February, primary-instruction law, Sainte-Beuve’s speech and the storm it raises. Corps législatif: Walewski; Jules Favre and Rouher; Émile Ollivier rallies to the majority and astonishes his friends; foreign-affairs interpellation (Thiers, Favre, Garnier-Pagès, Ollivier, Rouher); Rouher evokes 2 December; Walewski resigns; Schneider president, Jérôme David vice-president. Luxembourg affair; Moustier’s declaration; municipal-councils law; pension to Lamartine; Berryer and Mexico; budget; Ollivier attacks the ministry and calls Rouher vice-empereur; the Emperor answers by sending Rouher the diamond plaque of the Légion d’honneur. Close and summary of the 1867 session.

Chapitre II — La Neutralisation du Luxembourg (1867)

Post-Sadowa compensations, Benedetti talks, Dutch cession project, London conference, neutralization.

Need to restore imperial prestige after Sadowa; territorial compensations claimed from Prussia; dilatory reception; Prussia refuses any left-bank cession; Bismarck leaves France free to seek compensations in Belgium and Switzerland; the Benedetti treaty draft. Diplomatic situation at the end of 1866; Holland denounces the 1839 treaty; secret Franco-Dutch understanding on cession; the secret breaks; Prussian anger and Bismarck’s reply in the Berlin parliament; circular of 15 April; conference announced; neutralization of Luxembourg accepted as a truce; press exaltation and democratic division on war; Berlin students’ reply to Strasbourg students.

Chapitre III — L’Exposition de l’Industrie (1867)

Universal Exhibition, sovereigns in Paris, Berezowski attentat, Rome on the horizon.

Preparations and inauguration; real opening in early May; King of the Belgians; the Tsar’s hesitations calmed by Piétri; King William I of Prussia arrives reluctantly; false position of Napoleon III and his guests; review in the Bois; Berezowski attentat, trial, and condemnation; Tuileries ball; departure of Alexander II and William I; Viceroy of Egypt; the Sultan; sudden departure of the Empress; King of Italy expected in vain (General Dumont’s mission to Rome); the Roman question reappears; Paris transformed; the Frenchman and Frenchwoman of 1867; Emperor and Empress; prizes and closure; impression on foreigners.

Chapitre IV — Mort de Maximilien (1867)

Evacuation of Mexico, Querétaro, execution of the archduke.

Imperial dispatch of 10 January 1867 to General Castelnau; Napoleon III leaves to Washington the choice of a Mexican republican president, excluding only Juárez; Bazaine’s talks with Porfirio Díaz; French evacuation and Bazaine’s return to Toulon without military honours. Maximilian’s clerical attempt to hold the empire; march on Querétaro; siege and capture by the Juarists; Escobedo announces the capture; defence before the court-martial; condemnation; letter to Juárez; execution; effect on opinion; diplomacy and the question of responsibility.

Chapitre V — Le Congrès de la Paix (1867)

International Workingmen’s Association, peace leagues, Geneva congress, Garibaldi.

IWA in France and its hesitation to enter politics; Geneva and Lausanne congresses; Ligue de la paix (Passy, Michel Chevalier, Arlès-Dufour, Père Gratry, etc.); English peace society; Phare de la Loire call for an international peace congress; adhesions across Europe; presidency offered to Garibaldi; his arrival at Geneva; he proclaims the fall of the papacy; congress slides into mysticism and revolutionary tendency; Genevan government unease; dissolution and results.

Chapitre VI — Entrevue de Salzbourg (1867)

Dumont at Rome, Salzburg meeting, Moustier circular, Bismarck reply, Fleury at Vienna.

General unease; General Dumont’s mission to Rome; elections of conseils généraux; departure of Emperor and Empress for Germany; Salzburg interview and its consequences; speeches at Arras, Lille, and Amiens; Moustier circular; Bismarck’s reply; court to Biarritz; General Fleury’s journey to Vienna; Italian manifestations; death of Fould; state of minds; Emperor’s return to Paris (16 October).

Chapitre VII — Mentana (1867)

Garibaldian invasion of the Papal States, French intervention, battle of Mentana.

Garibaldi organizes a new expedition against Rome after leaving Geneva; Italian army on the Roman frontier; volunteers enter papal territory; first encounters with pontifical troops; Florence’s difficulty stopping the invasion; will the imperial government intervene? Empress for intervention; Emperor hesitates; Persigny and then Rouher rally; mixed occupation rejected; riot in Rome; French troops at Civitavecchia; battle of Mentana; General de Failly’s cruel mot; Garibaldi arrested; Spanish intervention negotiated between Queen Isabella and the Empress.

Chapitre VIII — Crise dans le gouvernement (1867)

Austrian emperor in Paris, Montmartre manifestation, financial and food crisis, Pinard ministry.

Arrival of the Emperor of Austria; Hôtel de Ville banquet; Italian crisis and state of minds; results of the Geneva congress; manifestation of 4 November at the Montmartre cemetery; workers’ summons to the Paris deputies; war fears; financial and food crisis; “grève du milliard”; seditious placards; government feels weakened; Magne at Finance; choice of interior minister; Rouher’s report on candidates; Pinard named under the Empress’s influence.

Chapitre IX — Session de 1868

Opening 18 November 1867, Roman question, Rouher’s « jamais », military law.

Throne speech; Roman question in the Senate (Moustier) and Corps législatif (Rouher’s famous jamais); German affairs; Buffet amendment on interpellation; Kerveguen–Guéroult–Havin incident; talk of dissolving the Corps législatif; military law; majority’s unease.

Chapitre X — Décadence de l’Empire (1867)

Parti de l’action, Montmartre, “Commune révolutionnaire,” first symptoms of decline.

New party of action; manifestation of 2 November; conspiracy styled the revolutionary Commune of the Paris workers; girls’ education; end of the year; bad business situation; grève du milliard; general discontent; Emperor’s illness; first symptoms of the Empire’s decadence. Pinard installed 15 November finds the Montmartre manifestation the first fruit of the Geneva alliance between militant republican youth and the proletariat.

Chapitre XI — La Lanterne (1868)

Recognition of the North German Confederation, press prosecutions, Rochefort’s Lanterne.

Tuileries receptions; recognition of the Confederation of the North German States; Château-d’Eau slides; arrests and condemnations; ten papers prosecuted for illicit reports; petition against higher education; Dupanloup brochures; Odéon manifestation; Manin exhumation and blocked Italian commission; fat dinner on Good Friday; Emperor at Rouen; force disperses a private electoral meeting at Nîmes; troubles at the École de médecine; election of Jules Grévy; first political meetings in Paris; La Lanterne.

Chapitre XII — Session de 1868

Press law, public meetings, Sainte-Beuve in the Senate, finances and Haussmann.

Senate: Chaix-d’Est-Ange on liberty of teaching; press-law debate; Rouher fails to close discussion; Sainte-Beuve’s speech and the snub the Senate gives him; popular libraries. Corps législatif: interpellations on newspaper trials and Paris cemeteries; general discussion of the press law (Pelletan, Jules Simon, Jules Favre, Thiers); weak reply by Pinard; article 1 carried; stamp; Guilloutet amendment; Berryer additional articles; accounts question and Thiers; Granier de Cassagnac’s coarseness toward Favre; public-meetings law voted; Brame and Pouyer-Quertier on the economic regime; Rouher–Ollivier dispute; Haussmann’s report and apology; finance laws; Mexican-bond indemnity; moral chastisement inflicted on Rouher; Thiers’s speech; Emperor at Troyes.

Chapitre XIII — La Souscription Baudin (1868)

Troyes and Châlons, Walewski’s death, Baudin’s tomb, monument subscription.

Emperor at Troyes and at the camp of Châlons; King of Prussia’s allocution alarms the Bourse; Berne congress; International at Brussels; elections of the Var, Moselle, and Nièvre; death of Walewski; Emperor returns from Biarritz; Baudin’s tomb at Montparnasse; manifestation around the tomb; subscription for a monument; numerous adhesions; letter of Berryer.

Chapitre XIV — Le Procès Baudin (1868)

Trial of the Avenir and Réveil, Gambetta’s speech, Berryer’s death.

Political importance of the prosecution opened over the Baudin subscription; choice of counsel — Léon Gambetta, Laurier, Emmanuel Arago, Leblond, Hubbard; opening; second sitting; Gambetta’s prodigious plea; judgment; second trial of Avenir and Réveil with Temps and Journal de Paris; death of Berryer; public meetings; general agitation; rumours of conspiracy; start of electoral agitation; threatened Montmartre manifestation and useless police measures. Delord presents the trial as a democratic opportunity to put the coup of 2 December back on the stage.

Chapitre XV — Session législative (1869)

Last session of the 1863 legislature, press law effects, Paris–Crédit foncier, budget.

Rumours of new liberal reforms; opening of the Chambers; Emperor’s speech. Senate: Maupas interpellation on the new press law; petitions; Paris–Crédit foncier treaty. Corps législatif: chamberlains question; half-defeat of the government; interpellation on the Réunion riot and press law; Paris finances and danger of the discussion; government act of contrition; Haussmann and Frémy; Rouher poses the cabinet question; strong opposition minority; general budget debate; Thiers on electoral corruption of 1863; budget voted; end of the 1869 session and of the 1863 legislature; glance over that legislature.

Chapitre XVI — Les Élections générales (1869)

Centenary of Napoleon I, campaign, Gambetta–Carnot, Rochefort, white blouses.

New Year speech; pictures restored to the Louvre; Magne’s financial report; Belgian question; Muséum revolution; Saint-Mandé banquet; centenary of Napoleon I; Emperor at Chartres; abstention and blank votes; failure of the Union libérale; red spectre; remaniement of electoral circumscriptions; campaign opens 3 May; Bancel and Ollivier; Châtelet meeting and troubles; Ollivier’s profession of faith; 1st circumscription Gambetta vs Carnot; Gambetta’s electors’ cahier; Thiers, Raspail, Guéroult; Rochefort’s candidacy; results and second ballot; Thiers, Jules Favre, Garnier-Pagès, Jules Ferry elected; troubles in Paris; the blouses blanches.

Chapitre XVII — Session extraordinaire (1869)

Ricamarie, Schneider crisis, tiers-parti interpellation, Rouher to the Senate presidency.

Persigny’s letter to Ollivier; rumours of liberal concessions denied in a letter to Mackau; Ricamarie affair; Franco-Belgian railway conference; Solferino anniversary; Châlons camp allocution; Beauvais speech; Schneider’s resignation and withdrawal; opening of the extraordinary session; Pyrénées-Orientales election; rastels; left and tiers-parti interpellations; Rouher’s manœuvre; right interpellation; new ministry formed; Rouher president of the Senate; effect on the Chamber; left protest project; senatus-consult discussion; Devienne report; Prince Napoléon’s speech.

Chapitre XVIII — Les Élections complémentaires (1869)

Emperor’s health rumours, convocation decree, Ledru-Rollin candidacy, left manifesto.

Alarming rumours on the Emperor’s health officially denied; threat of inquiry; Basel congress; Gorchakov in Paris; calls to convoke the Chambers (Kératry, Gambetta); decree of 3 October convoking Chambers for 29 November; abandoned manifestation project; Ferry’s collective left protest dropped; Aubin strike; complementary elections; unsworn candidates; Ledru-Rollin candidacy offered, profession of faith, hesitation, Rochefort’s journey to London, final refusal; election results; left manifesto.

Chapitre XIX — Reprise et fin de la session extraordinaire de 1869

Louvre inauguration, left interpellations, collapse of the majority, end of the authoritarian Empire.

Session inaugurated at the Louvre; imperial speech; public admitted to Senate sittings. Corps législatif: four left interpellations; left bill on constituent power; bureau; Rochefort’s proposal; centre left and centre right; dissolution of the majority and difficulty of forming one; Raspail demands indictment of the ministry; verification of powers; Raspail–Rochefort proposal; rastels; elections of Girault and Clément Duvernois; debates lose interest; end of verification and of the extraordinary session; last days of the authoritarian Empire.

Chapitre XX — Préface de l’Empire libéral (1869)

Ollivier–Duvernois negotiations, disguised visit to Compiègne, ministry of 2 January 1870 prepared.

Negotiations resume between the Emperor and Émile Ollivier through Clément Duvernois; grave embarrassments — the government must recede or advance; can the Empire transform itself? Ollivier offers to attempt the trial; his conditions and programme; disguised journey to Compiègne; Emperor hesitates and makes his will; Ollivier returns to Saint-Tropez then is summoned back to Paris; he accepts the ministry; ministerial negotiations; Daru, Buffet; Girardin’s discontent; Magne blocks Duvernois; rupture between the two founders of the liberal Empire; Emperor’s letter of 27 December; Ollivier communicates it to the two centres; Segris and Talhouët will enter only with Daru and Buffet; Magne sacrificed; definitive formation of the ministry; effects on public opinion.

Chapitre XXI — Préliminaires du Concile (1867–1869)

Infallibility propaganda, liberal Catholics, Hyacinthe, Dupanloup polemics, opening 8 December 1869.

Announcement of the council; propaganda for papal infallibility; new devotion; league of infallibility encouraged by Pius IX; alarms of some bishops; Malines congress; Bishop of Orléans, professional schools, and the teaching league; episcopate and Duruy; clergy and the imperial family; Deguerry chaplain to the Prince imperial; Sorbonne incident; Father Schrader’s book and attacks on Bossuet approved by bishops and Pope; Pope’s letter to the archbishop of Paris; Civiltà cattolica article; infallibility assured in advance; liberal Catholics and the council; dogmatization of the Syllabus; Assumption dogma; episcopal mandements; episcopate and the 1869 elections; Montalembert’s letter to German Catholics; Mgr Maret’s book; Father Hyacinthe’s protest and excommunication; imperial government and the council; polemics between Dupanloup and Manning / Deschamps; Dupanloup’s pastoral on Veuillot; Rome ends the bishops’ polemic by refusing the imprimatur; opening of the council 8 December without special incident; Sixtine preparatory allocution — remedies for Christian and civil society’s ills: the remedy is the Syllabus.
