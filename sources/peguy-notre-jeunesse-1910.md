# Charles Péguy — *Notre jeunesse* (1910)

**Charles Péguy** (1873–1914), poet, essayist and founder of the *Cahiers de la Quinzaine*, wrote *Notre jeunesse* as the twelfth cahier of the eleventh series of his review, published in 1910. It stands as his fullest statement on the meaning of the Dreyfus affair — which he calls "our youth" — and on the difference between the *mystique* of republican and revolutionary faith and its degeneration into *politique*. The essay, addressed in part to his collaborators Daniel Halévy and Jean Variot, is written as an "examen de conscience," a public self-examination of the generation that had fought for the revision of the Dreyfus trial a decade earlier.

## The Milliet family and the tissue of republican faith

Péguy opens by recounting how Paul Milliet brought to the *Cahiers* the archives of his republican, Fourierist family — letters from Victor Hugo and Béranger, papers touching the conquest of Algeria, the Mexican expedition, the Crimean war. What Péguy insists he wants is not the letters of the great men, of which he says the libraries are full, but precisely the letters of ordinary people, "the admirables troupes" behind the great republican leaders. He seeks "l'histologie ethnique" — the tissue of an ordinary republican family, taken "à plein drap, à même le drap," to see what the people and the bourgeoisie and the republic were when "les républicains étaient héroïques et que la République avait les mains pures."

He recalls the generation of his youth as the last carriers of a "mystique républicaine," a lived faith rather than a set of propositions. The intermediate generation, he holds, has lost the instinct of republican mystique — "ça fait vingt ans." Péguy situates himself as "l'arrière-garde," a rear-guard "un peu isolée," the last generation that possessed the republican mystique. The Dreyfus affair was, for him, the last surge of this heroism, "le dernier sursaut, le soubresaut suprême de cet héroïsme et de cette mystique."

## The modern world against culture

Péguy contrasts his generation with what he calls "le monde moderne," those who "ne croient plus à rien, qui s'en font gloire et orgueil," who make a virtue of believing nothing — "pas même à l'athéisme." He insists the movement of the de-republicanisation of France is the same deep movement as its de-Christianisation, a single movement of "démystication," by which the people no longer believes in the Republic or in God. This is "la stérilité moderne," which dries up equally the political city and the Christian city. The modern world, he writes, opposes not only the ancien régime but every old culture together: "c'est la première fois dans l'histoire du monde que tout un monde vit et prospère, paraît prospérer contre toute culture."

He does not say this is forever — "cette race en a vu bien d'autres" — and hopes the following generation will be "enfin une génération mystique." But for the present, the Republic has ceased to be lived and has become a "thèse": "quand un régime, d'organique est devenu logique, et de vivant historique, c'est un régime qui est par terre." What had been for his fathers a living instinct is for the young a set of propositions, logical rather than organic.

## The great dichotomy: men of eternal salvation and men of temporal salvation

The heart of the essay is Péguy's account of what divided the two sides in the Dreyfus affair. His adversaries, he says, spoke the language of reason of state, of the temporal salvation of the people and the race — that one must not sacrifice a whole people for one man. The Dreyfusards, by contrast, spoke in a deep Christian movement: "une seule injustice, un seul crime... suffit à rompre tout le pacte social, tout le contrat social." One blot dishonours a whole people; "l'honneur d'un peuple est d'un seul tenant." He draws the corneille and Christian maxim, "je rendrai mon sang pur comme je l'ai reçu."

The true division of the affair, for Péguy, was between "les hommes du salut éternel" and "les hommes du salut temporel." The Dreyfusards "ne voulions pas que la France fût constituée en état de péché mortel." To illustrate, he invokes the passage of Joinville's life of Saint Louis in which the saint tells the clerk that he would rather be a leper thirty times over than commit a single mortal sin — for the leprosy of the body is cured at death, while the leprosy of the soul in mortal sin endures as long as God is in paradise. Péguy reads the Dreyfusards' refusal to accept an official injustice as precisely this Christian preference for the health of the soul over the body.

## Bernard-Lazare and the prophet of the affair

Péguy honours Bernard-Lazare — "né à Nîmes le 14 juin 1865; mort à Paris le premier septembre 1903" — as the prophet of the affair, the first to defend Dreyfus and the one whose word he calls "le mot décisif de l'affaire." He recounts how, even as some Dreyfusards grumbled at the returned captain's imperfections, Bernard-Lazare defended him "avec cet admirable aveuglement volontaire de ceux qui aiment vraiment": "Il est innocent, c'est déjà beaucoup."

## Mystique and politique

Péguy closes by drawing the distinction that gives the essay its enduring fame. The political parties, he writes, can speak only the political, parliamentary language, and they translate, betray (*traducunt, tradunt*) everything that is done for the mystiques. "La mystique républicaine, c'était quand on mourait pour la République; la politique républicaine, c'est à présent qu'on en vit." Since the degradation of the Dreyfusard mystique into Dreyfusard politique, Péguy says he and his collaborators have known fifteen years of poverty defending Christian consciences and republican liberties, only to find reactionary politicians forbid them being republicans and republican politicians forbid them being Christians. He answers that the *Cahiers* are "une compagnie parfaitement libre d'hommes qui tous croient à quelque chose."

Addressing the royalists of the *Action française*, he concedes that the only force of a traditional monarchy is that the king is loved — "la seule force, la seule valeur, la seule dignité de tout, c'est d'être aimé" — and finds in Maurras's phrase "nous serions prêts à mourir pour le roi" the one thing that speaks to him, for it is a truth of faith rather than an argument. Yet he holds that one founds no culture on derision and sarcasm. The essay ends with the words of Michel Arnauld at a gathering of the *Cahiers*: the royalists are but "une menace imprécise et théorique," and "le jour où ils deviendraient une menace réelle ils verraient ce que nous sommes encore capables de faire pour la République." Péguy closes with "FIN."

## Historical significance

*Notre jeunesse* is one of the defining texts of the Dreyfusard generation, and a central statement of Péguy's intellectual and spiritual position in the decade before the First World War. In it he aligns the republican mystique with the Christian, sets the eternal above the temporal salvation of France, and warns of a "monde moderne" that prospers against every culture — a theme he would carry into his later poetry and essays before his death at the Marne in September 1914. The essay belongs to the wider quarrel over the meaning of the Republic and the Dreyfus affair that occupied French intellectuals in the years between the affair and the war.
