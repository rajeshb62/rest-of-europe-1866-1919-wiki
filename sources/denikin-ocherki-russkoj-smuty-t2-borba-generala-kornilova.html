AuthorGen. A. I. Denikin — participant and memoirist of the “Kornilov movement,” Bykhov prisoner, then chief of staff and (from 31 March / 13 April 1918) commander of the Volunteer Army after Kornilov’s death

Character of the source. Staff, participant memoir of the White camp; Denikin writes as eyewitness of the Kornilov rising, prisoner of Bykhov, and chief of staff / successor to Kornilov. Tone apologetic toward Kornilov (opening speech: “eternal memory”), critical of Kerensky and of the “adulterers of the word.” Complements Vol. 1, issue 1 (to the 1917 offensive) and joins Zhevakhov, Vol. 2 (Kiev–South, different genre) on the 1917–20 calendar.

Preface

Speech delivered by the author at Ekaterinodar in 1919 (note: Brussels, 1922). “On 31 March 1918 a Russian grenade, directed by the hand of a Russian man, struck down a great Russian patriot.” Curse on those who in Kornilov’s lifetime awaited his execution and promised flowers on his grave; address to those faithful to Kornilov’s “testaments.”

I. The Kornilov movement and rising (Aug. 1917)

Chapter I. Divergence of the paths of the revolution. Inevitability of a coup

Generalizing the forces of the revolution into “two resultants” (Provisional Government and Soviet) fits only the first months; by July–August — a many-sided struggle. Poles: government, CEC of the Soviet, supreme command. After 3–5 July and the crisis of power — Kerensky’s third government; emancipation from the soviets formal; “inevitability of a coup” as the frame of the volume.

Chapter II. Beginning of the struggle: General Kornilov, Kerensky, and Savinkov. The Kornilov “memorandum” on army reorganization

In the Kerensky–Kornilov struggle, “absence of direct political and social slogans” that would divide the sides. Kornilov, per Denikin, had no definite “political programme” before the rising; the document known under that name is “the fruit of later” collective work. The “memorandum” on army reorganization; Savinkov’s roles.

Chapter III. The Kornilov movement: secret organizations, the officer corps, Russian public opinion

Lifting the veil over the “secret” of mid-1917. After the failure of the June offensive the officer corps moves into opposition to the government; “effective” opposition on a wide scale has not yet taken shape. Secret organizations, officers, the public.

Chapter IV. Ideology of the Kornilov movement. Preparation of the rising. The “political encirclement.” The three-sided “conspiracy”

The terms “affair,” “rising,” “conspiracy,” “mutiny” are narrow; more correct — Kornilov movement, and for 27–31 August — Kornilov rising. Ideology, preparation, “political encirclement,” “three-sided conspiracy.”

Chapter V. Kerensky’s provocation: V. Lvov’s mission, declaration to the country of the Supreme Commander’s “mutiny”

By late August Kerensky still wavers — with Kornilov against the soviets or with the soviets against Kornilov; Savinkov weighs; Kornilov has decided on a change in the structure of power, but not the methods; Krymov does not doubt the force of arms. V. N. Lvov’s mission; declaration of “mutiny.”

Chapter VI. General Kornilov’s rising. Stavka, commanders, Allied representatives, Russian public opinion, organizations, General Krymov’s troops — in the days of the rising. Death of General Krymov. Negotiations on liquidating the rising

Chaos in the “opposing camp.” Kerensky orders Lukomsky, then Klembovsky, to assume the supreme command — both refuse. Role of Stavka, Allies, the public, Krymov’s troops; Krymov’s death; liquidation talks.

Chapter VII. Liquidation of Stavka. Arrest of General Kornilov. Kerensky’s victory — prelude to Bolshevism

“Direction of means for liquidating Stavka”; Vitebsk and Smolensk committees; Korotkov’s detachment. Arrest of Kornilov; Kerensky’s “victory” as prelude to Bolshevism (the heading’s direct formula).

II. Bykhov, autumn of power, October, departure for the Don

Chapter VIII. Transfer of the “Berdichev group” to Bykhov. Life at Bykhov. General Romanovsky

The “Berdichev group of arrestees” goes to Stary Bykhov; prison life; portrait of Gen. I. P. Romanovsky. (The volume’s illustrative block richly documents Bykhov.)

Chapter IX. Relations of Bykhov, Stavka, and Kerensky. Plans for the future. The “Kornilov programme”

Shablovsky’s investigative commission; relations of the prisoners, Stavka, and Kerensky; plans; the so-called “Kornilov programme.”

Chapter X. Results of Kerensky’s victory: solitude of power; gradual capture by the soviets; breakdown of state life. Foreign policy of the government and soviets

“Kerensky has won” — an army without leaders, administration — “leaders without an army.” Cardinal question of constructing supreme power; progressive capture by the soviets; breakdown; foreign policy.

Chapter XI. Military reforms of Kerensky — Verkhovsky — Verderevsky. Condition of the army in September, October. German occupation of Moonsund

War Minister Verkhovsky, Navy — Verderevsky; “adaptability” to Soviet moods. Condition of the army in autumn 1917; German occupation of Moonsund.

Chapter XII. The Bolshevik coup. Attempts at resistance. Gatchina. Finale of Kerensky’s dictatorship. Attitude to events at Stavka and Bykhov

Causes of “non-resistance” to the advent of Bolshevism (fatigue, land/peace slogans, organized minority); Lenin’s 1905 quote on the impossibility of immediate socialism. Gatchina; Kerensky’s finale; reaction of Stavka and Bykhov.

Chapter XIII. First days of Bolshevism in the country and army. Fate of the Bykhov prisoners. Death of General Dukhonin. Our departure from Bykhov for the Don

Sovnarkom decrees (armistice, land, workers’ control, self-determination of peoples). Belief in the regime’s ephemerality (“two weeks”). Fate of the Bykhov prisoners; death of Gen. Dukhonin; departure for the Don.

Chapter XIV. Arrival on the Don of General Alekseev and birth of the “Alekseev organization.” Pull toward the Don. General Kaledin

2 November Alekseev at Novocherkassk; beginning of an armed force “destined… to play so considerable a role.” Pull toward the Don; Gen. Kaledin.

III. The South at the beginning of 1918 and the Volunteer Army

Chapter XV. General sketch of the military-political situation at the beginning of 1918 in Ukraine, the Don, Kuban, the North Caucasus, and Transcaucasia

“Balkanization” after the collapse of central authority — national, territorial, “pseudo-historical,” and accidental criteria. Survey of Ukraine, the Don, Kuban, the North Caucasus, Transcaucasia.

Chapter XVI. The “Moscow Centre.” Link of Moscow with the Don. Arrival on the Don of General Kornilov. Attempts to organize state power in the South: the “triumvirate” Alekseev — Kornilov — Kaledin; the “council”; internal frictions

Kaledin recognizes the need for joint struggle; “triumvirate”; “council”; frictions. Link with the “Moscow Centre”; Kornilov’s arrival on the Don.

Chapter XVII. Formation of the Volunteer Army. Its tasks. Spiritual aspect of the first volunteers

Kornilov’s hesitations (scarcity of means, frictions, “incomplete power”); pressure of “all-Russian” scale vs. local business. Tasks of the army; spiritual aspect of the first volunteers.

Chapter XVIII. End of the old army. Organization of the Red Guard. Beginning of Soviet armed struggle against Ukraine and the Don. Allied policy; role of the Czechoslovak and Polish corps. Fighting of the Volunteer Army and Don partisans on the approaches to Rostov and Novocherkassk. Abandonment of Rostov by the Volunteer Army

Brest-Litovsk — “bargaining” and “burning shame.” End of the old army; Red Guard; Allies; Czechoslovaks and Poles; fighting near Rostov and Novocherkassk; abandonment of Rostov.

IV. The First Kuban (“Ice”) March

Chapter XIX. The First Kuban March. From Rostov to the Kuban: military council at Olginskaya; fall of the Don; popular moods; fight at Lezhanka; a new tragedy of the Russian officer corps

“We were leaving.” Madness in the wake — plunder, murder of the wounded, fear of families. Council at Olginskaya; fall of the Don; Lezhanka; “new tragedy” of the officer corps.

Chapter XX. March toward Ekaterinodar: mood of the Kuban; fights at Berezanka, Vyselki, and Korenovskaya; news of the fall of Ekaterinodar

23 February — entry into the Kuban region. “Kuban — the promised land”; greeting, bread and salt. Fights; news of the fall of Ekaterinodar.

Chapter XXI. Turn of the army south: fight at Ust-Laba; Kuban Bolshevism; army headquarters

Night march; Bolshevik pressure on Bogaevsky’s rear-guard; fight at Ust-Laba; Kuban Bolshevism; arrangement of headquarters.

Chapter XXII. March into the Trans-Kuban; fight beyond the Laba and at Filippovsky; shady sides of army life

Night of 8 March — crossing the Laba (junkers in cold water “blowing bubbles”). Continuous Bolshevik encirclement of khutors; “shady sides” of life.

Chapter XXIII. Fate of Ekaterinodar and of the Kuban volunteer detachment; meeting with it

Abandonment of Ekaterinodar by “Kuban government troops” — psychology no less than military necessity. Kuban volunteer detachment; meeting with the Volunteer Army.

Chapter XXIV. The Ice March — fight of 15 March at Novo-Dmitrievskaya. Treaty with the Kubantsy on joining the Kuban detachment to the army. March on Ekaterinodar

15 March — the Ice March — “glory of Markov and the Officer Regiment, pride of the Volunteer Army.” Rain, water, and mud; treaty with the Kubantsy; march on Ekaterinodar.

Chapter XXV. Assault on Ekaterinodar

By 27 March — Erdeli’s cavalry and Bogaevsky’s 2nd Brigade on the right bank of the Kuban; Markov’s brigade covers the train. Bold design “not carried to its logical end”: the leader’s sense of humanity vs. tactical speed of concentration.

Chapter XXVI. Death of General Kornilov

31 March — artillery shelling of the farm district. Kornilov is asked to move headquarters: “It is not worth it now; tomorrow the assault.” Last conversation with the author; death. (The volume’s opening speech rhymes with this chapter.)

V. After Kornilov — Denikin’s command, the Don, Drozdovtsy, Germans

Chapter XXVII. My assumption of command of the Volunteer Army. Lifting the siege of Ekaterinodar. Fights at Gnachbau and Medvedovskaya. Feat of General Markov

Chief of staff: “Will you take command of the army?” — “Yes.” Lifting the siege; fights; feat of Gen. Markov.

Chapter XXVIII. March east — from Dyadkovskaya to Uspenskaya; tragedy of the wounded; life in the Kuban

Dyadkovskaya — day halt, bread and salt of the stanitsa people; plan to confuse the Bolsheviks by suddenness. Tragedy of the wounded; life in the Kuban.

Chapter XXIX. Risings on the Don and in the Kuban. Return of the army to the Don. Fights at Gorkaya Balka and Lezhanka. Liberation of Zadonye

News from Prochnookopskaya and the Labinsk department; preservation of a secret organization. Return to the Don; Gorkaya Balka, Lezhanka; liberation of Zadonye.

Chapter XXX. The Drozdovtsy march

With arrival on the Don — contact with the outside world. 23 April the Don militia of the Southern Group of Col. Denisov takes Novocherkassk. March of Col. Drozdovsky’s detachment; cautious reports of Gen. Kislyakov.

Chapter XXXI. German invasion of the Don. Contact with the outside world and three problems: unity of the front, foreign “orientation,” and political slogans. Results of the First Kuban March

Strengthening of the Don capital; marching ataman Popov; “provisional government.” Germans on the Don; three problems (unity of the front, orientation, slogans). Results of the First Kuban March: “Two and a half years more their struggle lasted” — and the scattering of the survivors across Slavic lands, Allied camps, and “night shelters” of the Old and New World. Closing of the volume: when “peace rests” over the country, the people will remember “those who first rose in defence of Russia against the red scourge.”

Volume apparatus (lists)

At the end of the OCR — a list of maps (Krymov’s echelons 29.8.1917; Moonsund; Kornilov’s march with the Tekin Regiment; situation Jan. 1918; map of the First Kuban March by dates; Kuban detachment; assault on Ekaterinodar; Drozdovtsy march) and a list of illustrations (Kornilov 1906 and 1917, Bykhov prison and prisoners, Romanovsky, Dukhonin, Markov, badge of the First Kuban March, photograph of Kornilov’s body, Drozdovsky, and others).
