Hanotaux — Histoire de la France contemporaine, Tome I (Le Gouvernement de M. Thiers)

Gabriel Hanotaux, Histoire de la France contemporaine (1871–1900), tome premier. Paris: Ancienne Librairie Furne, Combet & Cie, Éditeurs, 5, rue Palatine (VIᵉ); no imprint year on the scanned copy (Internet Archive metadata: n.d.; publisher field Paris, Combet). On the Internet Archive as histoiredelafran01hanouoft (University of Ottawa / Robarts–University of Toronto copy; ABBYY FineReader OCR; 678 images). The half-title names the subject Le Gouvernement de M. Thiers. Series framing (author’s Avertissement and IA description): I Le gouvernement de M. Thiers; II–III La présidence du maréchal de Mac Mahon (two volumes); IV La République parlementaire. This wiki holds Tome I, Tome II (L’Échec de la Monarchie), Tome III, and Tome IV.

In the Avertissement Hanotaux undertakes the history of contemporary France from February 1871 to the end of 1900. The present volume contains, with the government of M. Thiers, the end of the Franco-German war, the peace negotiations, the Commune, the constitutional crisis, the debates of the National Assembly, and the liberation of the territory; it stops at 24 May 1873. The second volume is to be devoted to the presidency of Marshal Mac-Mahon and the foundation of the Republic; the third and fourth will take up the history of the parliamentary Republic. Hanotaux, then of the Académie française, presents the work as a “dossier” of democracy: he had been sixteen in 1870, came to Paris a few months after the Commune, and writes as one who has seen the facts he recounts.

Dominant years in the text are 1871–1873.

Avertissement

Programme of the four-volume series; personal origins of the book in the war of 1870 and its immediate consequences; the generation that left childhood under the shock of defeat; questions of France’s past greatness, present defeat, and coming recovery; insufficiency of democratic memory of problems already posed; the book as a contemporary “dossier.”

Chapitre premier — La Guerre

France in 1870; the imperial policy of nationalities leads to war with Prussia; portrait of Napoleon III as a reduced copy of the first Bonaparte. The Government of National Defence; conclusion of the armistice; France after the war. Elections of 8 February 1871: the great debate War or Peace dominates politics; republicans for guerre à outrance; peace lists in the departments. Meeting of the National Assembly at Bordeaux; all eyes turn to M. Thiers, elected in twenty-six departments with nearly two million votes as “administrator of the national misfortune.” Portrait of Thiers: contrast with Napoleon III (prudence, experience, foresight against adventure); Marseillais origins, Histoire de la Révolution française, the National and 1830; short stature, spectacles, imperturbable aplomb. He is named chief of the executive power.

Chapitre II — L’Assemblée Nationale à Bordeaux

Constitutional crisis: four times in the century hereditary succession has failed; the principle of hereditary sovereignty itself is shaken; popular sovereignty and the law of majorities replace divine right, with the difficulties of representation. Organisation of Thiers’s government; recognition by the foreign powers; cabinet of 19 February 1871; first administrative measures. Negotiation of the preliminaries of peace and their ratification; Thiers at Bismarck’s (21 February); Bismarck’s reserved hand versus French openness and the passion for peace after Favre’s earlier mission; Alsace and Lorraine, the question of Metz and Belfort. Versailles becomes capital. Dispositions of the monarchical parties; the comte de Chambord; first steps toward fusion; Orléans princes elected deputies; the Biarritz understanding; the pacte de Bordeaux.

Chapitre III — La Commune

Origins and explosion of the Paris insurrection after the Assembly’s measures and the attempt to seize the guns of Montmartre (18 March 1871). Dual power: Versailles and the Commune; composition and factions of the revolutionary government; the Central Committee, the International, Blanquists and Jacobins. Military operations of the civil war; the siege of Paris by the Versailles army under Mac-Mahon; provincial risings and their failure. The Bloody Week; repression; legal and political aftermath. The chapter carries the narrative from the March rupture through the crushing of the Commune and the reassertion of national authority at Versailles.

Chapitre IV — Première Crise constitutionnelle

After peace and repression, the Assembly still has given no constitution. Struggle over the form of the state while Thiers governs under a provisional title. Parties and groups at Versailles; monarchical majority without a single candidate; republican minority growing with by-elections. The Rivet constitution (law of 31 August 1871): Thiers becomes President of the Republic, remaining responsible before the Assembly; provisional organisation of the public powers. First crisis over the extent of presidential authority and the Assembly’s constituent claim; Vitet’s report and the reluctance to silence “France’s incomparable orator” by message only.

Chapitre V — Le Traité de Francfort

From the preliminaries of Versailles to the definitive Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871). Territorial clauses (Alsace and part of Lorraine); the five-milliard indemnity and occupation schedule; commercial and transit stipulations. Thiers’s negotiation under the double pressure of a Paris still armed and already insurgent and of a country resolved on peace; debate whether Metz might have been saved and whether the London conference was neglected; Belfort retained for France and a milliard struck from the original demand. Ratification and the first organisation of payments and occupation zones.

Chapitre VI — Vers la libération

General balance of the war; the indemnity as Germanic wehrgeld on French savings and future. Thiers declines Bismarck’s introduction of German financiers (Bleichröder, Henckel) and stakes the payment on France itself. The loan of two milliards (law and issue of June 1871): perpetual rentes at 82 fr. 50; 381,906 subscribers; relations with the Bank of France and prior war borrowings. Pouyer-Quertier at Berlin; conventions of 12 October 1871; beginning of the evacuation of German troops. Discussion and vote of new taxes; elections to the general councils of 8 October 1871. Bismarck’s policy: rapprochement of Germany and Austria-Hungary; meetings of the two emperors at Ischl, Gastein, and Salzburg.

Chapitre VII — Le Travail parlementaire

Winter session of the National Assembly; Thiers’s message of 7 December 1871 (long, obscure, sparing the name Republic, upholding the Assembly’s sovereignty, offering to return the deposit intact). Parties and groups; Versailles as “Palais de la Pénitence”; the Orléans princes in the Chamber; Falloux and the flag question; fiscal debates. First resignation of Thiers (20 January 1872) after defeat on the raw-materials tax and the Féray–Lucien Brun resolution; emotion in all groups; Mac-Mahon joins the pressure to retain him; Batbie’s conciliation commission; Thiers withdraws the resignation. Parliamentary fusion manœuvres; Bonapartist propaganda; budget of 1872; Gambetta in the provinces, Thiers at Paris.

Chapitre VIII — L’Apogée

Session of 29 April–3 August 1872 as the apogee of Thiers’s government: fifteen months after Bordeaux, peace signed, Commune defeated, reconstruction under way. Interpellations; debate on the war contracts; inquiry on the capitulations; Marshal Bazaine sent before a court-martial. Negotiations for payment of the last three milliards; military law of 27 July 1872; convention of 29 June; budget of 1873 and new taxes; the three-milliard loan. Parliamentary situation: the centre left adheres to the Republic; attempt at a “conjunction of the centres”; Council of the Nine; the “bonnets à poil” demonstration. Vacations: Thiers at Trouville; Gambetta’s second oratorical campaign (La Ferté-sous-Jouarre; Savoie and Dauphiné; Grenoble, 26 September 1872 — the “new social stratum”; praise of Thiers at Annecy; clericalism named the remaining enemy). Alsace-Lorraine; expulsion of Prince Napoléon; Chambord and the Orléans princes; religious manifestations; elections of 20 October 1872.

Chapitre IX — La Libération du territoire

Germany after victory; Bismarck’s foreign policy and fear of French revanche (circular of Reims, instructions to Arnim); meeting of the three emperors; the Kulturkampf. Winter session; Thiers’s message of 13 November 1872 pronouncing for the Republic; protest of the right; commission of Fifteen demands ministerial responsibility; commission of Thirty; ministerial reshuffles; debate on dissolution; the government breaks with the left. Legislative work; goods of the Orléans family; death of Napoleon III; further fusion efforts; Chambord’s letter to Mgr Dupanloup (8 February 1873); the Roman question. Negotiation for anticipated evacuation: Gontaut-Biron, Saint-Vallier, Arnim, and Bismarck; the Belfort reserve and the substitution of Verdun; convention of liberation signed 15 March 1873; the Assembly declares that Thiers has deserved well of the country. Thiers yields ground to the commission of Thirty; the law of the Thirty; Thiers is edged from the tribune even as liberation is secured.

Chapitre X — Le 24 Mai

Party struggle at the height of exasperation. War-contracts commission as a combat weapon; attack on Challemel-Lacour over the Lyon markets (30 January 1873) and his three-hour defence. Municipal regime of Lyon; petition of Prince Napoléon after his expulsion; pact among the three monarchical parties. Resignation of Jules Grévy; Buffet president of the Assembly. Elections of 27 April 1873: Barodet elected at Paris; further elections of 11 May; resignations of Jules Simon and de Goulard. Conciliabules fix on Marshal Mac-Mahon as presidential candidate; Thiers reshuffles his ministry; interpellation of the right; Thiers deposits bills on the organisation of the public powers. Sitting of 23 May: duc de Broglie and Dufaure; sitting of 24 May: Thiers’s great speech (peace signed, Commune defeated, indemnity paid, evacuation decided; “I have taken my party on the Republic”); declaration of Casimir-Perier; the Target group. Thiers, put in minority, resigns. Mac-Mahon elected president (390 votes; the left abstains except Laurier for Grévy); he accepts after hesitation and a call on Thiers, with a soldier’s word to continue liberation and restore ordre moral. Conclusion: three years of French misfortune after a prosperous century; dismemberment, civil war, and the shift of European weight to a reconstituted Germany; yet the “imperishable France” of activity and radiance, the prompt recovery of the people, the dignity of the National Assembly, and Thiers’s triple task (peace, repair, durable political form) — with the judgment that the right toppled him before he could found the Republic, and that conservatives and republicans alike misread the act.

Series note

Tome I ends at 24 May 1873 (fall of Thiers; election of Mac-Mahon). Continuity — the two first Broglie ministries, ordre moral, the monarchical campaign and Chambord’s letter of 27 October, the Septennat, fall of Broglie (16 May 1874), and the recovery chapters — is the matter of Tome II (La Présidence du Maréchal de Mac Mahon — L’Échec de la Monarchie). The Constitution of 1875 through the letter of 16 May 1877 is Tome III; the parliamentary Republic through Gambetta’s death is Tome IV.
