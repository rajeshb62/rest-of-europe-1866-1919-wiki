# Kornilov — Course of Russian History in the 19th Century, Part 3

**A. A. Kornilov (1862–1925)** — *Курс истории России XIX века. Часть III.* — Second edition. — Moscow: M. and S. Sabashnikov Publishers, **1918**.

The course is based on lectures given by the author in 1910–1912 at the Higher Women’s Courses, the Shanyavsky People’s University, and the Moscow Commercial Institute. The first edition of Part III appeared around 1914 (preface — Petersburg, 2 April 1914); this second edition — Moscow, 1918. Parts I and II of the same course (2nd ed., 1918) are in the [Rest of Europe 1800–1865 wiki](../../Rest%20of%20Europe%201800-1865/wiki/sources/kornilov-kurs-istorii-rossii-ch1-2.md).

**Contents of Part III.** In the preface the author recalls that Part III was originally meant to cover “the internal history of Russia over the last 35 years of the 19th century.” The abundance of material and the “decisive turn in the mood of society” after the famine of 1891–92 led him to set aside the last eight years of the 19th century together with the first years of the 20th as a separate period — “a direct prelude to the great events of 1904–1906” — which might have become a fourth part of the course; in 1914 he expressed no confidence that such a fourth part would be written. The published Part III treats the **reactionary period** of recent internal history that began in 1866 and, with a brief clearing in 1880–1881, ran **to the famine of 1891–92** — from the Karakozov attempt through the end of Alexander II’s reign and the whole reign of Alexander III. This epoch, the author notes, had until then received little systematic study.

**Character of the course.** Kornilov belongs to the liberal-populist school of Russian historiography. The course is attentive to socio-economic processes, the peasant question, the zemstvo, education, populism, and the revolutionary movement; the exposition is lecture-style, with discussion of sources and literature. Pre-reform orthography. OCR quality — medium, with artifacts (Ѣ/Е, І/И confusion, garbled Roman lecture numerals, etc.).

**Raw volume:** ~1.28 MB, 17,879 lines. Lectures XXVII–XLI (15 lectures) plus extensive bibliographic lists for the reigns of Alexander II and Alexander III and statistical appendices (population density, distribution of landownership).

## Part III — Lectures XXVII–XLI

### Lecture XXVII. The Karakozov attempt and the onset of reaction

The attempt of 4 April 1866. The reaction that followed; wherein it consisted. Development of Russian internal life after the reforms of the 1860s despite reaction. Continuation of some transformations. Completion of the peasant reform by extending it to appanage and state peasants. Overall picture of landownership of various groups of the population after the reforms of 1861, 1863, and 1866. Degree of land allotment security for peasants of different categories.

### Lecture XXVIII. Immediate results of the peasant reform

Population growth in Russia before and after the reform. Critique of views in the literature on this subject. Settlement of the population before and after the reform. Growth of the urban population. Economic condition of Russia after the reform era. The industrial crisis and its causes.

### Lecture XXIX. The financial position in the reform era and subsequent years

Tasks of financial policy under M. Kh. Reutern. Reutern’s activity. Drive to raise the country’s general productivity and especially export trade. The railway-building question; course of the matter to the mid-1870s. Concern for organizing commercial credit. Opening of private banks. Struggle with deficits in the state budget. The question of continuing internal reforms and Reutern’s report.

### Lecture XXX. The Municipal Statute of 1870 and Miliutin’s military reforms

Drafting of the new municipal statute. Former urban arrangements in law and in practice. Work of the 1840s. Course of work on the statute of 1870; content and criticism. The question of army reorganization. Reforms of D. A. Miliutin. Abolition of recruitment and the statute on universal military service of 1874. Cultural and educational significance of the transformations in the War Ministry.

### Lecture XXXI. The Ministry of Public Instruction after 1866

Count D. A. Tolstoy and D. A. Miliutin as exponents of two opposite sides of Alexander II’s reign. Tolstoy’s views. Tolstoy and Katkov. The question of secondary-school reform. The struggle for the introduction of classicism. Essence and significance of the reform of 1871. Tolstoy’s plans regarding the universities and the measures he took. Development of women’s education in 1866–1878. Elementary education. Statutes of 1864 and 1874. The ministry’s struggle with the zemstvo. History of the zemstvo school in 1866–1880.

### Lecture XXXII. Zemstvo institutions, 1866–1878

Development of zemstvo institutions. Sphere of zemstvo activity; tasks and means. Zemstvo dues and obligations in the pre-reform period. Zemstvo budgets. Drive to tax trade and industry and collision with the government. Zemstvo expenditures and their growth. Class interests in the zemstvos. The question of natural obligations and the assessment of zemstvo dues. The tax-reform question and zemstvo projects. Government struggle with the zemstvo and restriction of its activity.

### Lecture XXXIII. Judicial institutions and the press after 1866

Opening of the new judicial institutions and their first steps. Valuev’s struggle against them. Reactionary activity of Count Pahlen. Beginning of the position of the judicial statutes. Position and direction of the procuracy. Special procedure for initiating and deciding cases of state crimes. Change in the position of the bar and the question of removing various cases from jury competence. Position of the press in the post-reform period. Principal press organs and literary currents after 1866. Mood of society.

### Lecture XXXIV. The popular masses and agriculture in the 1860s–1870s

Course of the redemption operation. Pauper allotments. Cut-offs (*otrezki*). Leases in northern and southern provinces. Rise in lease prices. Landowners’ indebtedness. Intensified ploughing up of land in view of grain export and rising grain prices. Livestock numbers among landowners and peasants. Scale of landowners’ ploughland. Conclusions on landowners’ economy in the north and south. Sale of lands; purchasers. Expansion of peasant economy at the expense of landowners’. Condition of peasant economy: insufficiency of allotments, burden of taxes and payments, inequality of assessment. Famine of 1868. Zemstvo investigations. Arrears. Peasant economy in the black-earth provinces. The Samara famine of 1872–1873. Food loans. Conclusions of Yanson and Prince Vasilchikov on the condition of peasant economy in the 1870s.

### Lecture XXXV. Government, society, and the populist movement

Attitude of government and society to the condition of the popular masses. The populist current in literature. Student disturbances of 1869. Nechaev and Nechaevism. The Chaikovtsy in Petersburg; their ideas and plans. Bakunin and Lavrov abroad. Lavrists and Bakuninists. *Vpered*. Beginning of “going to the people.” Arrests. Pahlen’s memorandum. The society *Zemlya i Volya*. Development of populist ideas in legal literature.

### Lecture XXXVI. The borderlands, the Eastern Question, and the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–1878

Government policy in the borderlands. Pressures in Little Russia and Poland. Foreign policy. The Eastern Question. Rivalry of Russian and English interests in Asia. Conquest of the Caucasus and the Central Asian khanates. Troubles in Turkey. Movement of the Balkan Slavs. The Serbian war and the Bulgarian massacres. Negotiations of the great powers. Russo-Turkish War of 1877–1878: course and outcome. The Berlin Congress. Economic and financial results of the war. Reutern’s resignation. Impression of the war and congress on Russian society. Slavophiles. The zemstvo movement. The revolutionary movement. Government appeal to society. Zemstvo addresses. Formation of the party *Narodnaya Volya*.

### Lecture XXXVII. Attempts on Alexander II, Loris-Melikov, and 1 March 1881

Organization by revolutionaries of a series of attempts on the life of Emperor Alexander II. Confusion and vacillation in the highest circles. Explosion in the Winter Palace and establishment of the Supreme Administrative Commission headed by Loris-Melikov. Loris-Melikov’s programme and its realization. Attitude of liberals and revolutionaries toward him. Tolstoy’s resignation. Loris-Melikov’s reforms. Senatorial inspections and the peasant question. The constitutional movement. Loris-Melikov’s report on establishing a special preparatory commission. The catastrophe of 1 March 1881.

*Further in the volume — list of works and printed materials relating to the history of Russia in the reign of Alexander II.*

### Lecture XXXVIII. Emperor Alexander III — first steps

His upbringing. Attitude of society toward him before accession. His real views. First steps. Struggle of two tendencies in the highest governing circles. Conference of 8 March 1881. Vacillations. Katkov and Aksakov. Pobedonostsev’s agitation. Manifesto of 29 April 1881. Resignation of Loris-Melikov and some other ministers. Ministry of N. P. Ignatiev. His programme. Measures to improve the economic condition of the people. Compulsory redemption. Noble agitation. “Knowledgeable people.” Reduction of redemption payments. Bunge’s policy. Abolition of the poll tax. Introduction of an income tax (question and measures).

### Lecture XXXIX. Peasant policy at the beginning of Alexander III’s reign

Measures to ease peasant land shortage. The Peasants’ Bank and its first steps under Bunge. Facilitation of lease of state lands to peasants. Ordering of peasant resettlement. Rules of 1881. Law of 13 July 1889. Introduction of factory inspection and the law on protection of minors and women in factories. Taxes on inheritances and on interest-bearing securities. The question of administrative peasant reform. The Kokhanov commission: participants and liquidation of its work. Collapse of the Ignatiev regime.

### Lecture XL. Decisive turn toward reaction

Role of Pobedonostsev. Count D. A. Tolstoy. Reaction in the Ministry of Public Instruction. Noble reactionary policy in internal affairs. Jubilee of the Charter to the Nobility in 1885 and the noble reactionary movement connected with it. Pazukhin’s programme. Liquidation of the question of reforming peasant institutions after the closure of the Kokhanov commission. Law of 12 July 1889 on land captains (*zemskie nachalniki*). Statute on zemstvo institutions of 12 June 1890. Judicial novellas. New press law of 1882 and the position of the press. Persecution of non-Orthodox and non-Russians. The Jewish question. New arrangements in the army.

### Lecture XLI. Finances of the second half of Alexander III’s reign and foreign policy

Financial policy. I. A. Vyshnegradsky and his system. Extreme development of protectionism in tariff policy and in railway tariff legislation. Results of this system. Foreign policy of Alexander III. Conquest of Turkmenia. Russo-English relations in Central Asia. Balkan affairs. Bulgarian complications. The Franco-Russian alliance and its significance. The Siberian railway and the beginning of a new policy in the Far East.

*Further in the volume — list of works and printed materials relating to the history of Russia in the reign of Alexander III; table of contents; statistical appendices (population density by province; distribution of landownership by category of owners).*
