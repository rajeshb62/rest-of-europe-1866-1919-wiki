## Chapters

### Preface

Author’s introduction to the *Essays*; the task — the collapse of authority and of the army in 1917 as the first act of the “Russian Time of Troubles.”

### Chapter I. Foundations of the old regime: faith, tsar, and fatherland

“The inevitable historical process that culminated in the February Revolution led to the collapse of Russian statehood.” No one expected the elemental force so quickly to sweep away the supreme power, the intelligentsia, and the “ten-million-strong army” within three to four months.

### Chapter II. Condition of the old army before the revolution

The Japanese war as a turning point: bitterness of defeat, a rise among the military youth, inertia at the top of the War Ministry and the General Staff.

### Chapter III. The old army and the sovereign

August 1915 — Nicholas II assumes the supreme command “under the influence of the Empress’s circles and Rasputin”; fruitless representations by eight ministers; official motives (unifying government and command; risk of responsibility in a zone of retreats) vs. fear of incompetence and of the “German-Rasputin” entourage.

### Chapter IV. The revolution in Petrograd

The author learned the details “afterward”; for continuity — a brief summary. Telegram of members of the State Council on the night of 28 February: disruption of transport, factory stoppages, food crisis, hatred of the government.

### Chapter V. The revolution and the imperial family

The sovereign at Mogilev “alone, without family, without intimates”; Protopopov and the government at first see “popular disturbances” to be met by “decisive measures”; machine-guns for Khabalov; General Ivanov with a detachment.

### Chapter VI. The revolution and the army. — Order No. 1

Denikin on the Romanian Front, 8th Army Corps; the front “absorbed in local concerns,” winter offensive; isolation from the capital. **Order No. 1** as a blow to discipline (developed further in the chapter).

### Chapter VII. Impressions of Petrograd at the end of March 1917

Decrees concerning Prince Lvov and Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich as Supreme Commander; 9 March — communication of the Provisional Government on the undesirability of leaving the Grand Duke in post; letter of Prince Lvov.

### Chapter VIII. Stavka; its role and position

**25 March** — Denikin’s arrival at Stavka; reception by Alekseev; refusal/hesitation over a staff post; appointment as quartermaster-general on condition of frankness “as with my old professor.”

### Chapter IX. Trifles of life at Stavka

Mogilev — “a small, quiet, half-Jewish town”; jokes about “Mogilev — the grave”; modesty of the former imperial court in the governor’s house; guards and passports.

### Chapter X. General Markov

Second quartermaster-general; S. L. Markov invited by Denikin; “bound his fate inseparably” until a “glorious death” at the head of the volunteer division that bore his name. War path: Alekseev’s staff → 19th Division → Stavka.

### Chapter XI. Power: Duma, Provisional Government, command, Soviet of Workers’ and Soldiers’ Deputies

War + revolution require “strong authority”; after 27 February the Duma does not open a formal sitting, seeing itself as an institution of the “old order”; refusal to head revolutionary power; dual power.

### Chapter XII. Power: the Bolsheviks’ struggle for power; power of the army; the idea of dictatorship

First Bolshevik period — to October: breaking the “bourgeois order” and disorganizing the army. Lenin’s theses on arrival — in extract. The idea of a military dictatorship in conversations of the command.

### Chapter XIII. Activity of the Provisional Government: internal policy, civil administration; town and countryside, the agrarian question

A schematic sketch of internal conditions only “insofar as they affected the conduct of the world war.” Shulgin on dual power: “the old authority sits in the Peter and Paul Fortress, and the new — under house…”

### Chapter XIV. Activity of the Provisional Government: food, industry, transport, and finance

From spring 1917 — food shortage in the army and cities; official norms (~1–1½ pounds of bread for the army, ¾ pound for the population) not met; cities starving; crises on the fronts (except the South-Western).

### Chapter XV. Position of the Central Powers by spring 1917

The front as Stavka’s first question; weakness of foreign intelligence (the Paris organization of “negative reputation”); nonetheless — sufficient knowledge; the reality of Germany “exceeds all our assumptions.”

### Chapter XVI. Strategic position of the Russian front by spring 1917

The army by March 1917 — “an imposing force”; mobilization of industry, War-Industry Committee; 48th Corps / TAON (heavy artillery of special designation); reorganization of engineer troops; supply via Murman and Arkhangelsk.

### Chapter XVII. The question of the Russian army going over to the offensive

Central operational question of the issue. Provisional Government’s address of 27 March “to the citizens” — for Stavka “phrases” that obscure the meaning. Lack of coordination between western and eastern fronts; Ludendorff: had Russia attacked in April–May, the German high command might not have held the position — “only the Russian revolution saved us.” Episode of Miliukov’s idea of Constantinople / a landing; Zonguldak; troops “do not wish to go on a landing.” Closing of the issue: “the rusted, notched sword was still swinging.”
