# Hanotaux — *Histoire de la France contemporaine*, Tome IV (*La République Parlementaire*)

Gabriel Hanotaux, *Histoire de la France contemporaine (1871–1900)*, tome quatrième. Paris: Ancienne Librairie Furne, Société d'Édition Contemporaine, 5, rue Palatine (VIᵉ), **1903**. On the Internet Archive as [histoiredelafran04hano](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelafran04hano) (University of Ottawa copy; ABBYY FineReader OCR). The half-title names the subject **La République Parlementaire**. The series leaf (as cited in the *Avertissement* and front matter of peer volumes) frames the four tomes as: I Thiers; II Mac-Mahon and the failure of the monarchy; III Mac-Mahon and the Constitution of 1875; IV the parliamentary Republic. This wiki also holds [Tome I](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t1.md) (*Le Gouvernement de M. Thiers*), [Tome II](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t2.md) (*L'Échec de la Monarchie*), and [Tome III](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t3.md) (*La Constitution de 1875*), which ends at the letter of 16 May 1877; the present volume continues from that crisis.

In the *Avertissement* Hanotaux presents tome IV as completing the history of the Third Republic at the time of its birth and first growth — the age of uncertainties and struggles devoted to founding a new constitutional regime and to the recovery of the country. From **1883** a more stable political period (more troubled in the moral order) will test the new institutions; he suspends the narrative at the **death of Gambetta**, which he treats as a brutal conclusion after which figures and the aspect of things change. Dominant years in the text are **1877–1878**, with dense continuation through **1879–1882**.

## Avertissement

The volume closes the twelve-year “heroic age” of the Republic’s foundation. Hanotaux, writing too hastily by his own admission, records insufficiency of information, disproportion of parts, and stylistic failings, yet affirms renewed love of country and impatience, after having had to recount Bismarck’s successes, to return to France’s Richelieu. He suspends further narrative until more distance and impassibility are possible.

## Chapitre premier — Le Seize Mai

Emotion provoked by Mac-Mahon’s act of **16 May 1877**; plenary meeting of the Lefts; interpellation in the Chamber and Gambetta’s speech; constitution of the **cabinet Broglie**; presidential message and adjournment of the Chambers; manifesto of the Lefts; opinion; attitude of legitimists and Bonapartists; the government’s thesis. During the recess: ministerial circulars and administrative movements; the Marshal at Compiègne; Thiers’s role; Gambetta’s provincial speeches; the Left’s resolve to refuse the budget. Resumption of the session: second presidential message; the Marshal asks the Senate for dissolution of the Chamber; government declaration and interpellation; Fourtou; “the liberator of the territory”; union of republicans; **the 363**; Senate vote of dissolution; preparation of the elections.

## Chapitre II — La Question d'Orient

The Seize Mai crisis is set inside European liquidation after German hegemony. New European politics; France’s importance in the European system; consequences of German hegemony; Russia’s role and the Russo-German contest over the East; Russia appeals for French concurrence; the struggle framed as Slavism versus Germanism; England intervenes as third party; tactics of Bismarck and Gortschakoff; the **Pesth programme** of the Three Emperors. Origins of the Eastern Question: Turkish and Christian “reform,” the Russian crusade, traditional policies of the powers, Crimean War, Treaty of Paris, Hatti-Humayoun of 1856, situation on the eve of 1870. The two chancellors; Russia opens the crisis; English attitude. Recrudescence of disorders; Turkish armaments; **Berlin memorandum** (England alone refuses adhesion); interview of Ems; French efforts at conciliation; **Reichstadt convention**; Serbia and Montenegro against Turkey; Serbian defeat; Bulgarian atrocities; English opinion and Disraeli; accession of Abdul-Hamid; Bismarck for Austria and for opposing England to Russia; Russia not ready; **Constantinople conference**; European ultimatum rejected by Turkey; powers “playing close”; **London protocol**; agreement impossible and war inevitable.

## Chapitre III — Les Élections du 14 octobre 1877

Electoral campaign and official candidature; the Marshal pushed forward; republican party and union of the 363; Gambetta’s “submit or resign”; prosecution of Gambetta; quarrels among conservatives; delayed elections. The clerical question: Vatican and Seize Mai; jubilee of 1877; Crispi in France and Germany; German threats; the Left denounces the peril. Death of Thiers; Jules Grévy as chief of the republican party; the Marshal’s journey to Bordeaux and the West; manifesto of 19 September; governmental optimism. Elections of **14 October**: government beaten; composition of the new Chamber; ministry remains; departmental elections and a further republican victory; uncertainties; return of parliament; parliamentary inquiry on Seize Mai; fall of the Broglie ministry. **Cabinet Rochebouët**; Chamber refuses the budget; Dufaure called to the Élysée, the Marshal refuses his conditions; Batbie charged to form a ministry of resistance; general excitement; Labordère incident; Batbie fails; the Marshal calls Dufaure.

## Chapitre IV — Le second cabinet Dufaure et le « parti libéral »

Parliamentary work; senatorial majority; budget of 1878; liquidation of Seize Mai. Deaths of Victor-Emmanuel and Pius IX; projected interview of Bismarck and Gambetta; Gambetta’s journey to Rome; election of Leo XIII. Continuation of the 1878 session; diverse laws and proposals; the Universal Exposition.

## Chapitre V — La Guerre russo-turque et le Congrès de Berlin

The war: Gourko’s raid; Russian checks and **Plevna**; European impression; Romania enters the campaign; fall of Plevna and taking of Kars; Russians at Adrianople; English veto; armistice; Russians at San Stefano; Bismarck’s intervention — Germany as “honest broker”; **Treaty of San Stefano**; Schouwaloff–Salisbury convention; programme of the congress; European missions; France accepts the invitation with reserves. The congress: Bismarck’s tactics; revival of Anglo-Russian disagreement; work of the congress in Europe and Asia; Germanic expansion in the Balkans; satisfactions distributed among the great powers; **Cyprus convention** and the Egyptian question; **Tunisia offered to France**; birth of world policy.

## Chapitre X — Le Ministère Gambetta

Return of the Chambers; interpellations on the Tunisian affair; formation of the **Gambetta ministry**; difficult beginnings; Weiss and Miribel; Gambetta takes contact with the new Chamber. Exterior situation: Triple Alliance; Egyptian affairs; revolt of Arabi Pasha and the Anglo-French condominium; Gambetta and England. Ministerial activity and reform projects; state of opinion; triennial renewal of the Senate; project of constitutional revision and *scrutin de liste*; debate; government beaten; resignation of the Gambetta cabinet; second Freycinet ministry.

## Conclusion

Failure of the Gambetta cabinet cuts the wings of hope and disconcerts the logic of events. Bombardment of Alexandria and end of the condominium; fall of the Freycinet cabinet; Duclerc cabinet; last projects and **death of Gambetta**; the democratic and social Republic. Hanotaux closes the first period of the Third Republic — the “heroic age” Gambetta himself named — spanning the fall of the Second Empire, the German war, the Commune, the failure of Bourbon royalty, the Constitution of 1875, Seize Mai, and the foundation of the parliamentary Republic.
