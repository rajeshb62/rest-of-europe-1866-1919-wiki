Recouly — Histoire de la grande guerre, 1914-1918

Raymond Recouly (1876–1950), Histoire de la grande guerre, 1914-1918. Paris: Les Éditions de France, 20, avenue Rapp, 1934 (© 1934 by Les Éditions de France). A single-volume synthesis of some four hundred pages, drawn from the BnF scan (barcode 3 7502 00662084 5) via Anna’s Archive; the raw text in this wiki was obtained by OCR of the 416 scanned images (no embedded text layer). Recouly, a war correspondent and historian of the Third Republic, wrote the book “vingt ans après” — for the generation born at the moment the first shots were fired, who know nothing of the war at first hand, and for his own generation of soldiers and civilians who lived it. He declares his method at the outset: against the “anarchic and enfeebling theories” of Tolstoy, war is directed and conducted by chiefs; the intelligence and will of commanders matter as much as, if not more than, the courage of the commanded. The book rests on the published memoirs of the principals (Joffre, Foch, Pétain, Gallieni, Weygand, Haig, French, Pershing, Hindenburg, Ludendorff, von Kluck, von Kuhl, von Hausen), the official histories of the belligerent staffs, and a closing Bibliographie.

Dominant years in the text are 1914–1918.

Introduction — Vingt ans après

Written in 1934, twenty years after the outbreak. A generation has passed; thousands of young men entering the barracks were born at the first shots and know nothing of the war personally. Recouly proposes to gather, in a short space, the dominant features of the drama and its principal points, which like milestones mark the long road. His central thesis: Germany, for all its marvellous military preparation, was finally defeated by France and her allies because, too sure of its warlike superiority and committing its habitual sin of pride, it entered the conflict in bad diplomatic conditions and ranged too many enemies against itself. Conversely, the political (not military) defection of Russia brought victory within two fingers of Germany; the grave blunder of its military chiefs — ignorant of politics — in deciding on unrestricted submarine warfare and so provoking American intervention, added one more enemy. Only the threat of catastrophe compelled the Allies to accept a single command, confided to “a soldier of genius,” Foch. The Introduction also gives summary definitions of military terminology for readers unfamiliar with it (the division as the base unit of military organisation).

Chapitre premier — Causes et responsabilités

Opens on Sunday morning, 28 June 1914: the archduke-heir of Austria, Francis Ferdinand, and his morganatic wife assassinated at Sarajevo, capital of Bosnia-Herzegovina, the province occupied and then annexed by Austria, peopled chiefly by Slavs and Muslims. The double assassination in a distant Balkan town, “barely European,” provoked within a few weeks a war that lasted fifty-two months and in which the most powerful nations of the world were engaged. The chapter examines the causes and the responsibilities for the war.

Chapitre II — Mobilisations et déclarations de guerre

After the exchange of diplomatic notes that held the capitals in suspense during the tragic decade, the “chassé-croisé” of mobilisations and declarations of war. After a partial mobilisation, Austria’s declaration of war on Serbia opens the series of bellicose acts. General mobilisation of Russia, quickly followed — before the news reached Vienna — by the general mobilisation of Austria-Hungary. General mobilisation of Germany and of the other powers in turn.

Chapitre III — Les plans de campagne

The German plan. Germany, wedged between France and Russia, faces the problem of a war on two fronts, east and west. Almost on the morrow of the victories of 1870, Moltke the victor reflected on it, seeing in it “the most formidable test” that could threaten the existence of the new empire. The chapter sets out the pre-war strategic plans of the powers.

Chapitre IV — Concentration des armées — Premiers combats

Joffre, entirely won over to the idea of the offensive, on grounds both moral, political and military: the offensive answers to the French character and temperament; an army that has not fought in Europe for half a century can only regain confidence in itself through advance and attack. Formal engagements had been concluded between the French and Russian staffs binding both armies to attack the German forces as soon as they were ready. The chapter covers the concentration and the first combats of the frontier battles.

Chapitre V — La bataille de la Marne

Nothing is more complicated or confused than the battle of the Marne, if one tries to follow it in detail; nothing simpler or clearer if one holds to its great lines. A great battle always more or less partakes of chaos — like the hero of La Chartreuse de Parme wandering, blind, all day across the plain of Waterloo, which beside a modern battlefield seems no bigger than a pocket handkerchief. The skill and sang-froid of the French generalissimo, and the mistakes that excess of confidence made the German generalissimo commit, turned the situation; the battle of the Marne was lost, and at a stroke the German machine was half-dismantled — the war that was to have been short instead embarked on a long duration.

Chapitre VI — La course à la mer

The exploitation of victory having not given the results Joffre expected, he sought to accent the German retreat and free the territory, operating in the still-free country north of the Oise — a continuation of the manœuvre of the Marne. It collided with an analogous manœuvre by the enemy, and the two opposed movements tended to cancel each other out. In this race to the wing the Germans had an appreciable advantage, resulting from the very shape of the situation.

Chapitre VII — La guerre sur le front oriental

The decision that could not be obtained on the western front — the principal theatre, where the Germans had hoped the war would end in a few weeks — was equally impossible on the eastern front. There too, from the eddies of battle, after alternatives of victory and defeat for the Central Powers, the struggle threatened to drag on. It can be summarised thus: the German army, even with relatively reduced forces, almost always beat the Russian army, which was clearly inferior in high command and in certain qualities.

Chapitre VIII — Le mauvais bilan de 1915

On the western as on the eastern front, war everywhere came to a standstill. Winter, with its cold, fogs and rain, fell on the trenches, where millions of half-buried human beings lived like troglodytes, with no one able to foresee the end. War, until then dynamic, became static. The stabilisation of combat, imposed by the obstacle, by fortifications and the power of fire, was not in principle an absolute fact. The chapter weighs the poor balance-sheet of 1915 for the Allies.

Chapitre IX — Le redressement de 1916 — Verdun, la Somme, le plan des alliés

At the beginning of 1916, the third (though not the last) year of the war, how does the situation present itself for each of the two enemy blocs, and under what aspects to Joffre examining it at his headquarters at Chantilly with robust and clear common sense? The year that was ending had marked a setback for the Allied cause; yet the failures they suffered, especially in the East (for on the front in France they amounted only to a missed gain), were not catastrophic. The chapter covers Verdun, the Somme offensive, and the Allied plan of 1916.

Chapitre X — La disgrâce de Joffre et la révolution russe sauvent l’Allemagne

Judgment on Falkenhayn. The German failure before Verdun; the great Somme offensive which, on Ludendorff’s own admission, subjected the German armies to a most disquieting attrition; the entry of Romania into the war on the Allied side — three characteristic setbacks for the Central Powers within a few months, three very hard blows falling on the German staff. Yet the disgrace of Joffre and the Russian revolution were to save Germany.

Chapitre XI — Les offensives de Ludendorff

“La veillée des armes” (winter 1917–1918). After the military collapse of Russia, after the defeat of Romania, Germany could be said to have won the war, three-quarters of the way, on the eastern front; only the inter-Allied army of Salonika, maintained and reinforced thanks to the clairvoyance and energy of the French, prevented it from having won entirely. By the peace treaties signed with Russia and Romania (February–March 1918), Germany obtained what it sought. The chapter covers Ludendorff’s offensives of the spring of 1918.

Chapitre XII — Les offensives de Foch

There is in Casanova’s Mémoires a penetrating observation on man: he had only climbed, he said, up to a certain moment; from that point he begins to descend, and in a descent the more rapid the nearer he approaches the end — motus in fine velocior. The failure of 15 July followed, three days later, by a still graver failure, marks for Ludendorff the beginning of the descent that would become ever more rapid and soon vertiginous. The chapter covers Foch’s counter-offensives that broke the German army in 1918.

Chapitre XIII — Guerre et paix

To follow the unfolding of the war in its great lines, to understand the laws that determine it, one must observe it from above: it resembles an immense rugged landscape, with its summits and hollows, and one must dominate it to grasp its unity. In this unbroken sequence of events, at once political and military, there are very clear points of bifurcation where, according as the chiefs and the troops proved superior or inferior to their adversaries, merit or failure gave the facts a different direction from what they would otherwise have taken. The war became nearly inevitable the day the civil chiefs of Germany, the Kaiser first of all, seized by a contagious collective intoxication, were persuaded that Germany had not in the world the place to which it had a right; having developed its strength to the extreme limit, they awaited and seized the occasion to use it. The first battles of the frontiers seemed to give them reason; but the skill and sang-froid of the French generalissimo, and the errors of over-confidence by the German, turned the situation at the Marne. The chapter closes with the aftermath: a badly conceived and still worse executed treaty, and the “sombre perspective” of a new war only sixteen years after a war that had nearly laid France low. The tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Berlin bears the inscription of the scholar Wilamowitz-Moellendorff, INVICTIS VICTI VICTURI (“to the unconquered soldiers, today’s conquered who will be tomorrow’s conquerors”) — a lapidary phrase illustrating the state of mind of Germany on the morrow of its defeat. Germany proclaimed aloud that militarily it had suffered no defeat, that its soldiers had not been vanquished on the field by enemies from without but stabbed in the back by the triumphant revolution from within; and Recouly asks how those who had vanquished it with such difficulty, France first, could have committed the folly of not taking more solid precautions against it.

Bibliographie

A closing bibliography lists the works most frequently consulted: the official histories (the French Les armées françaises dans la grande guerre of the Service historique; the Reichsarchiv, der Weltkrieg 1914-1918; the Official History of the War; the Belgian Bulletin belge des sciences militaires; the Russian staff’s La Grande Guerre translated by Commandant Chapouilly, 1926; Österreich-Ungarns letzter Krieg), the memoirs of Foch, Joffre, Pétain (La Bataille de Verdun), Gallieni, Weygand (Le Onze Novembre), French, Haig’s dispatches, Pershing, Hindenburg (Aus meinem Leben), Ludendorff (Souvenirs de guerre), von Kluck (La Marche sur Paris), von Kuhl (La Campagne de la Marne en 1914), von Hausen (Souvenirs de la campagne de la Marne), and others.

Series note

This volume is a single, self-contained history of the whole war. It stands at the chronological end of the 1866–1919 period covered by this wiki — the First World War as the culminating event of the era treated by Sybel, Delord, Ollivier, Hanotaux, and the Russian works. Written from the perspective of 1934, it is a retrospective French synthesis (contemporary to interwar France) rather than a contemporary record of the war years themselves.
