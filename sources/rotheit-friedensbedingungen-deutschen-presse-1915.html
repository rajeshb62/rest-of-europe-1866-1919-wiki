Rudolf Rotheit — Die Friedensbedingungen der deutschen Presse / Los von Reuter und Havas!

Rudolf Rotheit, Auslandsredakteur der Vossischen Zeitung, Die Friedensbedingungen der deutschen Presse / Los von Reuter und Havas!, Puttkammer & Mühlbrecht (Buchhandlung für Staats- und Rechtswissenschaft), Berlin W. 56, Französische Straße 28, 1915 (OCLC 17172170). Short wartime pamphlet (~58 printed body pages) arguing that German foreign news traffic is trapped under the Reuter–Havas cartel through Wolff’s Telegraphisches Bureau, and that peace conditions for the German press must include liberation from that dependence by founding a large independent German international news bureau. The title page carries Crown Prince Wilhelm’s interview with the Berlin representative of the American United Press, attributing America’s attitude almost wholly to English press control and its worldwide connections, and admitting that Germany had not hitherto duly appreciated the role of the press in world politics.

Opening purpose and wartime awakening

PAGE 00000007 (title; lines ~36–66); PAGE 00000009–00000011 (printed pp. opening–5; lines ~71–161).

From his own impulse the author offers material toward clarifying and hopefully solving one of the most important problems of international relations, whose lasting neglect has done the German people heavy harm. A reordering of Germany’s news traffic with abroad has become an unavoidable demand after many most unpleasant phenomena. Politicians and representatives of German economic life who already before the war turned their attention to the subject will welcome closer insight into the relevant conditions. A large part of the pamphlet reproduces complaints about the defects of the news service and proposals for remedy; some may find that section too extensive and wearisome, yet the proof must be delivered how strongly and in how wide circles these evils have already made themselves felt. Failed attempts at improvement and newly emerged plans are treated, and the author also puts forward his own proposals; where he has not shrunk from calling things by their right names, that should be allowed him for factual reasons.

In the midst of the patriotic rising of the war’s outbreak and the victories in the field, the German people underwent a painful experience: a flood of hatred poured against it from the foreign press. From the papers of open enemies nothing else was to be expected; but what drove the sheets of neutral, friendly, and even allied states to such wild wrath? In a flash it came home to Germans — many of whom tend to underestimate the press — how dangerous this raging storm was. A poisonous seed had sprung up that had been systematically sown for years and carefully tended for the hour of decision. When passion against Germany flared from the Bering Sea to Cape Horn, when Japan dared to challenge Germany mockingly, when under the impression of false French victory reports agitators in Italy nearly led the country into fatal steps, and in Bucharest an ambitious temperament-politician sought on Russian and Serbian misrepresentations to drag the population demagogically against the Central Powers, fighting Germany had to take up with all speed also the “struggle for the souls of the neutrals.” All hands were called to the ramparts. What had so long been neglected was suddenly to be made good with the greatest conceivable effort under the rallying cry “Die Wahrheit ins Ausland!” — field fortifications thrown up in haste because permanent works were lacking. The German Reich, in the shining armour of its moral, military, and financial preparation, stood unarmed in this field. In every branch of social, economic, and scientific life Germany had shown the most brilliant successes and overtaken all rivals; only in news traffic had it remained in the tow of abroad, and in the art of influencing the foreign press it lagged far behind other great powers. How that came about, and what must happen so that to Germany’s lasting benefit the heavy mischief is at last removed — that is the task of the present writing.

Cartel geography and Wolff’s dependence

PAGE 00000011–00000017 (printed pp. 5–11; lines ~163–425).

Every newspaper reader knows the names Reuter and Havas. With some practice he will recognise a wire from Tokyo or Madrid, Sydney or Buenos Aires — even without an express mark — as having reached the German press via the London news-collecting point Reuter or the closely allied Paris Havas agency and thence through the Berlin Wolff bureau. These agencies and some lesser ones stand in contractual exchange with one another. In principle there is nothing against such a system; much of organised world traffic rests on similar arrangements. The question is only what role falls to each member. The modest corner to which Wolff has hitherto had to resign itself does not correspond to the place in the sun that even the pre-war German Reich might justly claim; and the relation between Reuter and Havas on one side and Wolff on the other answered fairly exactly to the throttling policy that envious abroad practised against the Reich until the explosion came. While Wolff was bound to Reuter and Havas, they worked in full freedom as effective handymen of encirclement diplomacy, goading the public opinion of the inhabited earth against Germany — by force of the vast world-spanning apparatus they had deliberately built, while Germany on this field was condemned to a Cinderella role.

That hangs in part with the known late-comer character of the German Reich, which at first had to be content with crumbs from foreign tables. Colonial and naval history and international banking (formerly wholly in French and English hands in Turkey and South America until German banks took up competition) furnish parallels; the German press was not granted to keep pace, and the German news service lagged for want of the springboard that colonial politicians and financial institutes received from government support or from the flourishing national economy.

Three kinds of territory are to be distinguished: (1) French and English possessions; (2) states with so-called national agencies; (3) states without agencies of their own. Reuter’s net covers Great Britain and all its colonies including Egypt; Havas covers France with all its colonies — German agency work there is as good as excluded and almost certainly forbidden to Wolff by cartel treaty (the treaties are kept secret). Even in most states with allegedly national news bureaux, Reuter and Havas sit on top behind a national cloak: the Roman Agenzia Stefani is a shoot of Havas fed with French money; likewise the Agence Roumaine, the Spanish Agencia Fabra, the Agence d’Athènes, and others. Wolff maintains nowhere branch establishments of that kind. The Petersburg Telegraph Agency, which geographically should have depended on Wolff, slipped away under the Russian Finance Ministry — always turning to France for loans — and drew closer to Havas and Reuter into anti-German waters, extending its news-suction apparatus over Mongolia, northern Manchuria, and Persia with French money.

Worse still are states without national agencies — South America, Japan — where the field is wholly occupied by Havas and Reuter: they may spread about Germany whatever they please, and Wolff can scarcely defend itself; Germany is delivered over to them hand and foot — nearly a muzzle. Yet precisely there entry and competition would be easiest to accomplish. The resulting map: Great Britain with colonies (Australia, Canada, South Africa, India, etc.), Holland, Belgium (a combined Reuter–Havas agency), Norway, China, Japan, Arabia and Mesopotamia, Central America and some lesser overseas states are Reuter territory; Havas works Spain, Portugal, Italy, a large part of the Balkans, and all South America; northern and central Asia are the domain of the Petersburg agency. For the Balkans and Turkey Wolff leans, not always to advantage, on the Vienna k. k. Telegraph Correspondence Bureau. Wolff’s original firm name, “Internationale Continental-Telegraphen-Compagnie,” remained continental — and even that frame is too wide. He has a representative each in Paris, London, and Petersburg, who by cartel must lean closely on the local cartel agencies; other representations he does not maintain. The only binding of foreign agencies by Wolff is a contractual duty to publish and forward certain specially marked telegrams sent at Wolff’s own cost — a duty the permanently anti-German foreign papers do not share; the waste-basket is used all the more eagerly in the editorial rooms.

Reuter and Havas receive current German factual material from Wolff for themselves and for onward transmission, yet nothing hinders them from also putting out other news from and about Germany, coloured so that German prestige, policy, and trade suffer damage — as notoriously happened for years. Other injury-means include forwarding spiteful cuttings from English or French papers under feigned disclaimer of responsibility, and distorting German press voices or speeches. The fear of Brazilians of the alleged German danger was, Rotheit asserts, generated and raised by Havas through years of underground work. On the other side Germany itself, because dependent on Reuter and Havas, lacked adequate political and commercial orientation on the most important fields: South America existed for Germany so to speak not at all — weeks, months, even years without news beyond empty cabinet lists or presidential messages tailored to Paris banking circles.

That at the beginning of the world war Germany’s own overseas cables were cut by the English is not the fault of Reuter and Havas; that during the war they misled the public abroad may even be allowed to pass, since they cut into their own flesh and will not soon recover lost trust. But it must never be forgotten how they already beforehand agitated against Germany, systematically stirred hatred, and by stubborn fight against German political and commercial standing share guilt for the war forced on the most peaceful of all peoples by unheard-of chicaneries. It falls to the German press to shake off these enemies. “Los von Reuter und Havas!” is therefore the field-cry. After the war a new settlement of Wolff’s relations with them will probably follow — under the Allied practice of declaring even private treaties void it is at least doubtful whether the old contract revives — and more favourable terms for the German partner might be won. But that alone does not serve: the danger of ever again falling into Reuter and Havas’s tow must be removed from the ground up. That can only mean: establishment of a large independent German news bureau on the broadest basis, with sufficient funds, complete organisation, and best performance.

Early warning voice: the “Auslandsdeutsche”

PAGE 00000018–00000019 (printed pp. 12–13; lines ~452–530).

Among the first to recognise the urgent necessity of such a creation was the excellent patriot who, in consular service under the pen-name “Der Auslandsdeutsche,” published a series of outstanding pamphlets. His 1906 Zürich writing Die Presse und die Weltpolitik opens at once with the indictment of Reuter and Havas: almost all civilised states have despatch bureaux bound somehow to publish only those political communications agreeable to the state directors of the day; almost all are subscribed to Reuter’s London bureau for foreign material, while for Romance states Havas performs that service — and Havas is newly closely allied with Reuter, so that the despatch “Zweibund” works nearly the whole world systematically in an anti-German sense with lying despatches. Officially satisfactory relations with Havas, Reuter, and their Romance and Anglo-Saxon branches appear for the present unfortunately wholly excluded; French and English power-holders have long understood the advantage of their international political situation, owed in part to their world despatch monopoly. German foreign policy cannot renounce influencing the public opinion of those states, which alone already mean half the world (three-quarters, adds the Referent). Partial undeniable failure of German foreign policy since Bismarck’s dismissal toward France, England, the United States, even Italy, is to be sought in insufficient contact with the press that works on the popular mass there. Among the “Auslandsdeutsche“‘s counsels is founding an independent German press bureau of large style — modestly pictured as some 50 to 100 German newspaper publishers forming a G.m.b.H. with contributions of 500 to 1,000 marks for five years provisionally. Of that plan and others more later; first the voices of banking and commercial world, politicians, publishers, and journalists.

Industry, overseas trade, and press voices for a German bureau

PAGE 00000019–00000033 (printed pp. 13–27; lines ~531–1210).

Bund der Industriellen (Berlin). On 21 June 1913 the Bund circulated to the press that one-sided influencing of the foreign press by certain French and English news bureaux had long been increasingly harmful also for overseas business relations and the sale of German products; the industry therefore had cause to turn increased attention to greater German influence on the international news service. Already on 15 April, in committee session, it had demanded that the Reich place means for improvement and completion of the international news service, preferably on the basis of founding a German international news bureau. A November 1914 reply to Rotheit’s inquiry recapitulates: improvement of journalistic service for trade-political and world-political interests hand in hand with improvement of armaments; the board on 25 June 1913, after hearing experts and receiving reports on preparatory work for a committee for safeguarding German economic interests, resolved to promote those preparations; with several committees pursuing the same goal, concentration is indispensable; the Bund hopes the Imperial Government and Reichstag will approve means to support such a news service, yet also recognise the necessary independence of the enterprise — points submitted to the competent authorities after the board meeting of 9 January 1914. The unanimous declaration on the Wehr- und Steuerfrage links means for an Internationales Deutsches Nachrichtenbüro with support of the Reich to countering the international press campaign against German policy and industry, as important as army and navy armaments for firming Germany’s place in the world.

Albert Ballin / HAPAG. At the end of September 1914 in the Hamburger Nachrichten, Generaldirektor Albert Ballin writes that the enemy has cut the German cables and so shown that he wishes to monopolise not only world trade but also the international news service; once abroad realises that English–French reports have granted themselves a moratorium for truth, that credit will be shaken like commercial credit once stained. It is not enough to bring truth abroad in wartime; after peace too, fruitful relations must be cultivated through the printed word and the telegraph wire — a prompt, reliable news service and true clear pictures of German life and work. How much that has been lacking, one sees today with horror.

Deutsch-Argentinischer Zentralverband. On 15 September 1914 the association for promoting economic interests (Berlin) told the press that too little attention had been paid in Germany to influencing the foreign press and general propaganda of German interests; timely and rightly spent thousands would today spare Germany as many millions, for the lying campaign against German morality aims at shaking and if possible destroying Germany’s hard-won economic position abroad. The many efforts called forth by the war must as soon as possible be gathered into one great powerful organisation.

Der Zeitungsverlag and related press voices. O. St. (17 May 1912) argues that private correspondences bring a vast mass of foreign reports largely not from themselves but from English, American, or French sources whose patriotic purposes — for much of that press equal to direct opposition to Germany — yield the monstrous result that anti-German-trimmed foreign reports are delivered to the German press under the cloak of sole validity; self-help is required: the German press must create from itself the possibility of being served from abroad by the fastest, i.e. telegraphic, route with all that is worth knowing in the national interest — a mighty costly apparatus needing the greatest possible outlay. Zeitungsverlag No. 39 of 1912 records that many German–American news items are still coloured in the English sense in London — a view shared by the Berlin publisher Dr. Franz Ullstein then in America, who told the Paris edition of the New-York Herald that direct news exchange between the United States and Germany is of the greatest importance for friendly relations, English bureaux trimming German government measures as America-hostile and colouring American trade and New York stock-exchange reports to devastating effect. A. F.’s New Year retrospect (3 January 1913) notes high Reichspost fees for overseas press traffic: so long as it is financially more advantageous to send a New York message via London to Berlin than direct, there can be no talk of a nationally independent cable service; a thorough reform exceeds any single entrepreneur — should not rivalry be softened enough for a common foreign service on the model of America’s Associated Press? Dr. Alfred Fiedler (1913 retrospect) stresses creating an adequate German foreign service independent of the German overseas press itself, which above all does not sufficiently supply foreign countries with uncoloured news of German conditions; the Revista del Pacifico experiment cannot break Havas’s undisputed monopoly of telegraphic supply to South American dailies. Reich Office of the Interior Nachrichten für Handel und Industrie arrive too late for exporters competing for foreign tenders; journalistic penetration of German interest areas needs a far-sighted independent reporting service — the most important task of the near future. Further Zeitungsverlag pieces (1 November 1912) complain that the highly semi-official Wolff bureau resorts to English agencies (here the London Commercial Telegram Bureau) even on matters of outstanding German interest — a state of affairs unthinkable in reverse for an English publisher.

Reichsverband der deutschen Presse (Leipzig, June 1914). Chairman Chefredakteur Marx notes great defects in the news service and caricatures of German intellectual life abroad. Wolff director Dr. Diez, reporting, finds complaints against Reuter largely unjustified except for East Asia — an Englishman sees the world with English eyes — and doubts founding a German world news organisation for want of means; improvements through trustful cooperation of official places with the press abroad, and an inner-European press tariff. Georg Bernhard (Berlin) insists Reuter is not so harmless — Reuter is a great financier with own interests especially in lands of future possibility; control of Reuter and Havas matter by Wolff has been extended, yet the controlling official can only control what flows through foreign pipes; a special German foreign service must be organised in areas of German economic and political interest by cooperation of press, publishers, and Wolff — industrial support appears questionable. The Stoffers motion is adopted: the Reichsverband deems expansion of the foreign news service through an independent purely German organisation an urgent necessity, with participation of the Reichsverband, the Verein deutscher Zeitungsverleger, and Wolff’s directors; a commission (Bernhard, Diez, Rippler, Stoffers, Ginschel, Mohr, Giesen) is charged with further negotiations.

Further wartime and earlier voices: Berlin local group of the Deutscher Schriftsteller-Verband (November 1914) leitmotifs after Günther Thomas on Germany and foreign public opinion — independent organisation of the German daily press for joint uninfluenced procurement of purely factual news from all parts of the world and a telegraph connection with the whole abroad independent of England; Verein Berliner Presse discussion (20 January 1915, Chefredakteur ten Brink) adjourned without resolution; Prof. C. H. Becker, Deutschland und der Islam, on how late Germany recognised the press preparation of the European war and how enemies’ news service rules the inhabited earth; a prominent German-American in the Vossische Zeitung (2 October 1914) on New York papers seeing everything European through English spectacles; Argentine report (Berliner Tageblatt, 3 October) calling for a German news bureau capital-strong enough to face Reuter and Havas, linked to the cut German cable via Monrovia; Kreuz-Zeitung (April 1906) on Algeciras — French, English, and Italian presses working as by programme, German papers writing only for Germany while Reuter and Havas fed coloured extracts abroad; Düsseldorfer Zeitung (8 April 1906) — make the German foreign news service independent; founding a rival to Wolff would be idle waste — rather enable Wolff with very large means to set up an independent German foreign service approximating Reuter’s, with support of Reich, federal states, press, industry, and trade, and simultaneous raising of subscription prices against the fifty-pfennig papers that prevent the press from flourishing.

The abundance of proofs is meant to show how deep and wide the need for thorough improvement is felt: So it cannot go on!

Failed remedies: Kabelgramm-Gesellschaft and the industrial syndicate

PAGE 00000033–00000046 (printed pp. 28–40; lines ~1222–1807).

Attempts at change have already been made and miscarried — instructively.

Some years earlier there arose in Berlin, with official favour, a new telegraph agency calling itself the Deutsche Kabelgramm-Gesellschaft, not intended to compete with Wolff but, with Wolff’s consent, to fill gaps in Wolff’s service — financed with apparently inadequate means, perhaps a small government subsidy. Its name announced the overseas service it meant to cultivate; as an independent enterprise it need not mind Reuter and Havas. The idea was not bad; the execution less praiseworthy. A news bureau that cannot compel papers to take its reports has missed its calling. After early indulgence, South American and other offerings proved such that they would scarcely have found notice as local items; the press gradually withdrew; the field shrank from South and Central America, Morocco, Persia, Japan, and China down to Japan and China alone, mostly unusable. Rotheit assumes the society often entrusted reporting as a sideline to journalistic dilettantes for slight pay — prophecy being the unfailing mark of the dilettante; the newspaperman wants what was and is, not what will be. Even had it hit the useful, as a mere receiving station for foreign reports without outbound stations for inland reports abroad it would have been wing-crippled. Numerous other telephone-spawned bureaux of recent years only flooded editorial rooms with superfluities; Paris as mid-point — cartel contracts among Paris and London papers, Paris editions of the Daily Mail and New York Herald — let a Paris correspondent pour Paris, London, and New York material, mostly anti-German coloured, onto Berlin desks. None of these agencies remedied the German news evils; some increased them.

In 1914 various runs were made at a German news apparatus answering manifold needs. The Magdeburgische Zeitung early in the year reported that efforts toward training and improving German news service to and from abroad had reached a certain fundamental close; committees had formed in the Rhenish–Westphalian industrial centre and in South Germany; in Berlin on the 4th of the month a meeting under Geheimer Justizrat Dr. Waldschmidt (Ludwig Löwe A.-G.) in the Herrenhaus urged avoiding fragmentation. In June 1914 the Berlin Deutsche Export-Revue reported a newly founded syndicat for Auslandsnachrichtendienst — board Geheimrat v. Borsig, Landrat a. D. Rötger, and Direktor Schacht of the Deutsche Bank; Verwaltungsrat including Duisberg, Hagen, Hasenclever, Hecht, Heineken (Norddeutscher Lloyd), Helfferich, Huldermann (Hapag), Kosegarten, v. Langen, Rathenau, Reuter (Maschinenfabrik Duisburg), Salomonsohn, v. Siemens, Bohlen, and others; Geschäftsführer Asch and Dr. Hansen. A loose three-year syndicate, minimum annual contribution 1,000 marks; the Auswärtiges Amt had granted a subsidy of 250,000 marks on condition that industry raise the like sum (already oversubscribed). The Export-Revue itself doubted sending out journalists as Reich-supported syndicate agents and preferred working through embassies, consulates, and German–foreign economic associations.

Berlin was angry at the publication as betrayal of a secret — yet the English Embassy already knew: Sir Edward Goschen had reported to London almost immediately after the Bund der Industriellen’s February memorial, and after the war outbreak the English government laid those reports before Parliament (September 1914) as “Reports of the Royal Ambassador in Berlin on an official German organisation for influencing the press of other countries.” Rotheit reprints them in translation: founding under government oversight of a powerful secret association to influence the foreign press in favour of German export trade and German influence generally; Ballin’s shift from a German–American economic association toward a more delicate secret organisation; a secret meeting at the Auswärtiges Amt prompted by Dr. Hamann of the Press Bureau, with Norddeutscher Lloyd, Hapag, Deutsche Bank, Diskonto-Gesellschaft, AEG, Siemens und Halske, Schuckert, Krupp, Grusonwerke, etc., founding a private society for “promotion of German industrial prestige abroad,” private contributions (enthusiasm rising to 500,000 marks yearly) plus 250,000 marks yearly from the Auswärtiges Amt’s entire secret fund for such purposes; intended arrangements with Havas (English footnote: never concluded) and Reuter, or else financing the Deutsche Kabelgesellschaft against Reuter; pooling of members’ foreign advertising budgets (at least another 500,000 marks) so that only papers taking the society’s German news receive announcements; agents abroad to watch the foreign press and wire corrections; South America and the Far East first; lowered German cable fees for press telegrams. Goschen’s later despatches report the Weekend-Telegram rate reductions from 1 April 1914 to the United States, Canada, Argentina, Chile, Peru, and German colonies, then extension to South African and Asian destinations — and the Export-Revue article of 5 June confirming the Auswärtiges Amt’s 250,000-mark secret-fund support, after which high official places ordered that the article neither be reprinted nor referred to. The Times (4 September 1914) headlined an extract: “Germany and the foreign press — International blackmail — Exposure of a conspiracy.”

Critique of industrial secret organisation; Associated Press contrast

PAGE 00000046–00000052 (printed pp. 40–46; lines ~1808–2062).

From the professional journalistic standpoint only a news service borne by the confidence of the entire German press and effectively supervised by it can give the German nation what it needs in this field. Examination of the “Gesellschaft zur Förderung des deutschen industriellen Ansehens im Auslande” proves the sentence. Already the goal is wrongly set in that circumscription and would drive the German news bureau — which has far more extensive tasks — into false paths. The danger that either real special interests of certain groups assert themselves or lurking mistrust feels entitled to such accusation must not be risked. Wolff himself, as property of a great bank, knows how often he was suspected of serving that house. The good will of bank and trade gentlemen is not to be doubted; sincerity only bids warn them against errors. They mean to undertake something that is not and cannot be their affair — as if journalists by conventicle resolution founded a new bank and told the bankers to use it as the normal institution. Men whose expertise on their own ground commands highest respect confront journalism in unsurpassable naïveté. It is illogical to call into life by shady ways an institution meant for the greatest possible publicity worldwide and to build it on a secret organisation and secret-police surveillance; illogical to unfold far-branching journalistic activity without thoroughly trained journalists (the Export-Revue report’s “Schließlich” on sending journalists is delicious — putting the horse on backwards); illogical that captains of trade who always cry for the open door on all world markets should seek to start the news service by striking monopolies for themselves through money and fair words. What matters is freeing the German news service from its fetters, not putting foreign news traffic in fetters. Free path for all! Exclusion of competition as Reuter and Havas practised and Wolff endured cannot be the affair of a great German news bureau. The German press, rich in energies and intelligences, does not fear competition; the qualities by which the German merchant won and held his place — industry, toughness, conscientiousness, adaptation to foreign conditions, and shortest delivery time — are not wanting to German men of the pen. Not the world, only our news service, do we wish to improve. The Weekend-Telegrams, useful to the merchant whose wires need not be bound to the day, are of no use whatever to the journalist who cannot put an earthquake on ice from Tuesday to Saturday night; of news transmission from abroad to Germany the whole programme says nothing. What remains of the society’s plans, Rotheit concludes sharply, is a great bribery aria played on a greased dementi-machine — money wasted, more likely to produce scandal suits than to promote German industrial prestige abroad.

Another path must be sought. The creation needed should best grow from the widely branched tree of the German press itself — inwardly non-partisan and independent, outwardly patriotically effective. A partly to-be-followed example is the mutual, cooperative news bureau of the Associated Press in the United States. From the AP’s own little guide (as of December 1914): a mutual society of persons representing newspapers, collecting and distributing important world news, about 900 members; leased wire network of roughly 22,000 English miles by day and 28,000 by night across the American continent; each member obliged to communicate neighbourhood news to the AP; yearly income mainly from members’ contributions over three million dollars; over 50,000 words daily received and passed on at the more important service points. Because the AP maintains own representatives in all world capitals, it has freed itself from other agencies though it still stands in relations with them for completeness. Yet the AP too has not been impartial — at least not until the war: serving English-language papers (though among 900 are 24 German), with foreign head office in London, it also swam in English waters (The Vital Issue, New York, 21 September 1914). As a collecting basin from which journalistic profit enterprises draw, the AP itself is freed from profit activity — no surpluses, no dividends; it does not sell news to papers outside its circle, hence not to foreign ones. In that point mainly the German news service would have to differ: it wants precisely to make its news accessible to all the world. Apart from that, the AP and the roughly similar United Press point the right goal. With famous German organisational ability it cannot be hard to form a similar combination of German papers with unrestricted possibility of joining and graded contributions. America has about 3,500 political newspapers; Germany about 4,000 news papers needing despatches, of which about 1,500 appear daily — and no one in Germany wants a service of AP’s 50,000-word daily bulk. A current among German publishers toward an AP-style combination exists, though nothing tangible has yet resulted; a new strong impulse would come from combining the wishes and material means of the newspaper world with those of banking and commercial circles.

Organisation, financing, and relation to Wolff and the Reich

PAGE 00000052–00000056 (printed pp. 46–50; lines ~2063–2225).

Reuter A.G. is said to work with 20 million marks and has the great finance house of the same name behind it; Havas A.G., whose chief shareholder is the Crédit Lyonnais, has about 8½ million francs capital; AP yearly contributions exceed three million dollars. Compare Wolff, existing since 1849 and a joint-stock company since 1874, still on its original share capital of one million marks yielding average dividends of more than 11 per cent. The “Auslandsdeutsche” thought he aimed high inviting 50–100 publishers at 500–1,000 marks yearly. Goschen’s figures on the industrial society’s financing do not quite agree; so far as known the government declared readiness for 250,000 marks yearly, the first government and first industrial 250,000 to form the basis for further running contributions — sums with which something could be done if rightly used, but as conceived the outlay would be a Danaid jar and would miss the goal Rotheit has in view.

A great German news bureau would require by estimate a founding and operating capital of five million marks. Bank and industrial circles ready to sacrifice large yearly contributions to their planned enterprise would fare far more cheaply and usefully with a once-for-all outlay as part of founding capital — an officium nobile toward the German people — the other part to be taken by publishers already tributary to Wolff and obliged in any case to sacrifice for any expansion. With the once-for-all industrial contribution their participation would be closed; the rest must be the affair of publishing and journalism. The only forum where an independent German news service would have to give account would be the yearly meetings of the Verein der deutschen Verleger and the Reichsverband der deutschen Presse. Strict public criticism is needed and desired, yet only the professional can with certainty find the fault-sources and judge whether the right man stands in the right place. Despite all professional competence of German journalism there is still a shortage of suitable foreign correspondents; the hitherto patchy system was unfavourable to training a rising generation, and the German press, with smaller means than the American and English and happily without unclean “side incomes” like the French, has been far less able to place its foreign correspondents favourably.

As the venturesome merchant, industrialist, shipowner, or technician went out on his own fist and created German overseas interests to which the government then lent dutiful support, so the same is to be expected of the German press, which never enjoyed a Medici’s goodness. It needs official protection and goodwill in overseas activity if it is to fulfil its important task; official binding, however, would be an evil. The watchword must therefore run: in close, trustful understanding with the government, but independent of it. Not only for moral reasons: a great national foreign service would forfeit its right to exist and rob itself of its most important sources if it set itself above the government’s respective viewpoints — yet pure culture of the offiziös hinders development. The Reich authorities would still have enough to do. Means hitherto available for journalistic foreign purposes were so tiny (some 300,000 marks yearly) that one best passes them in silence; after recent experiences the Reichstag will have to be more liberal. Corruptionist by-paths and Reptilienfonds need not be thought of — though cases exist where one and a half belong to a rogue — yet that does not mean viewing the whole world from the bribery standpoint. Support-needing and support-worthy German overseas papers exist enough; in some states papers in the local language can be drawn — as already partly begun during the war — by purchase or subsidies to represent German interests; everyone in Italy knows the papers in French pay, and no one takes offence — open cards can be played.

Shall Germany suddenly have two great news agencies, a new bureau beside Wolff fighting for the field? To ask is to answer: of course not. If Wolff could be taken over at a fair price into the ownership of the German press and built out according to general needs, the embarrassment would be removed. Wolff shares are in firm hands and unsaleable less for the high dividends than for political reasons; yet the view is not to be dismissed that Wolff and those places that politically play on the Wolff instrument would listen if the German press seriously set about opening a bureau of its own. Private foreign reporting by the great papers would not have the ground cut from under it by an adequate, clear, solid, formally compact collective service; room would remain for the taste-directions of different sheets, and it would be a true blessing if the good solid political essay from abroad, now almost daily slain by the flood of telephoneates, came again to its rights — the foreign correspondent today more and more a hurrying telephone machine. This plan is not so easy as it reads, yet it is well the only one that, approached with earnest effort, promises success without inadequate patchwork or downright harm.

Press, diplomacy, foreign journalists, and press-telegram fees

PAGE 00000056–00000063 (printed pp. 50–57; lines ~2226–2569).

The correspondent abroad is by no means dependent only on diplomacy, nor should he be — his field is far larger than fetching diplomatic news and guidelines — yet the closest possible touch with his country’s diplomatic or consular representation belongs to his most important duties. The unsatisfactory note prevails in writing on diplomacy and journalism. The Berliner Nationalzeitung (17 June 1914) on Count Brockdorff-Rantzau’s Copenhagen table-speech inviting an entente cordiale between diplomacy and press: German diplomacy still regards the press as a necessary evil, with which one best has as little as possible to do — while in France, England, America, not to speak of Denmark, journalists intervene actively in politics, hold political posts, even reach the presidency. Rotheit finds the voice somewhat too sharp — a change of views has been slowly announcing itself in the last decade — yet a feeling wall between diplomacy and journalists will always remain: the diplomat’s life-element is secrecy, the journalist’s publicity. A middle line must be found. The diplomat who fears receiving a journalist often fears himself more than the journalist — lacking ease outside his professional circle and the gift of quick human recognition. The journalist who understands his craft seldom embarrasses a diplomat with whom he stands in pleasant relations by unwanted publications; he seeks not storm against professional secrets but direction and goal. The more deeply the journalist is initiated into details, the more he feels his responsibility; and in many respects the journalist can render the diplomat good auxiliary service — often the diplomat is the receiving and the journalist the giving part. What the journalist abroad asks of the diplomat is, apart from occasional communications for newspaper purposes, advancement in social relation — leaning on the official representation of the homeland is a social necessity abroad. Fürst Bülow, as envoy, ambassador, and chancellor, always valued touch with the press and seldom had cause to regret it; a diplomat who plays “enemy of the press” proves by deliberate closing against a reality the narrowness of his horizon.

On foreign journalists in Germany: some abuse hospitality — Times correspondents in Germany and Austria have sinned much therein; some who lived years in Germany used knowledge gained here after war outbreak only to besmirch the Germans further (Daily Mail, Petersburg Telegraph Agency). Nevertheless, since Germany is not and does not wish to be surrounded by a Chinese wall, a tolerable relation with representatives of foreign papers is to be sought. What the “Auslandsdeutsche” says of treating the German press applies also to the foreign press represented here: the press is today a world power; treat it as such — politely and obligingly; avoid needless embitterment from above. In the reports of the Berliner Verein der ausländischen Presse one finds much that runs straight against that admonition; “needless irritation” is the right word — the outcry over so-called German militarism has its origin in good part here. Foreign journalists will return after peace; it will be advisable to relieve them as far as possible of the bitter feeling of being merely tolerated strangers — the sensitivity of the defeated is doubly great. Careful treatment is in place: honey is not needed, the sting is an evil. Wilfully false reports from Germany abroad are best met by hanging them lower without spite through reproduction in German papers. Periodic informal meetings of official gentlemen with Berlin-resident foreign correspondents, on the model of wartime consultations with the home press, might prevent misunderstandings and silence the eternal complaint that at official places in Berlin “one learns nothing.”

Press-telegram fees. Germany is by no means ahead of the world here. Other countries long recognised the importance of cheapening press telegrams and acted accordingly; fiscal narrowness and hidden aversion to the press hinder following that example. France concluded an agreement for half-price press telegrams with Belgium in autumn 1913 — so the whole Belgian press, so far as it was not already, became a shoot of the Paris press; similar arrangements with Great Britain, Austria, Holland, the United States; French fees to China and Japan reduced 20 per cent. Can one wonder that abroad one finds extensive telegraphic agency and private reporting on English and French and overseas events, while telegrams from Berlin occupy a tiny space and for days drop out altogether? The German press enjoys relief only in traffic with the United States (fifty per cent reduction) — granted not by the Reichspost but by the private company owning the German–American cable. Not a single telegraphing correspondent for the Orient is resident in Berlin, because Eastern papers cannot afford the excessive despatch costs. In the Reichstag (March 1912) Chefredakteur Dr. Oertel asked for cheapening of press telegrams; Staatssekretär Kraetke replied that reduced press telegrams exist only to overseas countries, inland fees are already very low, the English relief burdens their budget by about eight million marks yearly, and the developed telephone system plus new letter-telegrams already help the press. The Zeitungs-Verlag (5 April 1912) found prospects unfavourable and urged continual pressure. The Frankfurter Zeitung, commenting on the Reichstag debate, regrets the Secretary’s rejection: cheap press telegrams are a not-to-be-underestimated means of international understanding and enlightenment; England recently halved press rates to colonies, Canada, and the United States (and to Australia and New Zealand), while London–Germany remains at the high fee of 2 pence — so a telegram from London to Vienna through Germany costs less than one from London to Berlin! When the Foreign Secretary from time to time laments anti-German, English-spectacled attitudes in the United States or elsewhere and regrets the smallness of his funds, here is the point at which to begin: cheap press telegrams abroad would break the English monopoly in news transmission and shaping, offer the press of those countries the possibility of informing themselves more directly from Germany, and what is lost in revenue would be far outweighed by increased German influence abroad and soon made good by growing use of the telegraph.

Closing program

PAGE 00000064 (printed p. 58; lines ~2571–2581).

We are at the end. With the wishes and needs of the German press concerning a thorough improvement of the German news service coincides the interest of all political and economic factors of the German Reich. The German press demands of no one that he lift it into the saddle. It already sits therein, can also ride, and knows the way. What it needs is free field of activity inwardly as also outwardly. And that it must and will create for itself — over Reuter and Havas.
