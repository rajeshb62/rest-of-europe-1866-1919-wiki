# Ollivier — *L'Empire libéral*, Tome XI (Livre XIX)

Émile Ollivier, *L'Empire libéral; études, récits, souvenirs*, tome onzième. Paris: Garnier frères, 6, rue des Saints-Pères, **1907**. On the Internet Archive as [lempirelibral11olliuoft](https://archive.org/details/lempirelibral11olliuoft) (identifier spelling *libral* without the second *e*; University of Toronto / Robarts copy). The half-title band names the subject **La Veillée des armes**. The body opens **Livre XIX**.

The volume is Ollivier’s continuous memoir-history of the Second Empire in the two years that close the authoritarian system and prepare the liberal ministry and the war of 1870. Dominant years in the text are **1868–1869**. The narrative runs from the abolition of discretionary press control and Rochefort’s *Lanterne* through the Spanish revolution, the Baudin affair, Eastern and Balkan crises, the Greek conference, Beust’s triple-alliance soundings, parallel Austrian and Prussian rearmament, Niel’s military work with the Emperor, the Belgian railway scare, Ollivier’s book *Le 19 Janvier*, the elections of 1869, and the first moves of the Hohenzollern candidacy. A short block of *éclaircissements* follows the twenty-one chapters.

## Livre XIX — La Veillée des armes

### Chapitre premier — La Lanterne — La France nouvelle — Les irréconciliables — La politique radicale de Jules Simon — Prophéties de malheur

Abolition of the discretionary press regime emboldens existing party papers and breeds new hostile sheets; on 31 May 1868 Rochefort’s red pamphlet *la Lanterne* appears on the boulevards at ten centimes. Government handling of the pamphlet; causes of its success; Paul de Cassagnac as Rochefort’s adversary. Condemnation and Rochefort’s flight to Belgium; the hostile current continues — Cavaignac’s son refuses to be crowned by the Prince Imperial; election of Jules Grévy; failure of Dufaure; Prévost-Paradol’s *La France nouvelle* and its bellicose character. Creation of the *Irréconciliables*; Jules Simon’s *Politique radicale* supplies their programme; Ollivier’s conversation with Gambetta and Jules Ferry; the irréconciliables separate from the republicans of 1848. Death of Walewski (23 September 1868); Thiers’s judgment; prophecies of misfortune for the liberal Empire.

### Chapitre II — La révolution espagnole — Prim — Napoléon III — Bismarck

European anxiety over an expected Franco-Prussian war magnifies every word of the Emperor and of the King of Prussia. Portrait of Isabella II; causes of her unpopularity; Prim’s ambition and successive pronunciamientos under Narváez and O’Donnell; death of O’Donnell; González Bravo; arrest of Serrano and exile of Montpensier. Topete’s conspiracy; Prim and the fleet (September 1868); battle of Alcolea; flight of Isabella; Serrano at Madrid and Prim’s *¡Abajo los Borbones!* Search for a king — Ferdinand of Portugal refused; first indication of a Hohenzollern candidacy, which Prince Anthony rejects “because France would not tolerate it”; Olózaga ambassador at Paris. Napoleon III’s abstention: he will recognise any government born of popular election, even Montpensier, and prefers Alfonso as the reasonable solution. Role Europe attributes to Bismarck in the Spanish revolution; Bismarck’s conversation with Clarendon disclaiming any part; Napoleon’s reiterated warning that Prussia will not be allowed to cross the Main; Bismarck will later use Spain against that casus belli. Dissent of Bismarck with Heydt; the King sides with the finance minister.

### Chapitre III — L’affaire Baudin — Gambetta — Sa plaidoirie — La souscription — Refus d’Émile Ollivier — Les réunions publiques

Delescluze and Blanqui; their plan to move the bourgeoisie through the Baudin affair. Scene at the Montmartre cemetery (2 November 1868); opening of the subscription; adhesions of Odilon Barrot, Prévost-Paradol, Berryer and others. Prosecutions under the law of general security; portrait of Gambetta; his plea; Clément Laurier. Death of Berryer (8 November 1868). Newspaper trials and acquittals; revolutionary manifesto of 3 November; difference between the revolutionaries of 1848 and those of 1868. Ollivier’s situation after the session of 1868; preparation of *Le 19 Janvier*; letter to the Emperor and the Emperor’s reply (29 September 1868); summons to subscribe. Why Ollivier refuses: nothing justified a revolution against the Empire; dangers of attempting one. Letter of refusal and its consequences. Public meetings of 1868 — oppressive and anarchic character; government tactics.

### Chapitre IV — La Turquie — Aali, Fuad — Bourée

Europe’s October 1867 declaration leaving Turkey to herself proves a service. Fuad and Aali as reforming statesmen; obstacles in the Sultan, the harem, and Russian intrigue; the Tsar’s relative moderation. Bourée’s beneficial action at Constantinople; Cretan firman (29 February 1868); Christians enter administration; Council of State; collective demarche of the ambassadors; Gorchakov’s incredulity; tobacco-régie affair.

### Chapitre V — Bismarck et la Roumanie

Prince Charles of Romania as Prussia’s sentinel on the Danube; Prussian army organisation; German engineers; encouragement from King William and consul Kaysserling. Bratiano’s perturbing policy; French and Austrian protest; Prince Napoleon at Bucharest; Beust’s momentary counsel of Turkish menace withdrawn on Moustier’s representation. Fall of Bratiano (16 November 1868) on Bismarck’s order — Bismarck seeks Hungarian goodwill with a view to the war he is preparing against France.

### Chapitre VI — Fin de l’insurrection crétoise

Last efforts of the Cretan rising; Greek obstacles to repatriation of exiles; Petropavloski’s bands and corsair ships; Turkish resolutions and ambassadors’ demarche; Turkish ultimatum (14 December 1868); rupture of relations; capitulation of Petropavloski; general amnesty (23 December 1868). Gorchakov’s ambiguous line on the Principalities and his need of Bismarck’s diplomatic help over Greece.

### Chapitre VII — Ministère Gladstone–Clarendon — Ministère Forcade–La Valette — Rouher maître absolu du gouvernement

Disraeli’s Reform bill of 1867 and his fall; Gladstone and the Irish Church; Gladstone ministry with Clarendon at the Foreign Office (10 December 1868). In France: resignation of Pinard (16 December); La Valette to Foreign Affairs; Forcade de la Roquette to the Interior; Gressier to Public Works; *Moniteur universel* replaced by the *Journal officiel* from 1 January 1869; Rouher absolute master of the government.

### Chapitre VIII — La conférence sur la Grèce

Bismarck, to serve Gorchakov, proposes a conference on the Turco-Greek dispute (18 December 1868); France accepts; Clarendon hesitates then accepts provided Turkish territorial integrity is not questioned; Greece admitted only in a consultative capacity. Conference (9 January–18 February 1869); declaration against Greece, which accepts; Ollivier judges the work ephemeral for want of applying the principle of nationalities — Sweden as contrasting example. The Emperor’s taste for European conference-parliamentarism.

### Chapitre IX — Politique de Beust vis-à-vis de la Prusse, de l’Italie, de la France — Négociations pour un traité d’alliance franco-austro-italien — Dispositions de Bismarck

After Bismarck’s “two sweeps of the broom” in Romania and Greece, friction with Beust over historical revelations of 1866 (Usedom note). Beust does not want war — Austria is not ready, Hungary does not want it, and peace seems the surest bar to North German absorption of the South — yet seeks to bind France without binding himself. Difficult Italian situation of the Menabrea ministry; anger at Pius IX over the execution of two Garibaldians; failed attempts to end the French occupation of Rome. Through general Türr, Beust opens talks for a defensive triple alliance of France, Austria, and Italy. Bismarck’s quarrel with the King; offer and withdrawal of resignation (26 February 1869); apparent calm; *Post* article and Reichstag speech; Pourtalès’s warning that the calm is a deception and that war is being prepared.

### Chapitre X — Préparation autrichienne

Under war minister Kuhn, complete reorganisation of the Austrian army: three laws on the active army, landwehr, and landsturm (5 December 1868) — personal obligatory service without replacement, one-year volunteers, twelve-year total obligation, contingent of 93,000. Parallel Hungarian law; honvéds. Correction of the errors of 1866 and improvement of mobilisation. *(Body OCR reverses the roman numerals of Chapitres X and XI; titles and the table control.)*

### Chapitre XI — La préparation prussienne — Nouvelle tactique de l’artillerie et de la cavalerie — Arrangements avec les États du Sud — Plan de Moltke

Prussia after 1866 does not sleep on victory; Roon refines the instrument, Moltke its employment; the old King multiplies inspections and cabinet work. Application of the federal army law; eleven corps plus Saxony and the Guard; perfection of every service including mobilisation. Steel guns debate; Stoffel’s erroneous report; fortifications and Mainz. Instruction of the men; royal leave effectively shortening the three-year infantry term; Hindersin and artillery; initiative among officers. New cavalry and artillery battle tactics. Military accords with the South German states. Moltke’s plan and Ollivier’s judgment on it.

### Chapitre XII — L’Empereur et l’armée

A recruiting law supplies elements; organisation is the sovereign’s work. Napoleon III’s constant care for the soldier’s well-being; circular from Biarritz (1 August 1854); Vaillant. Restoration of the Guard. Impotent struggle of the Emperor’s reforming spirit with bureau routine under Randon; attempt to reform the 1791 manœuvre regulation; little profit drawn from the Italian war.

### Chapitre XIII — Niel et l’Empereur

Sadowa awakens Napoleon III; with Niel he attempts serious organic reform — restoration of cadres suppressed in 1863, abolition of elite companies, summer and winter contingents, instruction. The chassepot. Revelation of Prussian artillery superiority; mitrailleuses. Fortresses — Metz works and Blondeau’s provisioning. Defects of French mobilisation versus regional organisation; the Emperor wants permanent brigading, Niel opposes. Mobilisation of matériel, depot battalions, divergences between Emperor and Niel. Subsistence, train, central magazines. Remarkable letter of the Emperor to Niel (22 November 1867); regulation of 16 March 1869; Niel’s combat instructions. Offensive tradition versus Niel’s defensive counsel. Strategic plan — the Emperor studies but does not fix a definitive plan. Conclusion: Napoleon III shared equally with Niel in one of the greatest military efforts France had made; judgment on Stoffel’s reports.

### Chapitre XIV — Après l’œuvre de Niel — Étions-nous prêts?

The Emperor assures the country the army is ready (opening speech, 18 January 1869); Niel confirms in repeated declarations (March–April 1869). Ollivier’s judgment: the Emperor and Niel were right — the army was magnificent and was beaten only by the incapacity of its chiefs.

### Chapitre XV — L’hallucination des chemins de fer belges

Portrait of Frère-Orban and of La Guéronnière at Brussels. Cession of the Grande-Luxembourg line to the Compagnie de l’Est (8 December 1868); further Est–Guillaume-Luxembourg treaty; Belgian emotion and bill forbidding cession of railways to foreign companies without authorisation; reciprocal French emotion. Emperor’s letter to Niel (19 February 1869); military preparations. Clarendon’s intervention; French and Belgian declarations; Frère-Orban in Paris; Rouher wants to pose the casus belli; the Emperor, again pacific, calms him. Belgians wish to invoke the guaranteeing powers; Clarendon restrains them and warns France; La Valette receives the warning badly. General pacification.

### Chapitre XVI — Lamartine, Berlioz, Rossini

Last days and funeral of Lamartine (d. 28 February 1869); definition of music; characters and works of Rossini and Berlioz.

### Chapitre XVII — La responsabilité ministérielle au Sénat — L’administration de la ville de Paris — Haussmann et Rouher

Imperial speech as electoral programme: “I will not go back, but I will not go forward.” Ministerial responsibility in the Senate — Rouher and Maupas. Haussmann and the city of Paris in the Corps législatif — three networks, *bons de délégation*, Crédit foncier treaty (11 April 1867); Thiers, Picard, Rouher on the extraordinary budget; Haussmann defends himself in the Senate.

### Chapitre XVIII — La veillée des armes au Corps législatif — Les officiers du parquet — La loi militaire — La responsabilité ministérielle — Pension aux anciens militaires

Budget discussion as complete inventory of questions since 1863 ahead of the elections. Embarrassed ministers versus an opposition at ease. Official candidatures — Forcade, Ollivier. Thiers demonstrates the need to restore the constitutional regime; Rouher’s protest; Thiers’s double language on liberal concessions as progress or comedy; La Valette’s pacific declaration. Pension for former soldiers — Buffet, Pelletan, Glais-Bizoin.

### Chapitre XIX — Le 19 janvier

Publication of Ollivier’s *Le 19 Janvier* (3 March 1869); press charivari. Digressions on the *je* and the *moi* in history and on the abuse and use of quotations. Public success; letter of Sainte-Beuve. Diplomatic conversation between Rome and Paris over Mgr Darboy’s letter as reported in the book. Earlier imperial attempt, through Fleury and Prince Napoleon, to stop the publication; offer of neutrality in the Var and later the Ministry of Justice; Ollivier publishes anyway.

### Chapitre XX — Les élections de 1869 — Le programme de Belleville de Gambetta — Les destructions nécessaires de Jules Ferry — Bancel et Émile Ollivier à Paris — Séance du Châtelet

Character of 1869 elections differs from 1863 on both sides: government invents “officieux” candidates; official candidates themselves liberalise and criticise Mexico, Sadowa, and armaments; opposition intransigence and antimilitarism. Paris elections run by intransigent papers; Gambetta against Carnot; Jules Ferry against Guéroult; sentence of exclusion against Ollivier. Gambetta’s Belleville programme; Ferry’s “necessary destructions.” Ollivier’s candidacy at Lodève and polemic with *le Siècle*; candidacy in the Var against Laurier; Mistral; Bancel raised against Ollivier at Paris; challenge and Châtelet meeting; revolutionary agitation; strange police attitude; withdrawal of the two government competitors — Ollivier is not thereby an official candidate. Girardin’s offer of a hall; Bancel retained in the Drôme by illness; Robert Mitchell; attitude of opposition deputies; last-hour manœuvre. Ollivier’s failure at Paris and election in the Var. Departmental elections not revolutionary; failure of Prévost-Paradol and Renan; Gambetta beats Lesseps at Marseille; disorders at La Ricamarie and Paris. Meaning of the elections: condemnation of the authoritarian Empire represented by Rouher and victory of the programme of the liberal Empire associated with Ollivier.

### Chapitre XXI — Les négociations entre l’Autriche, l’Italie et la France sont suspendues — Lettres de Napoléon III, François-Joseph, Victor-Emmanuel — Commencements du complot Hohenzollern — La monarchie sans roi — Bismarck et le « fatal parlementarisme » — Conversation du roi de Prusse et de Nigra à propos de la ligne du Mein

Twelve-article draft treaty (March 1869); impossibility of agreement on Rome; negotiations suspended; letters exchanged among Napoleon III, Francis Joseph, and Victor Emmanuel. Cadiz insurrection (3–13 December 1868); monarchical elections to the constituent Cortes; Prim and Olózaga in search of a king; new refusal of Ferdinand of Portugal (3 April 1869); Serrano fails to carry Montpensier. Bismarck’s Spanish policy — counterbalance French influence and, if possible, install a German prince; he floats Leopold of Hohenzollern through the press; Benedetti’s demarches to Thile (31 March 1869) and to Bismarck (11 May 1869); imperial instructions. Bernhardi sent to Spain; candidacy introduced via Steyssal; Salazar y Mazaredo as first agent; Kanitz’s denial; royalty without a king — Serrano regent, Prim president of the council (1 June 1869). Bismarck’s struggle in the Reichstag; he curses the orators without condemning “fatal parliamentarism”; resigns the Prussian presidency of the council and takes leave (30 June 1869). King of Prussia at Baden; conversation with Nigra on the Rhine frontier; impression on Napoleon III.
