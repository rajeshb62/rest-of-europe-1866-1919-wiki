# Beksics — *A dualismus*

Gusztáv Beksics (1847–1906), *A dualismus. Története, közjogi értelme és nemzeti törekvéseink*. Budapest: Az Athenaeum R. Társulat Kiadása, 1892. A Hungarian constitutional-juridical treatise of some three hundred and forty pages, drawn from the scan preserved in the Harvard Law School Library (University of California/Google-digitized copy, oclc 14025910) via Anna's Archive; the raw text in this wiki was taken from the already-OCR'd page texts of that scan (Hungarian). Beksics, a Hungarian publicist, lawyer and politician, wrote to settle the public-law disputes that "incessantly agitate" opinion and parliament over the meaning of the Compromise — in particular the claim, then beginning to be taught in the schools, that between Hungary and Austria there is a real union, and that Hungary's statehood has been impaired by the dualist system.

Dominant years in the text are **1867–1868** (with the long constitutional history that produced the Compromise).

## Preface — Előszó

Public-law questions incessantly agitate public opinion and parliament; heated debates turn on the dualism, especially on the content and public-law meaning of Act XII of 1867. Is it true that the meaning of that act is uncertain, so that the nation's sovereign rights are in doubt? Beksics declares the book's method: the 1867 Act can only be correctly interpreted by examining what Hungary's statehood consisted in before and after the Pragmatic Sanction, in 1848, and what it consists in since 1867 — requiring the study of all the sources of Hungarian constitutional history and public law, above all the *Corpus Juris*, which few now consult.

## I. Király és nemzet — King and nation

The Hungarian nation was always one with its king. The nation was the source, the royal power the stream that flows from it. The Hungarian people existed before Hungarian kings; it completed its formation into a nation and a state by placing the crown on its leader's head. Hungarian kingship was a consequence, not an antecedent; in Hungary not only political power but every right had the nation itself for its source. The German doctrine of the state, which set up kingship as an independent, primordial source of right with sovereign powers over legislation and judicature, exerted a harmful reaction on Hungarian public-law science; the Hungarian kingship never possessed such powers.

## II. Magyarország államisága a pragmatica sanctio előtt — Hungary's statehood before the Pragmatic Sanction

The examination of what Hungary's statehood consisted in before the Pragmatic Sanction — the character of the medieval Hungarian state and the constitutional relations that preceded the Compromise.

## III. Magyarország államisága a pragmatica sanctio idejében — Hungary's statehood at the time of the Pragmatic Sanction

Hungary's statehood in the age of the Pragmatic Sanction (1723), by which the succession of the House of Habsburg and the inseparable and indivisible possession of the kingdoms was settled, and on which the whole subsequent relation of Hungary to Austria rested.

## IV. A magyar állam és a közös ügyek 1848-ban — The Hungarian state and the common affairs in 1848

The constitutional settlement of 1848, the establishment of the responsible ministry, and the manner in which the common affairs between Hungary and Austria were then ordered — the antecedents of the dualist arrangement.

## V. A provisorium — The provisorium

The period of the provisional arrangement and absolutist administration that followed the failure of 1849, and the condition of Hungary in the interval before the Compromise.

## VI. A dualismus keletkezése — The origin of the dualism

How the dualist system came into being — the negotiations and constitutional work that issued in the Compromise of 1867, associated above all with the name of Ferenc Deák.

## VII. A magyar államiság és a dualismus — Hungarian statehood and the dualism

The relation between Hungary's restored statehood and the dualist system; the sense in which the Compromise preserved, and did not impair, the statehood of the Hungarian state.

## VIII. Külügy és Magyarország nemzetközi egyénisége — Foreign affairs and Hungary's international personality

The conduct of foreign affairs under the dualist system and the standing of Hungary as an international personality — in what degree Hungary figures as a subject in the international order.

## IX. Hadügy és hadsereg — Defence and the army

The common military establishment and the army under the Compromise; the organisation of the joint armed forces of the two states.

## X. A honvédség és a rendkívüli véderő — The home defence and the extraordinary levy

The Hungarian honvéd (home defence) force and the extraordinary levy established alongside the common army.

## XI. Delegáczió — The Delegations

The Delegations, the joint parliamentary bodies by which the common affairs of the two states are managed, and their powers and proceedings.

## XII. A közös ügyek miniszteriuma 1848-ban és 1867-ben — The ministry of common affairs in 1848 and 1867

A comparison of the handling of the common affairs in 1848 and in 1867 — how the common ministries were constituted in each settlement.

## XIII. Nemzeti királyi udvartartás — The national royal household

The national royal household (*nemzeti kir. udvartartás*) of the Hungarian kingdom within the dualist arrangement.

## XIV. Magyarország pénzügyi és gazdasági függetlensége — Hungary's financial and economic independence

The financial and economic autonomy of the Hungarian state under the Compromise, and the division of fiscal and economic matters between the two states.

## XV. Personál-unió — Personal union

The character of the union as a personal union of the two crowns — the tie that binds Hungary and Austria being the identity of the ruler — and the consequences that flow from it.

## XVI. A dualismus belső vonatkozásai — The internal bearings of the dualism

The internal relations of the dualist system: (1) **Horvátország** — Croatia and the Hungarian-Croatian settlement (the nagodba of 1868); (2) **Nemzetiségek** — the nationalities within Hungary and their place under the dualist state.

## XVII. Mikép változtatható meg az 1867: XII. t.-czikk? — How can Act XII of 1867 be altered?

The question of how the Compromise act could be changed, and the position of a hypothetical third factor in the monarchy alongside Hungary and Austria.

## XVIII. Hibás közjogi elnevezések — Erroneous public-law designations

The correction of erroneous public-law terms and usages that distort the true character of the dualist relation.

## XIX. A dualismus és nemzeti törekvéseink — The dualism and our national aspirations

The relation of the dualist system to Hungarian national aims: (1) whether the dualism can be developed, and (2) the Hungarian national state — how national aspirations are to be pursued within the framework of the Compromise.

## XX. A dualismus jövője — The future of the dualism

Since the Compromise the internal relations of Austria have passed through significant transformations; the German element, which had seized constitutional power, lost its leading role through its own fault, and the federalist tendency won a partial triumph. Beksics does not pronounce on the chances of Austrian politics, still less preach intervention in it; yet conditions might arise in Austria incompatible with the Hungarian national interest that would move Hungarian statesmen to interpose. His closing argument: such is the grand conception of the Compromise of 1867 — years and decades may pass, new relations may arise out of events at home and abroad, but they cannot destroy Deák's work, not because it is intellectually perfect but because it answers the interests of both Hungary and Austria; not because it is a new creation but because it gave form to a centuries-long historical development. The dualism existed before 1848; the common affairs and their (though unwritten) management existed; and through all the centuries and great changes — Hungary freed from the Turkish yoke, triumphant over the attempts to absorb it, its two great wars of liberty — only one thing proved firm and immovable: the tie that binds Hungary to Austria through the dynasty, and its consequence, the union of the two mutually independent states for mutual defence and for that alone. As long as the ruler's person is common to Hungary and Austria this personal union and its consequences will stand; only in the single case of the cessation of the identity of the dynasty would new arrangements follow — an eventuality entirely outside the range of political possibility. The great creation on which Deák's name is written, the historical dualism which the storms of centuries could not destroy, need not be feared from the weak breezes of internal quarrels.

## Series note

This volume sits at the constitutional foundation of the 1866–1919 period covered by this wiki: the Compromise of 1867 created the Dual Monarchy, the political frame within which the era's Austrian and Hungarian history unfolded. Written by a Hungarian participant in the public-law debates of the time, it is a contemporary Hungarian account (rather than a retrospective) of the settlement, and stands beside the German and other works of the period as the Austrian–Hungarian constitutional thread. It is chronologically prior to the works of Theodor von Sosnosky on the politics of the Habsburg realms in the same wiki.
