Hanotaux — Histoire de la France contemporaine, Tome II (La Présidence du Maréchal de Mac Mahon — L’Échec de la Monarchie)

Gabriel Hanotaux, Histoire de la France contemporaine (1871–1900), tome deuxième. Paris: Ancienne Librairie Furne, Combet & Cie, Éditeurs, 5, rue Palatine (VIᵉ); no imprint year on the scanned copy (Internet Archive metadata: n.d.; publisher field Paris, Combet); title page of this issue deuxième édition. On the Internet Archive as histoiredelafran02hanouoft (University of Ottawa / Robarts–University of Toronto copy; ABBYY FineReader OCR; 740 images). The half-title names the subject La Présidence du Maréchal de Mac Mahon with the band L’Échec de la Monarchie. Series framing (author’s Avertissement and IA description): I Le gouvernement de M. Thiers; II–III La présidence du maréchal de Mac Mahon (two volumes — this tome L’échec de la monarchie, the next La Constitution de 1875); IV La République parlementaire. This wiki holds Tome I, Tome II, Tome III, and Tome IV.

In the Avertissement Hanotaux explains that the presidency of Marshal Mac-Mahon required two volumes. The present tome contains the history of the two first ministries of the duc de Broglie (May 1873–May 1874) and the exposition of the double failure of the monarchical restoration attempts. The comte de Chambord is set aside after his letter of 27 October; the Septennat is organised as a provisional regime; soon afterward the National Assembly, by overthrowing Broglie at the moment when he lays a full constitutional system before it, ruins in advance the hopes of the Orléans family. “The monarchical past of France is liquidated. There is a clear place for the Republic.” Despite the Assembly’s initial dispositions, the Republic will be voted by a majority of one voice on 30 January (the matter of tome III). Chapters X–XIII form a second part: analysis of the country’s state of mind after the war and the Commune, material recovery, and intellectual activity — a retrospective national psychology spanning roughly 1871–1880. Tome III is reserved for the vote of the Constitution and the attempt of 16 May. Acknowledgments include Mme Taine (unpublished Taine correspondence), Haussonville, Costa de Beauregard, Vogüé, the duc de Magenta and the marquise de Mac Mahon (archives), vicomte Emmanuel d’Harcourt, general de Vaulgrenant, confidential material on Gambetta, and general Cuny on military questions. Printer at the close: Bordeaux — Impr. G. Gounouilhou, rue Guiraude, 9-11. Dominant years in the narrative half are 1873–1874; the recovery chapters dense through 1871–1880.

Avertissement

Two tomes needed for Mac-Mahon’s presidency; scope of the present volume (Broglie I–II, double monarchical failure, Septennat, fall of Broglie); monarchical past liquidated and place cleared for the Republic; chapters X–XIII as national psychology and material/intellectual recovery, c. 1871–1880; tome III promised for the Constitution and 16 May, completing a first whole — the history of the National Assembly and the establishment of the Third Republic; thanks for unpublished documents and interlocutors.

Chapitre premier — La politique du 24 Mai

Emotion of 24 May 1873 in Paris and the provinces as news of Thiers’s defeat, resignation, and Mac-Mahon’s election spreads from Versailles; foreign alarm calmed by the Marshal’s name. Portrait and military career of Mac-Mahon; the new president and politics; character. The cabinet of 25 May; presidential message; circular to diplomatic agents; the constitutional question; divine right and popular sovereignty. Portrait of the duc de Broglie — character and doctrines; parties in the Assembly; democratic ideas. Portrait of Léon Gambetta — origins and intellectual formation; under the Empire and during the war; deputy to the National Assembly; Gambetta the orator.

Chapitre II — L’« ordre moral »

Resumption of the National Assembly session after 24 May; Bismarck incident; the Pascal circular; Beulé; first acts of the cabinet; the formula of « ordre moral ». Interpellation Le Royer; the religious question; pilgrimages; the cult of the Sacré-Cœur; the votive church of Montmartre. Visit of the shah of Persia to Paris and the fêtes in his honour; first steps of the monarchical campaign; adjournment of examination of the constitutional laws. Measures against republican propaganda; special powers for the commission of permanence; Gambetta and the Grenoble speech; the centre left pronounces for dissolution. Summer reorganisation laws, including the general army law of 27 July 1873; prorogation from 29 July to 5 November; message of Mac-Mahon and manifesto of the Left groups. Liberation of the territory: last incidents of the German occupation; payment of the balance of the five-milliard indemnity; evacuation completed 16 September.

Chapitre III — La campagne monarchique

Hopes of the monarchical parties after 24 May; always the « fusion »; the comte de Paris at Frohsdorf; reconciliation of the two branches of the house of Bourbon; divergences between Orléanists and legitimists. The country and the monarchical campaign; meetings of the commission of permanence (25 August; 25 September); question of the duc d’Audiffret-Pasquier on the possibility of restoration; missions Merveilleux du Vignaux and de Sugny, then Combler, to Frohsdorf; note of Chambord to Ernoul and letter to de Rodez-Bénavent; decision for a meeting of the bureaux of the four Right groups on 4 October. Hanotaux frames the episode as a high cause ruined in verbal dissension — a tragicomedy of representative illusion disposing of France without consulting her.

Chapitre IV — L’entrevue de Salzbourg

Meeting of 4 October; constitution of the commission des Neuf; dinner of the quai d’Orsay; first session of the Nine; the army and the tricolour flag — « Les chassepots partiraient tout seuls »; Charles Chesnelong delegated by the Nine to the comte de Chambord. Parties and monarchical restoration; elections of 12 October; the Left organises resistance. Chesnelong at Salzburg: four interviews with Chambord; the verbal declarations of Salzburg on the constitution and the flag.

Chapitre V — La lettre du 27 octobre

Chesnelong’s return (16 October); meetings of the Nine and of the group bureaux; he reports and the campaign is to go forward; opinion. Government and restoration; preparations for the king’s return; incredulity of the country; the Lefts organise resistance; the army. Plenary meetings of the Right groups; procès-verbal of the centre droit; centre gauche refuses its concurrence; manifesto of the appel au peuple group; anxiety at Chambord’s prolonged silence; the letter of 27 October; whether Chambord meant to reign. Last meeting of the Nine; monarchical campaign abandoned; the council of ministers pronounces for prorogation of the Marshal’s powers; opinion and the parties. (The comte de Paris’s unpublished letter of 15 October already sketches the “line of retreat” toward the Marshal.)

Chapitre VI — Le Septennat

After the failure of parliamentary restoration, Chambord still contemplates a direct adventure; contact with the royalist chiefs in the Assembly is broken; they cling to prorogation prepared of long hand. Plenary meeting of the Right groups; attitude of the Orléans princes; the extreme right; prorogation to be proposed by the Rights. Resumption of the parliamentary session; Broglie proposes the septennat; presidential message; Changarnier proposition; Chambord at Versailles; Blacas with the Marshal — Mac-Mahon refuses to receive Chambord. Commission report on Changarnier; debate on prorogation; further presidential message; vote of the septennat (20 November 1873). Consequences; embarrassment of the extreme right; Chambord leaves Versailles. Hanotaux: the expectant policy once more profits the Republic.

Chapitre VII — Le deuxième cabinet de Broglie

What is the septennat? — “personal” or “impersonal”; Broglie’s circular of 25 November (firm, moderate policy; neither republic nor monarchy named). Interpellation on non-convocation of electoral colleges; pure-and-simple order of the day; resignation of the cabinet; duc Decazes; constitution of the second Broglie cabinet; its precarious situation; election of the commission des Trente. Budget of 1874; Magne’s financial system; new taxes. Trial and condemnation of Marshal Bazaine; character of the judgment; whether Bazaine “betrayed.”

Chapitre VIII — La paix armée et le kulturkampf international

Years 1873–1875 as liquidation and gestation: interior politics of the masses succeeding that of the classes; exterior politics of space soon to succeed that of nationalities. The new German Empire and Europe; Bismarck and French interior policy; the system of « paix armée »; the Kulturkampf and Germanic unity; the “white policy” and Germany. Guillaume at Saint-Petersburg; the Tsar at Vienna; Victor-Emmanuel at Vienna and Berlin; whether a quadruple entente; Germany and the monarchical campaign; Guillaume I at Vienna. Decazes at Foreign Affairs; Roman affairs and the international Kulturkampf; incident of the episcopal mandements; war fears; German military septennat; Reichstag elections in Alsace-Lorraine and protest against annexation. Fresh apprehensions from German armaments; Spanish affairs; the Austrian emperor at Saint-Petersburg; Europe and armed peace. Advent of the Disraeli cabinet; change in British policy; the Tsar’s European journey; Germany and the Eastern Question; Hohenlohe named ambassador at Paris; European situation in May 1874. First movements of “world policy”: Russia in Central Asia; Annam and Tonkin; China; Ashanti war; Great Britain and the Suez Canal; great international works.

Chapitre IX — La chute du duc de Broglie

Last struggle of royalty and the privileged classes as the old France expires; Broglie, after Chambord’s failure, tries to consolidate around the septennat a “government of the middle classes” and fails under a blow from the traditional monarchy’s faithful. Resumption of the session; the majority of 24 May disaggregates; law of the mayors and its application; the Marshal explains the duration of his powers; resistances; elections of 27 February and 1 March 1874; Bonapartist manifestations on the majority of the Prince Imperial. The republican party; Gambetta–Lepère interpellation; Challemel-Lacour; Broglie declares the septennat « incommutable »; rupture with the extreme right; Thiers; dissolution proposals; Easter recess; elections of 29 March 1874. Reorganisation laws; liquidation account; navigable ways; military administration; frontier problem — two fortified lines of defence in the East; fortifications of Paris. Summer session; Broglie’s constitutional projects — representation of “interests,” universal suffrage “expurgated,” organisation of legislative and executive power, municipal and political electorate bills, project of a second Chamber; fall of the duc de Broglie — causes and consequences (16 May 1874).

Chapitre X — Le relèvement, l’avènement de la Démocratie

Opening of the volume’s second part: the country “constitutes itself” while a neutral cabinet d’affaires holds office and the head of state has neutralised himself; the Republic will be founded by one vote in a hostile Assembly under national pressure (forward glance). Optimism of the French people after 1870; the soil of France; patriotism; national unity. Prosperous years — climate, production, harvests, industry, commerce, abundance, wages, public fortune; rapid material recovery. Population; classes of French society; bourgeoisie; the people; the « nouvelles couches »; advent of Democracy.

Chapitre XI — Les Lettres. — L’Opinion. — La Presse

Intellectual harvest after the war as a surprise to foreign opinion that had written France off. Surviving influences: Auguste Comte, Balzac, Victor Hugo, Michelet, George Sand. Realism; trouble and disenchantment after the war; Renan, Taine, Flaubert; the theatre — Dumas fils, Sardou, Bornier. The novel and naturalism — Zola, Daudet; the poets and the Parnasse contemporain — Leconte de Lisle, Sully Prudhomme, Coppée, Heredia. Literature of teaching and divulgation; foundation of the École des Sciences politiques; democratic literature; the livraison; periodicals and illustrated papers. Opinion and the press; newspaper regime; great political journals of the parties; popular press and the one-sou paper; the provincial press.

Chapitre XII — Les Arts. Les Sciences

Art as the most brilliant manifestation of humanity’s desire for survival and as first selection between the ephemeral and the durable. French art after the war; art of the cities; architecture, sculpture, painting. Music — influence of Wagner; the French school. Science — France’s scientific work; the high sciences and the principle of “Unity”; astronomy, mathematics, mechanics, physics, chemistry; organic chemistry (Berthelot); physiology (Claude Bernard); natural history; anthropology and palaeontology; the problem of life; Pasteur and microbiology; cosmic forces; Darwinism and evolutionism; medicine and hygiene.

Chapitre XIII — La crise morale

After material resources and intellectual production, the intimate holding of the social body in the crisis that purged the last consequences of the war. Moral law and society; the three risks — religious, economic, patriotic; authority and liberty; religion; interior and exterior crisis of Catholicism; Catholic France; symptoms of disaffection. Free thought; philosophical systems; philosophical opportunism; systems of morals. Economism; Saint-Simonism; the “orthodox school”; economic morals; inconveniences and benefits of economism. Morals without sanction; Descartes’s “generous man”; non-constraint; the religion of the fatherland; theory of Opportunism. Close: with Broglie gone and monarchical chances and middle-class influence spent, constitutional debates open that will lead by fatal and logical sequence to the vote of the Republic; the coming Constitution (Hanotaux here calls it of 1875/1876) as a work of equilibrium and transaction — Pascal: “La multitude qui ne se réduit à l’unité est confusion; l’unité qui ne dépend pas de la multitude est tyrannie.”

Errata

A short Errata du premier volume closes the book (corrections to tome I: Franck; Tachard; a date; Hugo title; Merveilleux du Vignaux; Janvier de la Motte). Printer line: Bordeaux — Impr. G. Gounouilhou.

Series note

Tome II ends with the fall of the first Broglie cabinet (16 May 1874) and the national-psychology second part that prepares the constitutional vote. Continuity — Cissey–Fourtou, the Constitution of 1875, Buffet, elections of 1876, Dufaure and Jules Simon, letter of 16 May 1877 — is the matter of Tome III (La Présidence du Maréchal de Mac Mahon — La Constitution de 1875). The government of Thiers through 24 May 1873 is Tome I; the parliamentary Republic from Seize Mai through Gambetta’s death is Tome IV.
