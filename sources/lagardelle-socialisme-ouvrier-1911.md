# Lagardelle — *Le socialisme ouvrier*

Hubert Lagardelle (1875–1958), *Le socialisme ouvrier*. Paris: V. Giard & E. Brière, libraires-éditeurs, 16 rue Soufflot et 12 rue Toullier, 1911. Volume IX of the *Collection des Doctrines politiques* published under the direction of A. Mater (in-18, 4 fr. 50). The raw text in this wiki was taken from the Ohio State University scan (call no. HX266 L17) via Anna's Archive; the scan carries a text layer of 464 page-files, here reassembled in numerical order. Lagardelle, the theorist of revolutionary syndicalism and collaborator of the *Mouvement socialiste*, warns in his Préface that this is not a literary work: the volume brings together fragments written "au jour le jour, sous le coup des événements," following in anxiety the transformations of contemporary socialism. Many parts have aged; his conceptions modified themselves as they underwent the test of life. He means only to mark the stages of a thought.

Dominant years in the text are **1898–1908** (the book published 1911).

## Préface

Lagardelle does not conceal the faults of the book: any gathering of articles presents discontinuities, repetitions, disproportions that make reading difficult. But he has not wished to make a literary work; he has simply meant to mark the stages of a thought that followed, in anxiety, the transformations of contemporary socialism. The volume has a graver vice: its fragments were written day by day, under the pressure of events; many parts have grown old, others remain uncertain; there are affirmations he would no longer present today in that absolute manner which circumstances sometimes demand but which always betrays the relative and changing reality. His conceptions modified themselves as they underwent the test of life. He feels the imprudence of gathering old pages whose tone and form hold only for the moment in which they were written.

## Première partie — Le socialisme et la démocratie

### I — Socialisme ou Démocratie ? (1902)

Socialism plays a decisive part. Will it be absorbed by democracy, or, while using democracy for its ends, will it remain itself? The sections trace: the democratic confusion; the programme of the Congress of Tours (1902); the conditions of a minimum programme; the characters of the Tours programme; its declaration of principles; democracy and class struggle; and democratic politics and economic organisation.

### II — La démocratie sociale et le Parti socialiste

Millerand and social democracy — his *Plateforme électorale*, February 1898. The Dreyfus affair, the amnesty, and the socialists; the Radical-Socialists and the programme; the work of Waldeck-Rousseau — the ministry of Waldeck-Rousseau-Millerand-Galliffet; ministerialism and socialism; the school and the proletariat; from individualist democracy to social democracy; and social democracy and social Catholicism (Saint-Mandé, the "paix sociale," Dreyfusism in power, triumphant democracy).

### III — La fonction du Parti socialiste

Action of the party and action of the class; corporatism, syndicalism, politics; socialist unity; electoral revolutionaryism; eclectic socialism.

## Deuxième partie — Syndicalisme et socialisme

### I — La formation du syndicalisme

The syndical congress of Bourges (1904), November 1904 — the congresses of Amsterdam and Bourges of 1904 have, for the social observer, an unequal import; the evolution of the collective consciousness of social groups, which are a formation of history, offers an interest of another kind. The later sections treat the formation of syndicalism more broadly.

### II — Les idées syndicalistes

"Révisionnisme de gauche" (November 1904): there is unanimous agreement that the socialist crisis is due to the divorce of theory and practice. The sections follow on the ideas of syndicalism — anti-statism, revolutionary evolution, the syndicate and the class struggle, direct action, the general strike, the socialist party and the public services.

### III — La politique socialiste et le syndicalisme

The general policy of the socialist party — an intervention at the socialist Congress held at Toulouse, 15–20 October 1908, from the stenographic report. The syndicalist experience; the syndicate and the class struggle; direct action; the general strike; the socialist party and the public services. Then the syndicalist policy and the peasants — industrial and agricultural milieux, the small rural proprietors, and the policy of socialism toward the peasants. The closing argument addresses the agrarian question: let the socialist party present itself before the non-working popular masses with a programme of reduction of the State and of financial morality, and it will penetrate the rural milieux; if instead it comes with dogmatic programmes and abstract formulas, it will not touch them; if it offers them a conservative programme, it will not seize them either. Carried by the idealism that issues from the syndicalist working masses, it must appear as the only party of political cleanliness and of anti-statist action, making its own the essential claims and fundamental ideas of syndicalism.
