Delord — Histoire illustrée du second empire, Tome VI

Taxile Delord (1815–1877) — Histoire illustrée du second empire. — Tome sixième et dernier. — Nouvelle édition. — Paris: Librairie Félix Alcan (successeur de Germer Baillière et Cie), 108, boulevard Saint-Germain, 1883 (tous droits réservés). 56 gravures dans le texte, têtes de chapitre et culs-de-lampe.

Republican journalist and deputy (Siècle), Delord writes a contemporary opposition history of the Second Empire as a continuous narrative, not memoir. This is the illustrated edition (Baillière/Alcan [1880–83]) of the same multi-tome work originally published 1869–75. The present volume is the sixth and last of the set. It opens with the 1868–1869 press regime (the law of 11 May 1868, the wave of prosecutions, Henri de Rochefort’s La Lanterne) and carries the narrative through the murder of Victor Noir, the trial of Prince Pierre Bonaparte, the plebiscite of May 1870, the Hohenzollern candidacy and the diplomatic crisis, the declaration of war against Prussia, the collapse of the French armies (Wörth, Forbach, Rezonville, Saint-Privat, Sedan), the fall of the Ollivier ministry, the 4 September revolution and the fall of the Empire itself, and closes with the formation of the Government of National Defence under General Trochu.

Core chronological span is 1868–1870 — the Liberal Empire’s final convulsion, the Franco-Prussian War as the regime’s terminal crisis, the complete military collapse, and the transition from imperial to republican rule.

Chapitre I — La Presse

Impossibility of creating new journals. Ollivier, Veuillot, Chassin apply in vain. Veuillot authorised to found L’Univers. Suppression of the prior-authorisation regime (law of 11 May 1868). End of the administrative regime. Inconvenients and dangers for the government of the old press law. The orleanist, legitimist, and bonapartist press. Journalists under the Second Empire. The administrative regime does not arrest the Empire’s decadence. L’Électeur libre. The new press law in practice: prosecutions and fines. Trial of the Marseillaise staff; Rochefort appears. Third warning to the Courrier du dimanche. The Temps, the Journal des Débats, provincial papers. Girardin’s path. Delescluze, Rochefort, Victor Hugo. The press congresses and the irreconcilable Left. Struggle between bonapartist papers. Prévost-Paradol appointed ambassador. The new law transforms the political landscape.

Chapitre II — Meurtre de Victor Noir

M. Haussmann relieved of his functions. The duel between Prince Pierre Bonaparte and Victor Noir. Henri Rochefort’s article; Pierre Bonaparte’s letter to the Marseillaise. The meeting on 10 January 1870 at the prince’s house. The murder at Auteuil. Death of Victor Noir. Arrest of Prince Pierre. Funeral of Victor Noir — 12 January 1870. The Marseillaise of 11 January. Attitude threatening public opinion. Precautions taken by the government; the great popular procession. The journée of 12 January; precautions. Execution of Tropmann. Condemnation of Rochefort. Protest of the Marseillaise editors. Formation of the Ligue de l’Ordre. The Mégy pistol shot. Trial of Prince Pierre Bonaparte — before the Haute Cour; audience and debates. Paschal Grousset’s incident. Deposition of the doctors. Speeches of Floquet and Laurier. Troubled scenes at the Faculty of Medicine. Acquittal of Prince Pierre. Arrest of Millière and Protot.

Chapitre III — Le Plébiscite

Inquietude caused to Ollivier by the Right. Question of J. Simon on the privy council. Guyot-Montpayroux’s proposal. Rochefort’s interpellation; Rochefort called to order. Demand for authorisation of prosecution against Rochefort. Esquiros interpellates on the Creuzot strike. Gambetta’s discourse. Thiers attacks the commercial treaty; the denunciation is rejected. The merchant marine. Keratry’s question on the arrest of Rochefort. The senatus-consult on the constituent power. Discourses of Grévy, Ernest Picard, Gambetta, and Jules Simon. Prorogation of the Chamber. Exterior situation of the Empire since the Crimean War; the Italian war and its consequences. Alliance of Prussia and Russia. Napoleon III lets Prussia march on Vienna. Napoleon III wants war but is not ready. Interior situation of the Empire — essays of transformation. The plebiscite of May 1870: government proclamations, manifesto to the army, attitude of the clergy, energetic intervention of the magistrature. Letter of Ernest Picard to Grévy. Rupture within the Left. The Blois trial. Results of the plebiscite.

Chapitre IV — La Question Hohenzollern

Confidence in the maintenance of peace. Gramont replaces Daru. The prince Leopold of Hohenzollern — his candidacy for the Spanish throne. The affair revealed. Benedetti’s conversations with Bismarck. The Benedetti-Bismarck draft. Session of the Corps législatif; imprudent discourse by Ollivier. Petition of the Orleans princes. Cochery’s interpellation. Declaration read by Gramont. Picard demands communication of the papers. Gambetta’s opinion. Bellicose disposition of the Senate. Observations of the English government. Withdrawal of Prince Hohenzollern. Ollivier announces the withdrawal to the Chamber. The withdrawal ill-received by the Bonapartists. Gramont and Werther. The last telegram from Gramont to Benedetti.

Chapitre V — La Déclaration de Guerre

Napoleon III’s demand for a guarantee from Prussia; Benedetti and King Wilhelm at Bad Ems; the Ems dispatch — Bismarck’s version. Rupture. The Corps législatif votes the declaration of war against Prussia. Gambetta and Ollivier. Thiers’s discourse. The Left demands communication of the dispatches. The “cœur léger” of Ollivier. The commission deceived by the ministry. Talhouët’s report. Gambetta’s discourse. War voted. The Emperor wants to undo what Sadowa has done. Closure of the session. The Corps législatif at the Tuileries. The Constitutionnel article. Diverse manifestations for and against the war. The Sénat at Saint-Cloud. Rouher’s discourse. Troop departures. The Emperor’s letter to the National Guard. The Regency declared. Proclamation of the Emperor. False politics of Gramont. The press campaign. The Emperor does not dare to transport Paris. Police favours the brawlers.

Chapitre VI — Paris en État de Siège

The declaration of war and Austria. Attente of Paris. The Marseillaise. Reply of the Emperor. First bulletin of the campaign. The surprise of Wissembourg. Crowd devastates the money-changers’ shops. False dispatch announcing a victory. Enthusiasm short-lived. Proclamation of the ministry. First news of the lost battle of Mac-Mahon. Mac-Mahon’s retreat. Proclamation of the Empress-Regent. State of the army; the Emperor at Metz. Numerical superiority of the Prussian army.

Chapitre VII — Forbach et Rezonville

The combat on the heights of Spicheren. Defeat of Frossard. Forbach. The imperial headquarters enters the camp of Châlons. Resignation of Marshal Le Boeuf. Strength of the two armies after Wörth; Mac-Mahon retreats on Châlons. March of the Prussians after Forbach and Wörth. Bazaine’s marching delays — he allows the enemy to cross the Moselle. The battle of Borny. The retreat ordered. Mac-Mahon’s march toward Metz. The army shut in. The battle of Rezonville (Vionville-Flavigny). Our right finally disengaged. The French remain masters of the battlefield. Bazaine does not decide to march forward. The attack of the IXth Prussian corps; Canrobert under fire of 200 cannon. The attack on Point-du-Jour repulsed. The French hold their positions. Canrobert asks for reinforcements. Battle seems won when the right is turned — the Prussians masters of Saint-Privat. Resistance of the IInd and IIIrd corps. Night ends the battle. The French army reunited around Metz. Enormous Prussian losses.

Chapitre VIII — Reichshoffen

Slow and scattered formation of the Ist corps of the Army of the Rhine. Mac-Mahon takes command — dispersion of his forces. Wissembourg to be occupied. The enemy crosses the Rhine. Hasardous position of the general commanding at Wörth. Mac-Mahon unsupported. The battle is lost. Mac-Mahon directs himself on Saverne. Hesitations. The 7th corps stops at Belfort. Mac-Mahon receives the order to go to Châlons. Arrival of the Emperor at Châlons. Palikao opposes the march of the Army of Châlons on Paris. Situation of Bazaine’s army. Trochu named governor of Paris. Want of provisions from the first stage. Mac-Mahon resumes his march toward Metz. The retreat is ordered. State of the two armies. First dispatch of Mac-Mahon. Second dispatch and Bazaine’s response. Sortie interrupted on 26 August. Council of war at the ferme de Grimont. The army cannot break through. Movement forward of 31 August. Ammunition uselessly spent. Nouilly and Noisseville taken by the French. The passage was not impossible. Discontent of the army. Order given to cross the Meuse.

Chapitre IX — Sedan

Failly surprised at Beaumont; rout of the 5th corps. Encombrement on the road to Sedan. The Emperor at Mouzon. Absence of orders from the commander-in-chief — he neglects to blow the Mouzon bridge. Arrival of the Germans at Bazeilles. The Bavarians occupy the railway bridge left intact. The enemy gives the signal for battle. The marine infantry receives the Bavarian shock at Bazeilles. Mac-Mahon’s wound. General Ducrot takes command and prepares to force a path on Mézières. General de Wimpffen claims command and stops the retreat — he wants to attempt a breakout on Carignan. Brief conversation with the Emperor. Burning of Bazeilles. The enemy gains ground on the right wing. Artillery combat on the left. The circle of German armies closes. Prussian artillery makes the struggle impossible. The battle is lost. Last efforts of the French army. The Emperor summons the corps commanders; negotiation decided. The Emperor orders the white flag raised on the citadel. Sortie from Balan. Wimpffen’s resignation, not accepted. Wimpffen arrives at Prussian headquarters. Harshness of the capitulation. Bismarck refuses better conditions. A delay granted. The Emperor goes to Prussian headquarters. Interview of the Emperor with Bismarck. Wimpffen exposes the state of negotiations.

Chapitre X — Chute du Ministère Ollivier

Convocation of the Corps législatif for 9 August. Preparatory meeting of 7 August. Trochu’s peculiar situation. The Empress opposes Trochu’s appointment. Precautions for the opening of the chambers. Ollivier poses the question of confidence. Jules Favre’s proposition. Fall of the Ollivier ministry. The Palikao ministry. Organisation of national defence. Bazaine commander-in-chief. Trochu’s proclamation. “The Empire first, France after.” Fears of religious war. News of the bombardment of Strasbourg. Attempts to create an anonymous government. Progressive weakening of the Empire. Trochu’s reception. Appeal to the provincial firemen for Paris. Thiers refuses power. Attitude of the Left. Jules Favre’s interpellation. Gambetta addresses a gathering. Council of ministers at Schneider’s house. Nocturnal gatherings. The morning of 4 September.

Chapitre XI — La Chute de l’Empire

Inaction of the government. The session of 4 September — the president’s communication. The Corps législatif does not accept power. Invasion of the Corps législatif. Arrival of Jules Favre; Favre and Gambetta at the tribune. The president withdraws. Scenes of disorder. Gambetta obliged to pronounce the fall. Meeting at Schneider’s house. The last Senate session. Could the Revolution have been prevented? Patriotic language of Thiers. The morning of 4 September at the Tuileries. Arrival of the Tiers-parti delegation. Grief of the Empress. Last dispatch from the prefecture of police. Arrival of the minister of interior. Farewell and flight of the Empress. She takes refuge at her dentist’s; departs for Deauville and embarks for Brighton. Parallel between Lamartine and Trochu. The Hôtel de Ville invaded. Arrival of Gambetta. Necessity of naming a mayor of Paris. Rochefort carried in triumph. The government to consist of Parisian deputies. The people acclaim the new government. Delegation of the Internationale sent to the Hôtel de Ville. The struggle with the Commune begins. Chute misérable de l’Empire.*

Chapitre XII — La Défense Nationale

The Government of National Defence installed. General Trochu at the Tuileries. Organisation of the defence of Paris. The army of Metz still enclosed. Proclamation to the nation. The first steps of the new government. Palikao replaced. Provisional mayors. The delegation to Tours. Gambetta’s escape from Paris by balloon (7 October). The provincial resistance: Army of the Loire, Army of the North. The siege of Paris begins. The Garde Nationale mobilised. Attempts at negotiation with Bismarck. The question of the armistice. Thiers’s European mission. The internal crisis: struggles between the Government of National Defence and the revolutionary movement in Paris. The 31 October insurrection. The issue of the siege: famine, bombardment, sorties. The capitulation of Metz (27 October) — the Army of the Rhine surrenders. The effect on the provinces. The end of the war; the preliminaries of Versailles. The volume closes with the Second Empire’s final legacy: defeat, occupation, and the uneasy birth of the Third Republic.

Notes

  • Series: Histoire illustrée du second empire by Taxile Delord; this is the Tome sixième et dernier of the illustrated edition (Baillière/Alcan [1880–83]), corresponding to the sixth and final volume of the original 1869–75 Baillière set (OCLC 6266334). Delord’s narrative ends with the fall of the Empire and the opening of the National Defence; it does not extend into the Paris Commune.
  • Illustrations: 56 wood engravings in the text (not preserved in this raw OCR) — the lowest count of the six illustrated tomes, reflecting the urgency of the war period.
  • Placement: Rest of Europe 1866–1919 wiki — the volume’s entire narrative (1868–1870) falls within this period’s chronological boundary. It sits alongside Delord’s Tome V (1867–1869) and Tome IV (1864–1867), both also in this workspace. Tome III (1857–1864) and Tome II sit in the 1800–1865 wiki.
  • Voice: Contemporary republican narrative history; represent what Delord states, without modern scholarly reframing. The tone darkens visibly as the narrative proceeds through the defeats of 1870 — from the galled satire of the press chapter to the elegiac descriptions of Sedan.
  • Related in corpus: Sybel’s Die Begründung des deutschen Reiches (vols. V–VI covering the same 1870 war from the Prussian victor’s perspective) sits under the German Unification shelf in this wiki. Delord’s is the defeated French opposition voice, complementing and counterpointing the German sources.
