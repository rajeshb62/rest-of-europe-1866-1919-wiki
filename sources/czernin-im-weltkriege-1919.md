# Ottokar Czernin — *Im Weltkriege*

Ottokar Theobald Otto Maria Graf Czernin von und zu Chudenitz (1872–1932), *Im Weltkriege*. Berlin und Wien: Ullstein & Co, 1919 (21.–30. Tausend). The memoir of Austria-Hungary's foreign minister from December 1916 to April 1918, published in the year of the armistice. The raw text in this wiki is the scanned copy (University of Michigan library), 452 pages, already OCR'd, concatenated in page order. The narrative runs through a Vorwort, twelve chapters, and a final Schlußbetrachtung, followed by an Anhang of seven documentary appendices and a Namen- und Sachregister.

## Vorwort

Czernin opens by disclaiming any intention to write the history of the war exhaustively — impossible in a small book and not its purpose. Its purpose is rather to portray single events and single personalities, which he saw from closer proximity and therefore more clearly than the public, to deliver individual moment-photographs of the great drama; out of these individual pictures a whole will nonetheless emerge that in many respects may deviate from the still so fragmentary war histories known until then. Every man sees men and events from his personal angle — that is unavoidable. He speaks of men who stood near him, of others who crossed his path without leaving a personal impression, and of still others with whom he had grave conflicts, and he tries to judge all of them objectively. He depicts men and things as he saw them; where the depiction appears false, the ground lies not in preconceived opinion but in a perhaps existing lack of judgment. Not everything was to be said; much was not explained though it could be explained, for the time separating him from the events is still too short to draw the veil from every occurrence. But what is suppressed changes nothing in the total picture, which he portrays as it mirrors itself in his head. Signed O. Cz.

## I. Einleitende Betrachtungen

Czernin's introduction to the war's causes. Just as a thunderstorm is preceded by the separation of electricities and an atmosphere no longer bearable, so the political barometer of the foreign ministries had stood at storm for years. The visible beginnings of the European tension reach back to the time of Edward VII: on the one side England's fear of the gigantically growing Germany, on the other the Berlin policy that had become the terror of the Thames and the belief that in Berlin the thought was taking root of seizing world dominion. He holds that France and England did not want the war in 1914, and describes the moral coalition against Germany, the mad armaments fever, and Czernin's last conversation with Franz Ferdinand. He treats the catastrophe, Berchtold and the ultimatum to Serbia, a Lichnowsky dispatch (Grey's mediation attempt), the telegram of King George to Prince Heinrich, the English proposal conditionally accepted, the war as unleashed by Russia, Tisza against the sharp ultimatum, and Tschirschky's own démarches — he, not Bethmann, for drawing Austria-Hungary into this war. He recounts his audience with the Emperor at Ischl, Rumania and Italy confronted with accomplished facts, and calls Austria-Hungary's greatest misfortune the German invasion of Belgium. On the evening of 4 August came the decision on England's neutrality at Germany. He marks the first fateful victory of the German military men and judges Bismarck's heritage a curse for Germany, Wilhelm II a prisoner of his generals. The Entente was never for a peace of understanding; he cites the London Pakt of 26 April 1915, Austria-Hungary's military inferiority, and the question of a separate peace for Austria as war with Germany. He closes with the politics of Stephan Tisza and the judgment that Austria-Hungary's disintegration was irrevocable.

## II. Konopischt

Czernin's portrait of the heir to the throne, Franz Ferdinand, framed by his estate at Konopischt. Konopischt has become the cradle of many legends, and the role the heir played before the war is subject to many and partly false commentaries. The Archduke-thronfolger was a quite peculiar nature: the main trait of his character was a great lack of balance — he knew no middle line, was as hot in hatred as in love, pursued nothing as other men do but did all he did in superhuman dimensions; his buying and collecting fury for antiquities was proverbial and bordered on the fantastic, and a first-rate shot, he hunted only on the greatest scale. Czernin treats the park of Konopischt, the Archduke's lack of linguistic talent, his embitterment, his antipathy to everything Hungarian, his aversion to cringers and flatterers, Franz Ferdinand and Aehrenthal, the great-Austrian program, the cooling of his relations with Franz Joseph, his approach to Kaiser Wilhelm, and his refusal of the role of warmonger. He writes of the Duchess of Hohenberg, the Archduke's personal fearlessness and his great qualities, the old Emperor, Franz Ferdinand's end, the planned reconstruction of the Monarchy, army and fleet, a three-emperors alliance against revolution, and the judgment that the Archduke was more a patron than an adversary of the Serbs and no partisan of a closer attachment to Germany.

## III. Wilhelm II.

Czernin's study of the German Emperor. He treats Wilhelm's belief in the divine right of kings (Gottesgnadentum), the emperor crisis of 1908, and his own dealings with Wilhelm II in 1917 and 1918. He writes of Emperor Charles's faith in his own popularity, of Byzantinism, of Emperor Charles and Conrad (von Hötzendorf), and the reinstatement of Joseph Ferdinand. He describes Wilhelm at Kiel Week, his nature, and the tragedy of his life. He examines his bearing before the world war, at the outbreak of war, at Kreuznach, his doubts about the war's outcome, and his oscillations in war aims. He recounts how the German Crown Prince became a pacifist in 1917, Czernin's visit to the Western front, a letter of Emperor Charles to the Crown Prince proposing territorial sacrifices in Alsace-Lorraine, Austrian renunciation of Galicia, and the attachment of Poland to Germany; and Ludendorff on the pacifist Crown Prince and Ludendorff's will.

## IV. Rumänien

Czernin's account of his time as minister in Bucharest from autumn 1913. He describes King Carol, the secret alliance as a scrap of paper, Bratianu, and Rumanian society. He recounts the day of Sarajevo, Take Jonescu, the sudden turn after the ultimatum — "Austria has gone mad" — and a wave of hatred. The King was full of grief; he writes of Carmen Sylva (Queen Elisabeth), and judges that Bratianu was never truly neutral. Queen Marie was for war. Czernin was interned; he recounts the Zeppelin raids on Bucharest and his return journey through Russia. He distinguishes three phases of the relations, holds that Hungary should make concessions while Tisza refused, and closes with reflections on diplomacy and on the Russian ruble's influence.

## V. Der verschärfte U-Bootkrieg

Czernin on his elevation to minister of foreign affairs and the decision for unrestricted submarine warfare. He recounts the peace offer of the Central Powers, Wilhelm II's judgment that the Entente had slapped him in the face, Tirpitz, the ruthless U-boat war, and Bethmann on the iron pressure of the military. He gives the warnings of himself and of Prince Hohenlohe, the visits of Zimmermann and Admiral Holtzendorff to Vienna, and Holtzendorff's guarantee of success; the optimistic posture of the German leaders still in April 1918; Hindenburg and Ludendorff; Ludendorff's judgment that the dynasty could not survive a peace of renunciation; his correspondence with Tisza; and the judgment that Germany misjudged the situation, the construction of further U-boats being restricted in the course of the war.

## VI. Friedensversuche

The longest chapter, on the peace efforts of 1917. Czernin sets out Austria-Hungary's foreign policy and Hungary's interference, Tisza, and a letter of Tisza. In February 1917 came peace feelers from tsarist Russia; then the outbreak of the Russian revolution, and fears on the Thames. In April 1917 he reported to Emperor Charles on Austria's exhaustion, the dull grumbling of the masses, America's intervention, and the necessity of peace. He gives Bethmann's answer, his attempt to reach the German Reichstag, Erzberger and Südekum, the Reichstag peace resolution of 19 July 1917, and Bethmann its victim; Michaelis's "as I understand it" and his letter demanding that Belgium, Courland, Lithuania and Poland be brought into close economic and military connection with Germany and that Longwy and Briey be made economically useful for Germany. He gives the demands of the German supreme command — military control of Belgium, Liège and the Flemish coast — and his own judgment that Belgium was a grave obstacle to peace. He treats the indiscretions and interferences, the belief of Paris and London in the dissolution of the Fourfold Alliance, the decline of the peace wish in the Entente, and the flirtation with a separate peace. He writes of England's fear of German militarism, the Stockholm socialist conference, the failure of the peace efforts, his Budapest speech on world disarmament, the fiasco of the U-boat war, Austria and the London dictate, England and Germany, the dead point, the misleading double policy behind the backs of the responsible men, Clemenceau for Germany's annihilation, and the intransigence of the Entente.

## VII. Wilson

On President Wilson in autumn 1917: his Fourteen Points, the national world problem, the President on Czernin's policy, and the interruption of the attempts at understanding by Czernin's resignation.

## VIII. Eindrücke und Betrachtungen

Short chapter of impressions and reflections: the defeat of the victors, Europe's self-laceration in 1917, and Hertling.

## IX. Polen

On the Polish question: Tisza against the Austro-Polish solution, Germany's demand for the evacuation of the part occupied by Austria, the Polish tactics, the German wishes for frontier rectification and Ludendorff's resistance, and the exchange of Poland for Rumania. He treats Czernin and the Poles, and Mitteleuropa.

## X. Brest-Litowsk

Czernin's account of the peace of Brest-Litowsk. He covers Kerensky's offensive and the Russian parties, the Bolsheviks, his journey to Brest-Litowsk, General Hoffmann, Joffe, Kamenev and Frau Bizenko, Kühlmann, and the annexionless peace. He gives the threats of the Bulgarians, the Germans' fear that the Entente might accept a general peace, Hoffmann on the border provinces, Hindenburg's furious telegrams over the "renunciation" of everything and Ludendorff's hourly telephone calls, the Russian contradiction and protest strike, the Ukrainians, the arrival of Trotsky, Czernin's demand for a free hand for himself in case the Germans broke with the Russians, Trotsky's acceptance of the ultimatum, Hoffmann's wish to "give the Russians a good beating," and his unlucky speech. He describes the famine catastrophe in Austria, Trotsky's reckoning on world revolution, Czernin in Berlin with Hertling and Ludendorff, Kaiser Wilhelm's telegram demanding Livonia and Estland, the peace with the Ukraine, and the Brotfriede (bread peace).

## XI. Der Friede von Bukarest

On the peace of Bucharest. Czernin treats Rumania and Hungary, the message of Emperor Charles to King Ferdinand, Austria's parity, Kühlmann informed by Czernin of the accomplished fact, and the judgment that without the King only an illegitimate peace with Rumania was possible. He writes of the Averescu ministry, his conversation with King Ferdinand, the Dobrudscha question, Marghiloman, his own constraint before the Hungarian frontier demands, Germany's economic demands, Germany's wish for occupation for five or six years after the general peace, and the Bulgarian-Turkish conflict.

## XII. Schlußbetrachtung

Czernin's closing reflection. The further the world war progressed, the more it lost the character of a work directed by individual men and took on that of a cosmic event that escaped the interference of even the most powerful individuals. The coalitions' agreements chained the cabinets to certain war aims; the promises given to the peoples, the whipped-up hopes of the advantages of final victory, the artificially bred boundless hatred, the increasing barbarization of the world, all created a situation that made every single man a small pebble of a stone avalanche that plunges down leaderless, choiceless and aimless and can no longer be directed by anyone. The Versailles Four-Power Council tried for a time to make the world believe it possessed the power to rebuild Europe according to its ideas — but these were four fundamentally different ideas, for Rome, Paris, London and Washington saw four different worlds, and the four representatives, "the big four," were each a prisoner of his programs, his promises and his peoples. At home one cursed and derided Wilson for abandoning his program; certainly there is not the least resemblance between the Fourteen Points and the peace of Versailles and St. Germain, but one forgets that Wilson no longer had the power to enforce his will against the other three. Clemenceau, the antipode of Wilson, was likewise not free: if he had tried to make a "Wilson peace," all the great and small rentiers of France would have risen against him, for they had been told for five years "que les boches payeront tout" — he did what he did with relish, but he had to do it, or France drove him away. He treats Italy's abyss of revolution and Lloyd George, concluding that the historical moment in which Wilson lost power and imperialism seized it was the year 1917. The Entente, which did not let the war be ended and continued the hunger blockade for months after the close of hostilities, made anarchy a world danger; anarchy and Bolshevism are siblings — Bolshevism is the most frightful grimace of state institutions, its father the war, its mother the famine, its patron despair. Versailles is no end of the war, only a phase of it; the war continues, if in changed form. Coming generations will not call the great drama that has dominated the world for five years the world war but the world revolution, and will know that this world revolution only began with the world war. Neither Versailles nor St. Germain will create a lasting work; in this peace lies the dissolving germ of death. Slowly, under unspeakable torments, a new world will be born; other generations will arise and build up what war and revolution have destroyed — to every winter follows spring, and to death resurrection. Signed, Juli 1919.

## Anhang

The seven documentary appendices: I. the decisions of the London Conference of 26 April 1915 (the text of the London Pact with Italy — the military convention, the promised Trentino, South Tyrol to the Brenner, Trieste, Görz and Gradisca, Istria, Dalmatia, Valona, the Dodecanese, the war loan of 50 million pounds sterling, and the exclusion of the Holy See from the peace — signed by Grey, Cambon and Imperiali); II. Count Czernin's note to the American government of 5 March 1917; III. State Secretary Helfferich on the U-boat war; IV. Count Czernin's speech in the Austrian Delegation of 24 January 1918; V. the protocol on the peace negotiations at Brest-Litowsk; VI. the protocol on the peace negotiations at Bucharest; VII. the Fourteen Points of Wilson. Followed by the Namen- und Sachregister (index of names and subjects).
