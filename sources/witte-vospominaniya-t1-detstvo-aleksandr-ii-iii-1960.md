# Witte — Memoirs, Vol. 1 (1849–1894)

**Count S. Yu. Witte (1849–1915)** — *Воспоминания.* Vol. 1: *(1849–1894). Childhood. Reigns of Alexander II and Alexander III.* — Moscow: Publishing House of Socio-Economic Literature, **1960**.

Introductory article and general editorship of commentary and index — **A. L. Sidorov**. Commentary on the volume — **R. Sh. Ganelin** and **B. V. Ananich**. Print run 75,000; set 15 March 1960, signed for press 31 August 1960.

**Place in the set.** The Soviet 1960 reissue arranged the memoirs **in chronological order** across three volumes:

| 1960 | Period on title | Chapters (running numbering) |
|------|------------------|----------------------------|
| **Vol. 1** (this file) | 1849–1894; childhood; Alexander II and III | **1–18** |
| Vol. 2 | 1894 – October 1905; Nicholas II | **19–51** |
| Vol. 3 | 17 October 1905–1911; Nicholas II | **52–70** |

**Vol. 1** covers career to the death of Alexander III — Tiflis and the Caucasus, Odessa and the South-Western railways, the “Holy Brotherhood,” Borki, the department of railway affairs, the Ministry of Ways of Communication, the Ministry of Finance under Alexander III. Nicholas II begins in [Vols. 2–3](witte-vospominaniya-t2-3-nikolay-ii-1960.md).

**Contents of the volume.** First-person testimony: ancestors (Witte, Fadeevs, Dolgorukys); the Caucasus under Vorontsov and Baryatinsky; education; service on the Odessa / South-Western railways; railway “kings”; 1 March 1881 and the “Holy Brotherhood”; Kiev under Drenteln; Alexander III’s journeys and the catastrophe at Borki; director of the department of railway affairs and journey to Central Asia; minister of ways of communication; cholera of 1892 and second marriage; Vyshnegradsky and appointment as minister of finance; portraits of persons in power; subordinates and activity of the Finance Ministry; Murman; extended characterization of Alexander III.

**Character of the edition.** Memoir text in **new (Soviet) orthography**; 1960 apparatus (Sidorov’s introduction, Ganelin–Ananich commentary, index). OCR medium–good: running heads “N Chapter …”, confusion of Roman/Arabic numerals and individual letters (П/Н, Ш/Щ); chapter headings restored from the table of contents and chapter openings.

## Chapters 1–18

*Before chapter 1 — introductory article by A. L. Sidorov, “Count S. Yu. Witte and His ‘Memoirs’” (assessment of Witte as a figure of the era of transition to imperialism and the revolution of 1905–1907; character of the memoir text).*

### Chapter 1. On ancestors

Birth in Tiflis, 1849. Father — Yuly Fyodorovich Witte, director of the department of state domains in the Caucasus. Mother — Ekaterina Andreevna Fadeeva, daughter of a member of the chief administration of the Caucasian viceroy; on the Fadeev line — Princess Elena Pavlovna Dolgorukaya (senior branch of the Dolgorukys from Grigory Fyodorovich). Grandfather Fadeev came to the Caucasus under Prince Vorontsov; earlier managed foreign colonies in New Russia and was governor of Saratov. Sisters; a woman writer in the family circle (E. A. Gan and others).

### Chapter 2. Rostislav Andreevich Fadeev

Uncle — only son of grandfather Fadeev; cadet corps; blow to an officer-instructor; exile as a soldier to a battery at Bendery under Nicholas I; later military and publicistic career. Family affection for “Rostik”; Fadeev’s role in the nephew’s fate.

### Chapter 3. Caucasian viceroys

Baryatinsky; appointment of Grand Duke Mikhail Nikolaevich. Recollections of the visit of the heir-tsesarevich Nikolai Alexandrovich with Count Adlerberg. Life and youth of the viceroy’s staff; a boy’s observations in Tiflis.

### Chapter 4. Recollections from childhood and youth

Early recollections (epidemic; grandfather on horseback); wet-nurse and nanny; family life; relatives’ laughter at the infant’s “impossible” memory. Transition to adolescence.

### Chapter 5. Early upbringing. Gymnasium. University

Grandmother Dolgorukaya teaches reading and writing; free-hired wet-nurse and serf nanny; pictures of household drunkenness. Gymnasium; university course (pure mathematics); intention to remain at the chair.

### Chapter 6. My service on the Odessa railway

University graduation (~1870). Prussia’s war with France; arrival of uncle Gen. Fadeev. Family objections to a professorship (“not a nobleman’s business”). Entry into railway service in Odessa; first years of practice.

### Chapter 7. On the railway kings

After the war: unprofitability of the Odessa, Kiev–Brest, and Brest–Grajewo lines; merger into the **Company of the South-Western Railways**. Competition of private companies; “kings” and superfluous figures when administrations were united (Odessa, Kiev, Białystok). Witte’s place in the new structure.

### Chapter 8. On the society “Holy Brotherhood” and my participation in it

1 March 1881: news in the Kiev theatre; letter to uncle Fadeev (comparison of the workshops’ “steam hammer” with the state’s powerlessness against anarchists). Participation in the “Holy Brotherhood”; Kiev context.

### Chapter 9. My service in Kiev

Governor-general and commander — Gen.-Adm. Drenteln (army rear in the Turkish war; chief of gendarmes; transfer from Odessa). Drenteln’s wife. Witte’s service in Kiev on the South-Western; local society and administration.

### Chapter 10. On Emperor Alexander III’s journeys over the South-Western railways. The catastrophe at Borki

Early visit of Alexander III with wife and sons Nicholas and George to Kiev. Escort of the imperial train (Andreevsky; replacement by Witte). **Catastrophe at Borki**; consequences for the Ministry of Ways of Communication and Witte’s career.

### Chapter 11. My appointment as director of the department of railway affairs. Journey to Central Asia

Vyshnegradsky at the head of finance after Posyet’s departure; request for views on the profitability of the network. Idea of controlling **tariffs** of private companies (higher charter norms; competition for freights). Appointment as director of the department of railway affairs. Journey to Central Asia.

### Chapter 12. My appointment as minister of ways of communication

After Borki: resignation of Adm. Posyet; Pauker. Characterization of Posyet (tutor of Grand Duke Alexei Alexandrovich; collision on the German Sea). Witte’s path to the Ministry of Ways of Communication.

### Chapter 13. Journey to the cholera epidemic. Second marriage

Spring 1892; cholera on the Volga. Alexander III’s commission to the “youngest minister” to inspect measures (waterways and railways; general powers). Secretary; Prof. Pavlovsky in Samara. Second marriage in the same years.

### Chapter 14. Ivan Alexeevich Vyshnegradsky. My appointment as minister of finance

Tsion’s memorandum against Vyshnegradsky (including accusation of a 500,000-fr. Rothschild bribe on the Paris loan); Witte’s report to the sovereign. Circumstances of Witte’s appointment as **minister of finance** in succession to Vyshnegradsky.

### Chapter 15. On public figures and persons in power while I was department director and minister

Portraits: Count D. A. Tolstoy (MVD; “extreme right”; two fleeting meetings); other ministers and dignitaries of the end of Alexander III. Assessment by actions, not by personal acquaintance.

### Chapter 16. On my subordinates and my activity as minister of finance

State Bank — Zhukovsky (reputation as a “leftist” from the 1860s *Sovremennik*; mediocre manager). Pleske — credit chancery, then bank manager; later Witte’s deputy as minister of finance. Recruitment of staff and first steps in the ministry.

### Chapter 17. On my journey to the Murman coast

Alexander III’s attraction to the Russian North (type of peasantry; famine-relief committee while still heir). “Murman steamship company” and figures of the heir’s circle (Sheremetev and others). Witte’s journey to Murman.

### Chapter 18. Emperor Alexander III

Summary characterization: “a great emperor”; undervalued by contemporaries; only 13 years of reign. Was not prepared for the throne (heir — elder brother Nicholas); circumstances of upbringing. Assessment of foreign and internal policy; death and reception in Russia and abroad. **End of the first volume** — on the threshold of Nicholas II’s accession (continues in vol. 2, ch. 19).
