# Ollivier — *L'Empire libéral*, Tome XVI (Livre XXIV)

Émile Ollivier, *L'Empire libéral; études, récits, souvenirs*, tome seizième. Paris: Garnier frères, 6, rue des Saints-Pères, **1912**. On the Internet Archive as [lempireliberal16olli](https://archive.org/details/lempireliberal16olli) (UCLA / University of California Library copy; series dating in metadata from 1895; this volume’s imprint is 1912). The half-title band names the subject **Le Suicide** — premier acte: Wœrth–Forbach; renversement du ministère par la majorité impérialiste. The body opens **Livre XXIV**.

The volume is Ollivier’s continuous memoir-history of the opening disasters of the Franco-Prussian War and of the parliamentary overthrow of his own ministry of 2 January. Dominant year in the text is **1870**, with narrative focus on **4–11 August**. It runs from Mac-Mahon’s Alsatian campaign (Wissembourg, Wœrth) and Frossard’s action at Forbach through the double crisis at Metz and Paris, the Chamber session that forces the cabinet’s resignation (9 August), the formation of Palikao’s ministry, the Emperor’s loss of effective command to Bazaine, the German concentration toward Metz, and the diplomatic formation of the Ligue des neutres. A block of *éclaircissements* and a short errata for tome XV close the volume.

## Livre XXIV — Le Suicide

### Avant-propos

Ollivier opens with the conviction, drawn from the study of facts, that no government is destroyed by its enemies — enemies prop the edifice like the flying buttresses of Gothic churches — and that governments perish only by **suicide**. Since 1789 successive powers have destroyed themselves: the Constituants exclude themselves from their work; the Girondins deliver themselves; the Jacobins annihilate one another; the Directors put their Republic up for auction; Napoleon I abdicates twice; Charles X abdicates and departs; Louis-Philippe abdicates and flees. These failures are contrary to honour. The volume frames the first act of the liberal Empire’s self-destruction: military reverse paired with the overthrow of the ministry by the imperialist majority.

### Chapitre premier — Wissembourg

Narrating a war one has declared is a double torment; Ollivier alone can restore certain unknown or ill-known facts. Disposition of Mac-Mahon’s army in late July: Abel Douay at Haguenau, Ducrot at Reichshoffen, Lartigue and Raoult at Strasbourg (Mac-Mahon’s headquarters); Félix Douay under the marshal. Mac-Mahon organises his advance toward the Lauter in four echelons. Description of Wissembourg; Abel Douay’s arrival on the evening of 4 August; he leaves Liaud’s battalion in the town and places the rest on the heights; his quietude; Mac-Mahon’s warning. March of the Crown Prince’s army on 4 August; Bavarian division Bothmer opens fire from the heights dominating Wissembourg (8 a.m.); dispositions of Liaud; general Pellé descends to his aid; Vth and XIth corps support the Bavarians; Abel Douay orders retreat and is killed; Pellé replaces him; Liaud, not having received the order, clings to resistance, falls, and his battalion surrenders (2 p.m.). Retreat of Pellé; taking of the Geissberg and Schafbusch; judgment of the combat; losses; Mac-Mahon’s dispositions. Napoleon III returns incompletely to Niel’s abandoned project of three armies and places Failly under Mac-Mahon.

### Chapitre II — Entre Wissembourg et Wœrth

German uncertainty over Mac-Mahon’s situation after Wissembourg; preparation to act south or west; conviction that he is camped on the heights of Wœrth; conversion westward and decision to attack on 7 August after a day of rest. Description and real value of the Wœrth position; how Mac-Mahon disposes his troops. Failly’s various movements until placed under Mac-Mahon; on the evening of 5 August Mac-Mahon orders him to Reichshoffen with his whole corps as soon as possible; Failly disobeys, holds a council of war, and decides to send only Guyot de Lespart’s division. Mac-Mahon believes he faces only 40,000 enemies and, awaiting Failly and Conseil-Dumesnil, makes 6 August a day of rest; Raoult, Ducrot, and Leusse urge withdrawal on Lemberg; Mac-Mahon consents and gives orders of retreat, then hears cannon toward Wœrth, rides there, and orders battle. The cannonade proves only a reconnaissance; Bavarian Hartmann treats it as the signal to enter battle; Bose on the left and Kirchbach with the Vth corps make the action general.

### Chapitre III — Wœrth

Kirchbach’s artillery silences the French guns while chassepots throw back troops trying the Sauer; the Crown Prince orders Kirchbach to stop; he continues, becomes critical, and is refused help by Bose and Hartmann before Bose finally sends Schkopp. Alternating success and reverse of Bose’s fractions; eastern edge of the Niederwald and Morsbronn taken; distress of Lartigue; Mac-Mahon promises Guyot de Lespart and puts Michel’s cavalry division at his disposal. Charge of Michel’s brigade. Arrival of the Crown Prince; imperfect execution of his instructions; Bose joins Kirchbach; struggle between Mac-Mahon and Kirchbach. Bavarians against Raoult; Suzzoni’s turcos hold them; Bose takes Elsasshausen and with Kirchbach menaces Frœschwiller; enveloping order of the Crown Prince. Heroic obstinacy of Mac-Mahon; charges of Bonnemain’s cuirassiers, of the artillery reserve, and of the 1st Algerian tirailleurs; arrival of the Württemberg brigade. State of the field; what Failly’s arrival would have produced; slowness of Guyot de Lespart; taking of Frœschwiller by the Bavarians; Mac-Mahon’s retreat (4 p.m.). Last resistances; Raoult will not leave the field and is killed; German losses. Mac-Mahon’s retreat; the Germans do not pursue; how he pushes the retreat as far as Sarrebourg. Failly during the day of the 6th. Causes of the defeat of Wœrth; grandeur of the battle.

### Chapitre IV — Bataille de Forbach

Description of the triangular massif between Sarreguemines, Saarbrücken, and Saint-Avold (Éperon and neighbouring heights). Continued uncertainties of the French general staff. How Frossard disposes his troops. German movement; Kameke engages the battle. Bazaine during the morning of the 6th. First manœuvres of Kameke; intervention of Bataille’s reserve; Alvensleben’s judgment; occasion of victory offered to Frossard, which he does not use. Reinforcements reach Kameke; Alvensleben takes direction of the battle; his plan is not realised and the French keep their positions. Frossard, though not beaten, constitutes himself as beaten; appeals to Bazaine, who sends Castagny, Montaudon, Metman. Causes of Frossard’s panic: approach of the advance-guard of the Prussian 13th division; he loses his head and flees toward Sarreguemines. Bazaine’s reinforcements arrive after Frossard’s departure; oscillations of the Guard; Prussian and French losses.

### Chapitre V — Qui est responsable : Frossard ou Bazaine ?

Mac-Mahon had accused no one, not even Failly, and had silently accepted full responsibility. Frossard tries to cover his nullity by charging Bazaine with not having succoured him in time. Examination: was Bazaine bound to go onto the field of battle? Ollivier weighs Frossard’s claim that the combat was to French advantage until four in the afternoon and that timely reinforcements would have turned failure into a magnificent advantage.

### Chapitre VI — Comparaison entre les deux armées

Above the particular facts of Wœrth and Forbach, judgment on the value of the two armies still under transformation. The new doctrinaire military school attributes French reverses to bad doctrine; Ollivier refutes that view and compares spirit of solidarity, initiative, and obedience in the two armies. Have the French shown less offensive spirit than the Prussians?

### Chapitre VII — L'Invasion

Real numbers of the German troops that invaded French territory. At the Prussian headquarters the news of Wœrth arrives incomplete in the night (“Deux aigles…”); Radziwill, Verdy du Vernois, and Brandenstein reconstruct the sense of the telegram.

### Chapitre VIII — A Metz après les défaites

Through 6 August sensational news succeeds at headquarters: morning rumour of a great French victory; Mac-Mahon’s dispatch that if Failly rallies he can take the offensive next day; Frossard attacked; station-master of Reichshoffen telegraphing retreat; Frossard first reassuring then crying distress. Prostration of the French staff. The Emperor’s idea of retiring on Châlons; Le Bœuf’s plan; the Emperor first rejects it, returns to it on Jérôme David’s intervention, then renounces again. Le Bœuf at Saint-Avold; in his absence the Emperor orders the Châlons retreat and asks the Council what effect it will produce at Paris; Le Bœuf’s intolerable situation and resignation; the Emperor’s consolations to Mac-Mahon. Prince Napoleon and Castagny counsel the Emperor’s return to Paris; opposition of the Prince Imperial; the Emperor spontaneously renounces the Châlons retreat; new ministry dispatch on the effect of that retreat; constitution of two centres, Metz and Paris; Bazaine’s plan; fatigues of the army in concentration on Metz (Montaudon’s testimony); Franceschini Pietri’s dispatch to the Empress asking the Emperor’s return to Paris; arrival of Changarnier at Metz.

### Chapitre IX — A Paris après la défaite. — Patriotisme ou partiotisme

How peoples bear reverse foreshadows their destiny — Greeks, Romans, English, French under the old kings, after the civil wars and the Revolution. **Partiotisme** set against **patriotisme**. Struggle of the two after Waterloo — Talleyrand, Lafayette, Carnot. Historians’ judgment (Mignet, Berryer, Barbès, Tocqueville) condemning partiotisme. Jules Simon’s admission that ministers, the majority of the Corps législatif, and the Opposition itself had regarded the French army as the first in the world.

### Chapitre X — A Paris. — Journée du 6 août

The Empress had promptly communicated the Saarbrücken skirmish (2 August) but was slower with Wissembourg: she received it at Saint-Cloud in the night of the 4th and only at eleven the next morning sent Conneau with a sealed packet for Ollivier. Effect at Paris of the defence of Wissembourg on the 5th; Trochu’s letter to Plichon. False news of a Mac-Mahon victory and its effect; Emile Ollivier on the balcony of the Ministry of Justice.

### Chapitre XI — La nuit du 6 au 7 août à Paris

About eight o’clock Chevandier sends a first Metz dispatch: Frossard is engaged, too far for help, hope not lost. Ollivier to Pietri: after today’s emotion tomorrow will be terrible. Police reports of rising fermentation; a long column descends the boulevards singing the Marseillaise and chanting “Ollivier!” — some cry “Ollivier à la lanterne! Vive la République!” and even “Vive la Prusse!” The ministry learns Frossard’s retreat and Mac-Mahon’s defeat; immediate measures for national defence. Arrival of the Empress at the Tuileries; ministers go to her; convocation of the Chambers fixed for 11 August despite the opposition of Ollivier and Chevandier. Second council at ten in the morning; the Council claims the Prince Imperial’s return; the Empress seems to consent and then dissuades the Emperor.

### Chapitre XII — A Paris — Journée du 8 août. — Activité des ministres et des partis

(Body OCR heading sometimes reads “journée du 7”; the table and sequence fix the 8th.) True sentiment of the Paris population and its patriotic attitude; the parties organise revolution. Programmes of the deputies of the Left, Centre-left, and Right; three distinct currents — against the Empire, against the Cabinet, against Le Bœuf. Appeal to Schneider and how he answers. Third council at nine in the evening; request for imperial authorisation to offer the War Ministry to Trochu; Ollivier’s report on public opinion; firmness of Baraguey d’Hilliers maintaining calm in the streets. Manifesto of deputies and journalists in *le Siècle*; resolutions on arming citizens; Trochu’s atrabilious disposition; interview of Ollivier and Trochu; Trochu’s refusal; contrast with Changarnier. Schneider asks Ollivier for the Cabinet’s resignation and is refused; Schneider offers the ministry to Trochu, who also refuses; Ollivier’s visit to the Empress and the questions she puts; he counsels offering War to Cousin-Montauban. De Pierres, the Empress’s écuyer, comes to ask Ollivier’s resignation; evening council interrupted by a deputation demanding the ministry’s dismissal; the Empress refuses; disorderly attempts repressed by Baraguey d’Hilliers. National-defence measures of the 8th begin to execute; Duvernois reproaches the ministers for lack of confidence in the Left.

### Chapitre XIII — Projet d'un coup de justice

In the first hours of the night after the terrible days of the 7th and 8th Ollivier walks his cabinet asking what to propose to the Council and the Chamber. First evidence: the bad fortune of the opening is due to the Emperor’s pitiful health and to the effect of his personal command. Chevandier proposes to arrest the revolutionary chiefs during the night; Ollivier adopts the plan in modified form. Return of Maurice Richard from Metz and his report on the situation there. Conference at the Tuileries among the Empress, the Prefect of Police, Chevandier, and Ollivier; Ollivier and Chevandier demand the Emperor’s return to Paris; Pietri supports them; the Empress after refusal seems to consent. Council of ministers; Persigny’s opposition to the Emperor’s return; in Ollivier’s absence (drafting with Dejean the declaration to be read to the Chambers) the Empress’s telegram calling the Emperor to Paris is withdrawn. Arrival of Palikao at the end of the council; short dialogue with the Empress and Ollivier.

### Chapitre XIV — Le renversement du ministère

From ten in the morning compact masses of workers descend from Belleville and other quarters onto the Place de la Concorde and the quays; four or five thousand communists, anarchists, and Blanquists are organised and resolved among them, mixed with plain republicans, Orleanists, and the usual curious. Baraguey d’Hilliers holds the approaches. Ollivier’s dispositions going to the Chamber; he reads the ministerial declaration from the tribune; insults of the Left; silence of abandonment from Centre and Right; violent emotion; he puts the question of confidence; Schneider sends his secretary to the Tuileries to announce that the fall of the ministry is certain. New dispositions of the Empress; she offers Palikao the formation of a new ministry; he accepts. In the Chamber: proposals of Latour Dumoulin and Jules Favre; Granier de Cassagnac’s apostrophe against Favre’s proposal; fury of the Left. Jérôme David declares that France was not ready; Talhouët’s proposal, rejected by the ministry, is voted by the Chamber. Baraguey d’Hilliers represses an attempt to invade the Chamber; Ollivier’s conversations with Thoinnet de la Turmelière and with Persigny. Duvernois finds a formula that unites all parties against the ministry; impossibility for Ollivier to speak; vote by rising and sitting on the Duvernois motion. Ollivier reads the ministry’s resignation from the tribune; attitude of the deputies toward him; Jules Simon’s interpretation — the Empire exists only in name. The Empress asks the Emperor for Le Bœuf’s dismissal; summary dismissal of the Cabinet by the Empress. The fall of the ministry of 2 January has been a national catastrophe.

### Chapitre XV — Cinquième victoire prussienne. — La haine du partiotisme l'emporte sur l'abnégation du patriotisme

The sitting of 9 August was like a military reconnaissance revealing the enemy’s dispositions. Jules Simon’s frank admission: until the fall of the Empire it was neither a man nor a meeting of men that commanded, but **hatred**. Paul Déroulède’s severe judgment on the opposition parties dominated by personal passions at such an hour. Patriotic attitude of the comte de Chambord, of the prince de Joinville, of Changarnier. Role Thiers might have played and the one he did play.

### Chapitre XVI — A Paris : journées des 10 et 11 août. — Le ministère du suicide

Portrait and career of general Cousin-Montauban, comte de Palikao — uncertain name forms, birth at Paris 27 June 1796, marriage, physique, service record. Schneider proposes to the Empress a “ministry of last prayers”; Palikao wants a different one; his difficulties; the Empress’s expansion with Segris. Palikao ministry; judgment on those who compose it; replacement of Baraguey d’Hilliers by general Soumain. Diverse national-defence measures — forced course of banknotes, armaments, permanence of the Chamber accepted by the ministry.

### Chapitre XVII — Metz. — Effet du renversement du cabinet du 2 janvier. — Destitution de l'Empereur

The Emperor is afflicted by the fall of the ministry: the Empress had given too wide a latitude to her authorisation of necessary changes; he had believed a partial change of persons, not the overthrow of the Cabinet itself, and had accepted Palikao as war minister in *their* cabinet, not as head of a new one. He measures the consequences and feels himself struck in the men to whose devotion he had counted; Le Bœuf is with him when the news arrives. Sentiments of Changarnier; mission of Duperré; increase of Bazaine’s powers; the Emperor persists in not delivering Le Bœuf. He renounces the constitution of two different corps at Paris and Metz and returns to concentration on Metz; defective manner of that concentration; what the true plan ought to have been. Summons of the ministry to the Emperor to dismiss Le Bœuf; the Emperor abandons the command and imposes it on Bazaine, who had refused it; letter of the Emperor to Emile Ollivier.

### Chapitre XVIII — Le commencement du suicide

Joy and hope that the Court had drawn from the ministry’s fall are short-lived. The happy battle that was to cast the fallen ministers into public contempt and halo their successors does not come; the unanimity the Court thought to obtain for the Emperor by sacrificing the cabinet to opposition hatreds and anarchist plots is not obtained; it has been necessary to sacrifice the Emperor after them and strip him of his command. The slide has begun — royal majesty sinks more easily from the summit to the middle than it climbs again.

### Chapitre XIX — Marche des Allemands sur Metz

Moltke’s fundamental plan has not been completely achieved: two French corps are beaten and entry on French territory is open, but the principal French army has not been annihilated between the three German armies. He rejoices nonetheless for distant consequences (“Eine victory is always welcome…”) and resumes what remains: march on Paris, crush the army met, prevent it from gaining the south, cut it from its communications. Germans organise to throw French forces back on Metz so as to march on Paris without hindrance. Surrender of Lichtenberg; heroic resistance of Bitche and Phalsbourg. Cavalry used more regularly for scouting; painful German march; constant expectation of French attack and stupefaction at being left the field free.

### Chapitre XX — Effet à l'étranger. — La Ligue des neutres

Success makes enterprises appear just; the day after Wœrth and Forbach the French are treated as culprits duly chastised. Americans speak aloud of a government that aided the Secession rebels and tried to plant a monarchy on the southern frontier; in Europe some do not hide their satisfaction. Hidden or open joy of neighbouring nations; sadness of Victor Emmanuel, who consults La Marmora on the possibility of aid; Sella carries the cabinet to refuse all help; Italian public opinion hostile to intervention for France. Italy proposes to England a Ligue des neutres; England fears being drawn into action and first refuses; Cadorna makes clear that the League will on the contrary permit non-intervention; Granville and Gladstone adhere. Visconti’s explanation of the League. Ollivier’s attempt with his Italian friends to decide an intervention; futility of his journey; regrets of Victor Emmanuel and La Marmora.
