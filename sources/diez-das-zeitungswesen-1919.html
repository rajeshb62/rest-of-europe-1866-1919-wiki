Diez — Das Zeitungswesen

Dr. Hermann Diez (b. 1866), Das Zeitungswesen. Leipzig und Berlin: B. G. Teubner, 1919 — Aus Natur und Geisteswelt, Sammlung wissenschaftlich-gemeinverständlicher Darstellungen, Bd. 328, Zweite Auflage (2nd edition). OCLC 62599474. The Vorwort is dated Berlin, April 1919. Diez surveys the German newspaper industry as history, contemporary trade, and critical reflection. The second edition drops the separate treatment of Zeitschriften and Fachpresse (periodicals and the specialist press) — “die ja ohnehin nicht im eigentlichen Sinne zu dem ‚Zeitungswesen’ gehören” — to a promised separate booklet, and uses the freed space for war and revolution material. The Vorwort opens on the unfavourable moment of reprint: the World War has shaken the national life and economy of the press; the hope that the brilliant development pictured in the first edition would resume after peace has been ruined by the war’s unhappy outcome; then came the Revolution, which must often destroy before it can build, and which, while driving the intellectual life of the press forward, threatens the already undermined economic existence of many papers. Under these circumstances no unified picture of German newspaper life can be drawn — one scarcely knows what still exists, still less what will become — yet the storms will pass, and German newspaper life will rise rejuvenated from the fires of the time.

Dominant years and themes for this wiki’s period are the Kaiserreich press, censorship and Preßfreiheit, the news-agency system (Wolff / Reuter / Havas), the World War, and the Revolution of 1918/19.

Vorwort zur zweiten Auflage (Lines ~200–227)

The reprint is forced at a moment unfavourable to any survey of German newspaper life. War, then Revolution, have broken the continuity of the first edition’s picture; periodicals and the specialist press are deferred to another volume so that war and revolution expansions can take their place. Signed Dr. Hermann Diez, Berlin, April 1919.

Einleitung (Lines ~301–698)

The Einleitung presents the periodical press at the opening of the twentieth century as the most distinctive and strongest among the formative powers of the age — already imposing as a part of economic life, incomparable as an organ of human society in all its cultural relations. The press hears and sees everything; remoteness may still delay news by days or, in extreme cases, weeks, but in general the events of Ultima Thule need no longer to circle the globe than news once needed to pass from one European capital to another. Speed of the news service is not luxury but the indispensable tool of a world-expanding outlook — root of Weltpolitik, world economy, and practical cosmopolitanism. The press does not only report; it claims expert judgment over its whole range and claims to embody the authority of öffentliche Meinung (public opinion) — a concept to which it first gave shape and life, and behind which it can also shelter. Whether this rise to a “great power” is a blessing remains for cultural criticism; the fact of its significance does not.

Diez distinguishes the words Zeitung, Presse, and periodische Presse. Zeitung originally meant the news-item itself (Schiller still uses it so in Don Carlos); only later did the paper take its name from its content. Presse transferred from the printing tool to the periodical products of the press — hence Preßgewerbe, Preßfreiheit, Preßrecht. Press is the genus, newspaper the species; the distinction between Zeitung and Zeitschrift (newspaper and periodical) resists a clean definition — folding versus binding is a practical but not a conceptual test; the newspaper aims at a higher degree of Aktualität, its centre in reporting and the discussion of day- or week-events, while the periodical surveys longer spans, essays rather than articles, and can become a book of lasting value when bound. Newspapers arise from the need to know more of one’s time than eyes and ears can grasp, and from the need of individuals or communities to speak to contemporaries more simply and accessibly than from pulpit, stage, or book. Early papers were poor in local news because local life was still known by word of mouth; foreign orientation came first, then state and trade. The press won, in a centuries-long emancipation struggle, the right to speak on all public affairs; it also dragged formerly private matters into publicity. Its claim to speak for public opinion dates from the era when it became an organ of peoples’ liberation — earlier than in the parliaments. That mandate remains hard to control and often fictitious; the hypertrophy of newspaper enterprise under freedom of trade also proves that business speculation, not only the people’s need of a new organ, calls papers into being. The fusion of political aims with commercial speculation creates a dubious newspaper type, sharpest where the masses’ political judgment is unripe.

I. Die Geschichte des Zeitungswesens

1. Die Wurzeln und Anfänge (Lines ~700–974)

Modern newspaper life has a fourfold root: the song of the travelling player (fahrender Sänger / Spielmann), the Flugblatt (flyer), the letter, and the Meßrelationen (fair-relations). Roman Acta Senatus and Acta diurna are early official publicity but no continuous link to the sixteenth-century beginnings; the Peking court gazette likewise forms no bridge. Newspaper life sprang spontaneously from social need, not from reason of state. Stieve (via Scherer and Herz) makes the Spielleute the wandering journalists of the Middle Ages; after Gutenberg (1450) their songs became printed “newspapers,” still often in rhyme into the seventeenth century. Flyers carried Turks, emperors, ceremonies, battles, prodigies, plague, crime; Luther’s Ninety-Five Theses spread as flyers. The letter, already organised in Rome for provincial officials, grew through monasteries, princes, towns, and scholars; in the last medieval centuries commercial correspondence centred on Venice, Rome, Nuremberg, Augsburg (Fugger), Ulm, Frankfurt, Antwerp — circular “Zeitungen” and detached news supplements. Venice’s Scrittori d’avvisi on the Rialto and Rome’s Novellanti / Gazzettanti (from a small coin, gazzetta) are often cited as first journalists’ guilds; Diez doubts how much this explains the German development. The oldest surviving German written newspaper in Munich begins 1583 (Hans Merer compiling Fugger news for Regensburg); a Nuremberg weekly of 1587–91 was long thought the oldest. Printed newspapers lagged because of confidentiality, censorship avoidance, and small readership. The fourth root, the Meßrelationen, half-yearly news digests for the fairs of Frankfurt, Leipzig and other markets — invented by Michael von Aizing (Relatio historica, Cologne/Frankfurt, from 1583) — approach the modern concept. Rudolf II’s order of 1597 that news of his Turkish war be printed only monthly produced short-lived monthlies. The oldest surviving printed newspapers proper include Johann Carolus’s Strasbourg Relation of 1609 in Heidelberg.

2. Das Zeitungswesen vom Dreißigjährigen Kriege bis zur Französischen Revolution (Lines ~975–1237)

Frankfurt is the first scene of newspaper competition: Egenolph Emmel’s weekly from 1615 (claimed ancestor of the later Frankfurter Journal), Johann von den Birghden’s Unvergreifliche Postzeittungen / Wochentliche Zeitungen from 1617 (postmaster vs bookseller; already a political colouring — Emmel more bourgeois-Protestant, Birghden imperial-Catholic), then Schönwetter’s third Frankfurt paper (1619). Leipzig gets the first daily from 1 January 1660 under Timotheus Rizsch (Neu-einlauffende Nachricht von Kriegs- und Welthändeln; today’s Leipziger Zeitung). Munich, Cologne, Hamburg and the Netherlands show early breadth; content is still mainly reportage, with tendency already in selection and tone. Papers were a thin national bond in the fragmentation sealed by Westphalia; war favoured the trade, its aftermath often surrendered the lead abroad.

England’s first printed weekly ~1622, first daily (Daily Courant) only 1703; Star Chamber censorship, monopoly under the Restoration (Oxford then London Gazette), final fall of censorship 1695, stamp duty from 1712, grotesque fights over parliamentary reporting. France: printed weekly from 1631 (soon a state paper); first daily Journal de Paris 1777; but France pioneers advertising — Montaigne’s father’s wish for a register of wants, Renaudot’s bureau d’adresse (1612/1630), transplanted to Germany as Intelligenzblätter. Hamburg has a Relations-Kurier with heavy “Notifikationen” already 1637; Prussia introduces intelligence offices by cabinet order of Friedrich Wilhelm I (6 January 1727); revenues to the Potsdam military orphanage until 1850. By the late seventeenth century every larger German town had at least one paper. Consentius’s monograph on early Berlin papers supplies a detailed case: privileges, monopolies, Rüdiger’s Berlinische Privilegierte Zeitung (1721), Voss, Lessing on the learned section, the Vossische Zeitung name from 1785 (to Ullstein in 1913); Haude’s Berlinische Nachrichten (1740), later Spener’s, to 1873 — “Tante Voß” and “Onkel Spener.” Hamburg’s Correspondent, Königsberg’s Hartung (1660), Breslau’s Schlesische Zeitung (1742; Frederick the Great as war correspondent; 1813 printing of “An mein Volk”) close the sketch.

3. Die Zeitungszensur (Lines ~1238–1311)

Through this whole span development is slowed, narrowed, interrupted by the state’s self-evident right to supervise and suppress newspapers. Censorship predates newspapers: Cologne process 1479/1477, Alexander VI’s bull of 1501, Speyer 1529 and Augsburg 1530 (printer and place on every print), Reich press ordinance 1548 making censorship a lasting police institution. Catholic lands kept ecclesiastical censorship; Protestant university towns used the university, sometimes with the magistracy — no kinder. Zürich newspaper censorship from 1622; Berlin papers provoke diplomatic complaint in Vienna (Schwarzenberg, 1622). Friedrich Wilhelm I forbade papers at accession so people should not “räsonnieren,” then restored strict censorship. Frederick II coined the famous word that gazettes must not be bothered if they are to be interesting, but did not make it a governing principle; after his death the darkest men and the strictest censorship returned.

4. Der Kampf um die Preßfreiheit und die Zeitungen des 19. Jahrhunderts (Lines ~1312–1628)

Resistance was long passive; Enlightenment made the fight for Preßfreiheit a popular part of inner political liberation. The French Revolution did not bring press freedom; Robespierre inaugurated suppression; Napoleon tolerated only a slavishly obedient press, treating press freedom as a mark of democracy suitable perhaps for England — yet fearing England might one day fall by it. Only in the Hundred Days did he allow full freedom; definitive abolition of French censorship dates from 1830. The German Confederation’s constitution of 1815 foresaw press freedom; the Carlsbad Decrees and federal press law of 1819 restored the sharpest reaction. Görres’s Rheinischer Merkur stood in the first rank until struck down; Cotta’s Allgemeine Zeitung (founded 1798, fled to Ulm then Augsburg) gathered young literary Germany and even Swiss political debate. Older still: Schwäbischer Merkur (1785), Hamburger Nachrichten (1792; later Bismarck’s mouthpiece in his grumbling years), Kölnische Zeitung (name and Dumont-Schauberg ownership from 1802, roots older). Daily post to the West let Voss and Spener go daily from 1830. Baden’s press law of 28 December 1831 was the first German statute on the ground of press freedom; 21 December 1848 proclaimed press freedom for the whole Reich territory. Concession duty and newspaper stamp remained substitutes for censorship; the stamp vanished in Germany in the 1860s (Austria to end of 1899); the Reich constitution made press supervision a Reich matter; the Reich post law of 28 October 1871 ended withdrawal of Postdebit except for foreign papers.

Party press and leading titles: 1848 Berlin National-Zeitung and Neue Preußische (Kreuz-)Zeitung (Bismarck among founders), Munich Neueste Nachrichten; then Frankfurter Zeitung (Sonnemann, 1856), Kölnische Volkszeitung (1860), Norddeutsche (from 1918 Deutsche) Allgemeine Zeitung (1862; half-official to the Revolution), Post, Börsen papers, after 1871 Germania, Berliner Tageblatt, Vorwärts (1884), Deutsche Tageszeitung, Berliner Morgenpost (1898), etc. War brought evening news sheets; Revolution boosted Social Democratic papers — Vorwärts twice daily, Independents’ Freiheit, Spartacus Rote Fahne, Republik, Freie Zeitung. From mid-century the party direction dominates; from ~1860 legal-administrative barriers fall; a generation later industrialisation and the Generalanzeiger type (e.g. Berliner Lokal-Anzeiger, 1880s–90s) weaken party character for business. The Revolution’s effect on newspaper life cannot yet be surveyed.

5. Die geographische Verteilung der deutschen Zeitungen (Lines ~1629–1687)

Despite Berlin’s post-Reich boom, the German press kept a many-shaped local development; all landscapes share in it; old imperial trading centres remain densest, but the giant cities have no monopoly — every place of independent intellectual or economic life has its paper. Kriegsresseamt yearbook 1917 (with 1918 supplement): in the Germany of 1914 borders, 2,066 newspaper towns with 3,227 newspapers (Prussia 1,147 / 1,871; Bavaria 259 / 380; Saxony 167 / 232; etc.; Alsace-Lorraine 25 / 48). About 550 towns have more than one paper. By contrast England and France concentrate: London’s newspaper zone covers nearly a third of England proper (38 of 230 UK dailies in London); Paris had 69 dailies before the war.

6. Aus der Geschichte der außerdeutschen Presse (Lines ~1688–1896)

Austria developed more slowly and thinly than “im Reich”; centralist and clerical Erblande; long reprinting of German papers; Joseph II’s censorship patent of 11 July 1781, stamp 1789, reaction under Leopold II; Metternich’s brief press push 1808–09 (Vaterländische Blätter); among surviving old titles only Wiener Zeitung (1703), Preßburger Zeitung (1762), Salzburger Zeitung (1774) are over a century old. Switzerland won press freedom somewhat earlier; Helvetic episode short; Regeneration of the 1830s; 1848 constitution makes press freedom fundamental. England: fall of censorship 1695 did not yet mean full political press freedom; eighteenth-century persecution; Junius letters; Sheridan’s toast to an unfettered press; stamp 1712–1855; Times (from 1785 Daily Universal Register, name 1788; Delane 1841–77) as first-rank power; no multi-daily mornings; Northcliffe (Daily Mail 1896) and Pearson; Northcliffe’s capture of influence on the Times, German-hatred, wartime propaganda ministry — for Diez an alarming latest stage despite older English models. France: type of the critical paper; personality and style; politicians found or buy their own sheet — Napoleon having prescribed signature of political articles.

7. Die Entwicklung der Zeitschriften (Lines ~1897–~2224)

Periodicals share periodicity, topicality, and many-sidedness in softer form; they enjoyed earlier collaboration of leading minds and more freedom under censorship. Christian Thomasius (1655–1728) founds German periodical life with his German critical monthly (1688–89). English models (Review, Tatler, Spectator) feed German “moral weeklies”; literary feuds (Gottsched vs Swiss); Nicolai; Wieland’s Teutscher Merkur (from 1773); Justus Möser, Schubart’s Deutsche Chronik, Weckherlin, Schlözer’s Staatsanzeigen, Moser, Campe — then Napoleonic pressure, Cotta’s Morgenblatt (1807), and the nineteenth-century magazine world. (The second edition truncates this strand relative to a full periodicals treatment deferred to the promised separate booklet.)

II. Die Presse der Gegenwart

1. Die deutschen Zeitungen zu Anfang des 20. Jahrhunderts (Lines ~2225–2918)

No official German newspaper statistics; private catalogues of advertising firms, the Kriegsresseamt yearbook, and provincial monographs must serve — already partly outdated by war and revolution. Early twentieth century: growth almost exclusively in six-times-weekly papers; old weekly small-town sheets die or increase frequency; twice-weekly remain the simplest rural form (strong in Posen, Silesia, Mecklenburg, East and West Prussia) but also decline; six-times (plus seven-to-eight) near half of all Reich papers at war’s start. War pushes many papers to twice daily. Tables for 1918 give totals by state (overall ~3,177 papers in the printed table) and a rough political grouping (conservative, National Liberal, Centre, Polish/Danish, liberal/freethinking, party-less, etc.) — imperfect because self-declared direction is often vague. Further pages map circulation bands, capital concentration, Generalanzeiger economics, and the structure of the trade on the eve of catastrophe.

2. Die deutschen Zeitungen im Kriege (Lines ~2919–3056) — period-core

Even in the war’s promising beginnings the effect on German newspaper life was far from as favourable as the huge demand for news might suggest. Big city news sheets and street sale swelled; late-evening organs flourished; but existence rested ever more on advertising, whose collapse at outbreak (often reckoned at about two-thirds) devastated most dailies. The official postal newspaper list at times showed over 1,200 titles temporarily suspended — more than a tenth of the total — though only ~200 were true newspapers, mostly modest provincial sheets whose life hung on a called-up owner. Staff shortage, wage rises with dearness, above all paper scarcity and price — even after the Kriegswirtschaftsstelle für das deutsche Zeitungsgewerbe regulated distribution and the Reich repaid a modest part of excess prices — weighed like a nightmare; Diez expects these troubles to last long after the war, recovery of advertising not while goods scarcity lasts, “perhaps not in ten years.”

Spiritually, convinced of the justice of the cause and of victory, the German press was in the first war years a faithful tool, strong support, and inspiring leader; most Social Democratic papers also held high the flag of the threatened fatherland. Censorship of military news under the law against betrayal of military secrets was accepted as inevitable; but the practice of censorship, and especially its spill onto politics, caused friction and lasting ill-feeling. Regular Berlin editorial conferences with ministries and the Kriegspresseamt (from 1 November 1915) did not fully cure the evils; the demand to dismantle censorship went unsatisfied until too late — only then a check on the unlimited power of the deputy general commands, unity under the Prussian war minister, and finally responsibility of the Reich Chancellor. Cable destruction and Central Powers’ isolation hurt the news service; yet great telegraph bureaus and individual papers built intermediary posts in neutrals, and Wolffs Telegraphisches Bureau continued to supply even enemy news material; enemy papers were almost never barred from the Reich. Delays from news censorship hurt. Unification of content by official W.T.B. matter and free official/semi-official correspondences — especially Nachrichten der Auslandspresse and Deutsche Kriegsnachrichten from the Kriegspresseamt / OHL — was welcomed by smaller publishers, lamented by larger papers and free writers. Foreign-language press in Germany: Alsatian opposition French sheets suppressed; enemy-colony organs and understanding-papers (Journal d’Allemagne, Continental Times) vanished (the latter restarting early 1919); prisoner and propaganda sheets appeared.

3. Die deutschen Zeitungen in der Revolution (Lines ~3057–3113) — period-core

Effects of the Revolution are of very different kinds. On 12 November 1918, fourth day, the Council of People’s Commissars proclaimed abolition of censorship; yet from the first day radical groups occupied major Berlin newspaper plants for their own organs. The Berlin Communist putsch of 5 January 1919 began with occupation of the great Berlin newspaper businesses and of Wolff’s Telegraph Bureau, forced to stop for a full week. The March general strike laid the capital’s whole newspaper life idle for some days. This play of principled liberation and actual violence repeated in Hamburg, Bremen, Düsseldorf, Munich, Leipzig. In Bavaria, after Kurt Eisner’s murder (21 February 1919), the purely socialist government introduced pre-censorship for political papers and forced the largest bourgeois newspaper enterprise into its service; the Bavarian soviet republic (7 April) renewed pre-censorship and put socialisation of newspaper enterprises — especially abolition of private advertising — on its programme, following the Russian Sovnarkom decree of November 1917 making paid advertising a state monopoly for soviet organs only. Diez judges that without a socialist majority such radical interventions will not last (Munich until the soviet republic’s fall in early May); some cutting back of large-capitalist form in newspaper life might even be salutary; but for now Revolution has enormously sharpened the economic crisis into which war had thrown the German press.

4. Die Auslandspresse im Kriege (Lines ~3114–3198)

Diez links England’s turn from Germany toward France and Russia in the first decade of the century — the constellation of the World War — to a “press Triple Entente” of the Times, the Spectator, and the National Review, and to Edward VII’s finding in Harmsworth / Northcliffe the chief of staff of encirclement. Belgian diplomatic reports from Brussels (found later) paint this “yellow press”; ties alleged to Amsterdam Telegraaf and New York Times. Liberal English papers kept a moderate tone at first; after the Lusitania a united front. France: Humanité after Jaurès’s murder lost ground; Hervé’s sheet became La Victoire; Clemenceau’s paper (Homme libre / Homme enchaîné) never knew measure in hatred of Germany. Threads to Rome Messaggero and Milan Secolo; Corriere della Sera and d’Annunzio; Sonnino’s Giornale d’Italia, Idea nazionale, Mussolini’s Popolo d’Italia. In America Hearst papers were fairly German-friendly until U.S. entry, even fighting British cable censorship via International News Service; from February 1917 they swung round and German-American papers vanished. Among neutrals Germany found understanding mainly in some Swedish papers and German papers of eastern Switzerland; western Switzerland (Geneva, Lausanne) matched Belgian refugee press in venom. On the press field too Germany faced huge superiority and went under.

5. Das Preßrecht der Gegenwart (Lines ~3199–3452)

The flowering shown in the statistics was fruit of the Reich’s economic-political rise and of the largely model press law of 7 May 1874: imprint of printer and publisher, responsible editor for periodicals, deposit copies, compulsory paid official notices, correction paragraph (§ 11 — Diez wants editors able to refuse demonstrably false corrections), bans on certain collections and premature process documents, no administrative or judicial stripping of press-trade capacity (§ 4), limits on police seizure, responsible editor as dolose perpetrator where the anonymous author escapes, negligence cascade through editor–publisher–printer–distributor, jury competence for press offences in some southern states, immunity for true parliamentary reports, unsuccessful efforts to free editors from witness duty (Bülow’s 1907 circular urging restraint). Copyright law of 19 June 1901 ends near-limitless reprint theft — unconditional reprint only of factual news; conditional reprint of unsigned newspaper articles with source; scientific/technical/entertainment pieces forbidden. Courts still often hostile (Reichsgericht denying § 193 StGB protection for editors claiming public interest). Swiss Bundespräsident Morel (1894) is quoted on the press as a kind of public office. Official, semi-official, and “offiziöse” relations — Norddeutsche Allgemeine Zeitung, Reptilienpresse and the Welfen / “Reptilienfonds” legend — belong largely to the past; war unfreedom and flood of official matter, and Revolution’s effects, have already been treated; the freer the state, the more important and responsible newspaper work becomes.

6. Die Teile der modernen Zeitung und die Mitarbeiter (Lines ~3453–3551)

A modern paper of larger style: political part (supreme since press freedom, retreating in party-less sheets), local/provincial, commercial, and literary-artistic-belletristic (centre in the Feuilleton). Strict separation impossible. Leitartikel ties to news; Feuilleton from light amusement to full cultural factor; commercial centre in price list and Bourse report; sport rising. Far-reaching division of labour; closing and night desks; Anglo-American models for making the day’s chief events visible at a glance (without American placard style). Organised reportage equalises news across a city’s papers; over 150 correspondences (about 100 in Berlin) supply ready copy from leading article to puzzle corner; mutual reprint is strongly developed in decentralised Germany (rare in England/France); plate and matrix supply to small provincials; extreme “headless” papers finished at a metropolis save a local front page — uniformly not yet entrenched.

7. Die Depeschenbureaus (Lines ~3552–3694) — high value for the newswires theme

Among the newspaper’s most important collaborators, for political papers indispensable, are the great telegraph bureaus — born quickly after scientific and telegraph advances, flourishing once first mistrust fell. Organisation nearly simultaneous in Berlin, Paris, London.

  • Reuter (London): Julius Reuter, German-born (Kassel 1816), first enterprise at Aachen after the Berlin telegraph (1849), to London 1851 after the Dover–Calais cable; present form 1865; ~2 million Mark capital; after Baron Herbert’s death (April 1916) taken over by a consortium headed by M.P. Mark F. Napier, apparently not without government help; Amsterdam branch.
  • Havas (Paris): name from a translation bureau of 1835; Charles Havas’s pigeon post to London and Brussels (1840); son Auguste uses railways and telegraph from 1850; for a time news given against advertisements; joint Reuter–Havas agency in Brussels.
  • Wolff / W.T.B. (Berlin): Dr. Bernhard Wolff (physician, then Vossische partner, business head of the new Nationalzeitung from 1848) starts commercial then political news service 1849, selling subscriptions to papers and the Bourse; 1865 Kommanditgesellschaft (Wolff director-general to 1871); 1874 Aktiengesellschaft Continental-Telegraphen-Compagnie. With Bismarck’s fostering interest, Reuter’s competition is driven from Germany and relations with foreign bureaus settled by treaty. Capital 1 million Mark, reserve as much, peacetime dividend ~10% almost entirely from foreign business; no profit since war outbreak. 800 employees; over a million Mark yearly in telegraph/telephone fees. Provincial bureaus (Munich, Stuttgart, Frankfurt, Bremen, Hamburg) become Wolff branches — Hamburg’s Neue Börsen-Halle had previously depended on Reuter. Telephone (1890s) decentralises via new agencies; leased wires remain for exact long texts.

Until the war the foreign service rested on cartel treaties. The basic Wolff–Reuter–Havas pact divided the world into exclusive competence spheres: Reuter greater Britain and East Asia; Havas the Romance world (with Reuter, South America); Wolff the German Reich and colonies plus northern and eastern Europe. Later national agencies — Vienna k.k. Telegraphen-Korrespondenz-Bureau, Rome Agenzia Stefani, St. Petersburg Telegraph Agency, Copenhagen Ritzaus Bureau — complicated but did not erase the scheme. British cables and empire gave Reuter overseas weight; yet receiving bureaus chose and worded what they issued, and Wolff kept independent agents in London, Paris, Brussels, Vienna, Rome, St. Petersburg, Constantinople, New York, plus the right to push German-interest news worldwide alone or with cartel partners.

War largely destroyed this organisation; how much can be rebuilt is doubtful. Exchange continued via neutrals — Ritzau (Copenhagen), Nederlandsch Telegraaf-Agentschap (Amsterdam), Schweizerische Depeschen-Agentur. Wartime foundations: Transocean G.m.b.H. (government and industry money; international service to supplement Wolff; wireless); Übersee-Dienst G.m.b.H. (economic centre of gravity). In 1919 the news bureau of the Verein deutscher Zeitungs-Verleger affiliates to W.T.B. and takes over among other things the parliamentary service. Smaller bureaus (Hirsch-Herald, Presse-Centrale, etc.) merged 1913 as Telegraphen-Union, second great German news bureau beside Wolff; a second international cartel system began in the war. Abroad: Associated Press (mostly mornings) and United Press (evenings), profits to member papers.

8. Die Technik des Zeitungswesens (Lines ~3695–4011)

(a) Redaktion: gathering, sifting, supplementing; space allotment; layout; editing under constraints of space, line, criminal law, readers, visual picture; style-policing; translation. Raw material from day, correspondences, Depeschenbureaus, staff, other papers; prepared themes and special reporters; editorial conference under the Chefredakteur once advertising volume fixes size. Fraktur for most dailies; Antiqua for papers with foreign circulation and scholarly periodicals. Factor over composing and press; Metteur en pages distributes copy (written one side only for cutting).

(b) Setzerei: Linotype (Mergenthaler, Cincinnati 1884), Typograph (Rogers 1890), Monotype overturn hand-setting; ship, column, proofs, imposing, stereotype mats for rotary presses; Autoplate casters (three plates a minute); line-casters remelt type but need near-perfect copy because corrections mean whole new lines.

(c) Druckerei: rotary press for larger runs (~6,000 up); hand to high-speed press transition and modern plant close the technical chapter (cross-reference to Teubner Bd. 175, Unger, Wie ein Buch entsteht).

9. Die Expedition und das Abonnement (Lines ~4012–~4235)

Finished paper to the Expedition for fastest circulation. Outside the local circle, distribution is the Post’s privilege; inside, branches, agencies, carriers. Outside subscriptions via Post (fees now by frequency and average weight, not subscription price); abroad partly same, else under band (dearer). Street single-copy sale matters in big cities but never as in France, England, America — Germany has no camelot class; war nonetheless made street sale very important in German cities. Circulation statistics are hard: papers hide small or fluctuating figures from advertisers and rivals. Kriegsresseamt handbook tables band ~2,000 papers by circulation brackets.

10. Der Zeitungsverlag und das Inseratengeschäft (Lines ~4236–4415)

Once the paper’s content was an end in itself and the publisher might bleed for it (Cotta as “paymaster” of the Allgemeine Zeitung for decades). Now the newspaper has generally turned back into a pure business; only party papers still represent the older type. Karl Bücher’s formula: formerly the paper sold its news to its readers; now it sells its readership to every solvent private interest. A great modern paper cannot live from subscribers — each new subscriber is a cash loss on production cost; expansion matters only insofar as it raises advertising. Jubilee figures for Münchner Neueste Nachrichten: daily production cost over 7,400 Mark, subscription/sale income only ~2,612 Mark (~one third); advertising must cover the rest and profit. Similar ratios for Neue Freie Presse and even the Times. Publisher interest thus parts from editorial interest; the latter serves only where it fits the former. Tables of Christmas-season advertising grosses document the scale. Schleuderpreise and free use of the press by authorities and private persons are organisational fighting grounds.

11. Der Stand und seine Organisation (Lines ~4416–4769)

Strange disproportion between the press’s undisputed importance and the wavering state and social esteem of the journalistic estate — unlike Britain, America, or republics where editor and minister trade places. In exam-stamped Germany the day-writer was long not treated as full currency: mistrust of “legitimate” authorities, distance from scholars, social slight touching education and livelihood. Journalism was long a refuge of the derailed; small local sheets still often unfit. Yet great-paper staffs are largely academically qualified or self-made talent; journalists are “born.” Movements for raising the estate stress economic security and training; some urge a journalistic higher education. Organisations: publishers’ and editors’ associations (interests partly shared against administration and courts, partly opposed); printers’ Tarifgemeinschaft as a model social achievement (Verband deutscher Buchdrucker ~30,000; Deutscher Buchdruckerverein >2,000 principals; census 1907: ~18,886 polygraphic establishments, 208,852 persons).

III. Kritischer Teil

1. Die Presse als sozialpsychologischer Faktor (Lines ~4771–~5040)

The newspaper boom falls in the age when culture becomes mass culture — third estate emancipated, fourth pressing after, creating its own press against bourgeois mass sheets. Transition culture is unsympathetic (statecraft, science, art descending to the masses; money dirtied below) yet the richest soil for newspaper life: born of extensive horizons, now also the million-membered organ of intensive popular life. The paper lives from the mass for the mass; generally impersonal, representing not only owners and staff but an indefinite circle of subscribers whose presumed mood shapes the sheet — while claiming to be organ of public opinion and simultaneously creating and leading that opinion. A great characterful paper in that exchange would be a “moralische Anstalt” in Schiller’s highest sense; practice is often anarchic. Pure business papers sacrifice their own standpoint to readers, patrons, advertisers — the “principles” of the party-less sheet. Even declared party papers fear local and financial displeasure. The multitude, deep into the educated, lets the paper suggest opinions and passions; respect for the printed word is shamefully great; mutual depression of newspaper and mass instincts. American/English society vanity and “getting into the paper” infect Germany; fear of bad publicity buys silence; Revolverpresse and unclean advertising are exceptions or scandals, but the great question remains whether the press as mass ruler and mass instinct, as grand educational tool and “Verdummungsanstalt,” raises or lowers humanity — empirically hard to answer, to be pursued through the following critical chapters.

2. Die Zeitung im Staatsleben und in der Politik (Lines ~5041–5284)

Carlyle: England’s true church the newspaper editors; nineteenth-century authorship the great motley unlimited parliament of the world. The press is as indispensable as electricity and more constitutive of public and personal life. It has drawn public-law functions to itself: parliament without publicity withers (Reichstag incident of 1906); openness of justice rests on the press; diplomacy has handed most of its real work to the press and learns its news from great papers, not from beautiful women and bribed valets. In foreign policy the press is often disastrous — mass of political print forces novelty, revelation, harsh polemic; the sharpening of German–English relations that led to world war was substantially a press work — yet the press can bind as well as divide (British Empire on “paper feet”; Anglo-American linguistic community). German-language press abroad matters for cultural community. Modern states that have had to renounce administrative control still need influence or their own press activity: Caesar’s Acta, Frederick II in the Schlesische Zeitung, Napoleon’s suppression, Bismarck (Kreuzzeitung; Ems/press communiqué of July 1870; alliance publications; “cold water jets”; journalism against the New Course after his fall). Official papers (Reichs- und Preußischer Staatsanzeiger, state Staatszeitungen, Prussian Kreisblätter); semi-official correspondences; offiziöse press and the old Reptile legend — past forms; freer polity means heavier newspaper responsibility.

3. Die Zeitung als Geschichtsquelle (Lines ~5285–5428)

The day’s chronicle ages fast, yet gains lasting value as a source for historiography, personal and factual. The mass of volumes oppresses libraries; the historian faces a waste — yet here the pulse of the age will still be felt after a hundred years; the ensemble of papers fills gaps and corrects partisanship. Heidelberg newspaper library; Aachen newspaper museum (von Forckenbeck); Spahn’s plea for a Reich newspaper museum; methodological trials — Scheffer on Prussian publicity 1859, Nirrnheim on Bismarck’s first ministry year and opinion, Mittelstaedt on 1859, Penzler’s seven volumes on Bismarck after dismissal (Hamburger Nachrichten 1890–98) as a path through anonymous, mixed-value copy. Scholars still hesitate (Bernstein’s Berlin labour movement; de Gosses on the Nord under the Second Republic often thought not “zünftig”); yet no past age had such sources. Editors and publishers should separate original, characteristic, historically valuable matter for later researchers when present duties of discretion lapse.

4. Die Presse und das geschäftliche Leben (Lines ~5429–~5460)

Short bridge from the advertising-centred modern paper to the economy at large: the press as market, price, and business intelligence — indispensable to trade as to politics — with the same double edge of information and interested distortion already treated under publishing and social psychology.

5. Die Presse und die geistige Kultur (Lines ~5461–5745)

The press as companion of intellectual and artistic life: less by positive creation than by warding off aberrations of art and taste; theatre criticism’s merits (and over-indulgence of naturalism and novelty); literary criticism’s mass decisions that make the “book of the day,” entrusted in better papers to competent heads — decisions not for eternity, not always right, but formative of popular taste.

Schluß. Die Zukunft des deutschen Zeitungswesens (Lines ~5746–5940)

Existence of the modern paper rests economically wholly on advertising; predominance of advertising interest flattens editorial content toward the opinion-less Generalanzeiger. That flattening is a premise of factory- or department-store newspaper operation on American and English models, advancing in Germany before the upheaval of November 1918 — not yet Northcliffe-trust extremes, but on the way. Berlin newspaper life is already in far fewer hands than outward variety suggests: Mosse, Scherl, Ullstein, plus large-capitalist combines issuing like-coloured or colourless sheets across the Reich; cost of independent news service drives the concentration. The development threatens the originality and many-sidedness of German spiritual life and the party press’s existence — yet parties remain indispensable media after the Revolution more than ever. End-point would be monopoly of a few big-city capitalist concerns — huge power in few, perhaps unworthy, hands, and loss of corrective variety. Mass circulation past a sharp limit makes a paper necessarily worse: cheapness via advertising maximises readers for advertisers, then the publisher loses interest in further spread; space shrinks by paper cost; the much-read sheet thinks of advertisers and paper prices, not of events or readers. Sensation, hasty telegrams, unverified “news” educate a hunger the public did not naturally have — though much conscientious work still exists and political instinct learns to sort false from credible. Whether Revolution means a turn — perhaps a split into costly colourless news sheets and serious political papers valued for spiritual-moral qualities, not the mad race to know first — is an earnest future question; politicisation of the broad strata, including women, might yet work the change. Preserve papers whose news is chosen with care and whose leaders and feuilletons are serious stylistic performances; stop the unnatural cheapening of the newspaper price (10 pfennigs a day would hardly burden the small official or tradesman); select more carefully those entrusted with the pen; university attention to newspaper life (cultural-historical, legal, technical) should grow — not only for future journalists but for all called to leadership, ignorance of newspaper work having been a root of administrative and judicial animosity. The estate’s economic raising (adequate salaries, security against illness, age, invalidity) will lift morals and educational claims. Of the journalist today perhaps more than of the artist it is true that the dignity of the people and of humanity is placed in his hand.
