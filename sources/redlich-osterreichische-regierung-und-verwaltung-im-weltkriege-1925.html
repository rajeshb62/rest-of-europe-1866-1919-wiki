Josef Redlich — Österreichische Regierung und Verwaltung im Weltkriege

Josef Redlich (1869–1936), Österreichische Regierung und Verwaltung im Weltkriege. Wien: Hölder-Pichler-Tempsky A.-G.; New Haven: Yale University Press, 1925. A volume of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, Wirtschafts- und Sozialgeschichte des Weltkrieges (Economic and Social History of the World War), Austrian and Hungarian series, under the general editorship of James T. Shotwell and the Austrian editing committee of Friedrich Wieser, Richard Riedl and Richard Schüller. Redlich, professor of public law at the University of Vienna and a former minister, wrote the culminating study of the Habsburg state’s government and administration at war. The raw text in this wiki is the scanned copy (University of Michigan library), 398 pages, already OCR’d, concatenated in page order. The narrative runs from an Einleitung through nine chapters, followed by the Literatur-Angaben, a Kapiteverzeichnis, and an Anhang setting out the plan and publication list of the Carnegie series.

Einleitung

Redlich announces his subject: the Austrian war government as both political organ — bearer and expression of a particular conception of the Austrian state — and as supreme administrative organ. He notes the peculiar pre-war condition of Austrian government: the ministry in Vienna had long exercised in practice the legislative power too, meeting the unavoidable needs of legislation through the emergency-notification provision of the constitution (Article 14) by imperial decree; the ministry had resolved to keep this system through the war, the Reichsrat having been sent home in the spring of 1914 on the renewal of obstructionist tactics among the Slav parties. He frames the whole as the last chapter of three and a half centuries of Habsburg government and administration — for the state that bore it disappeared entirely with the war, the Allied war aim of destroying the Habsburg realm having been realised in the peace treaties.

I. Regierung und Verwaltung in Österreich vor dem Kriege: ihre Organisation, ihre Aufgaben und ihr politischer Charakter

The pre-war system, in seven sections: the foundations of state power; the Emperor and the question of the monarchy; the ministries and ministerial responsibility; the Reichsrat, the upper and lower houses, and the nationality struggles within them; the organs of self-government and the provincial (crown-land) administration; the place of the officials and the political character of the civil service; and the relations between government and parliament on the eve of the war. Redlich describes an administration already working largely by Article-14 decree and marked by the deepening nationality conflicts.

II. Die legislatorischen Vorsorgen der österreichischen Regierung für den Kriegsfall

The legislative preparations made before 1914 for the event of war: the emergency statutes and decrees prepared for war, the penal and censorship provisions, the contemplated suspension of civil liberties, and the readiness of the whole apparatus of state to pass into a war footing at the outbreak.

III. Die letzte Friedensregierung in Österreich

The last peace government: disappointment at the supposed healing effect of the universal suffrage of 1907; the precarious position of the officials’ ministries before the house elected under it, as visible signs of the national-political decomposition of the realm in both Hungary and Austria; the influence of the struggle over the annexation of Bosnia in sharpening national antagonisms in Hungary, the intensification of the conflicts with Croatia and the perpetual keeping-down of the nationalities by the Magyar gentry; the growth of an anti-parliamentary mood at court, among the generals and in the conservative circles of Austria; the unceasing accession of national radicalism among the Czechs, South Slavs and Ruthenians; the worsening of the Monarchy’s position toward the Western powers, Russia and Italy since the annexation of Bosnia; the reflux of all this on the separatist and dissolving currents among South Slavs, Poles and Ruthenians and on the nationalist democracy of the Czechs, with symptoms during the Balkan wars of 1912–1913; and the influence of these phenomena on the political strivings of the Germans of Austria and of the Magyars.

IV. Die österreichische Kriegsregierung und der Geist ihrer Administration

The outbreak and the war government: the suppression of the Austrian parliament at the opening of the war; the issue of the emergency decrees; the passing of civil public power to the Armeeoberkommando over wide areas; the transformation of civil into military administration over a great part of the state territory, and the consequent permanent tension between the Austrian government and the supreme army command; the opening conflicts between population and army leadership, above all in Galicia, toward Poles and Ruthenians alike; the army command’s constant efforts to extend its powers; the unconstitutional transfer of jurisdiction over wide fields of civil justice to military courts; the suspension of jury courts throughout the state; the complete militarisation of the railways; the dictatorship of the army leadership over the civil population; the office and activity of the Kriegsüberwachungsamt (war surveillance office); and the system of the Austrian war government down to the assassination of the minister-president Count Stürgkh in the autumn of 1916. Redlich traces the supremacy of the Hungarian government and of Count Tisza, the subordination of Austria, Stürgkh’s tenacious anti-parliamentary policy supported by the German-bourgeois parties, the consequent suppression of public life, and the strictly bureaucratic character of the war government with its ruinous effect on the whole public condition of the state.

V. Die österreichische Kriegsregierung und ihre Verwaltungstätigkeit

The war government at work: the dictatorial legislation and administrative activity; the government’s provision for the victims of war (Kriegsfürsorge); the participation of the population in relief for those stricken by war-need and want of food; the organisational measures for raising the war loans, supported among the German population alone by the forces of society; the beginning of resistance and conflict with the authorities in the non-German lands; and the growing difficulty of carrying through the state food administration in the hinterland.

VI. Die öffentliche Verwaltung und die Kriegswirtschaft

The public administration and the war economy: the nature of the economic tasks that the modern popular war imposed on the state; the concept of the war economy as the inevitable consequence of the mass war; the blockade and its influence on the formation of the Central Powers’ war economy; Austria’s economic position at the opening of the war; the government’s concern for the feeding of the people from the very outset; the conduct of Hungary in regard of export to Austria — Austria’s complete economic isolation by Hungary; the introduction of a planned state raw-materials economy and the rationing of foodstuffs; the organisation of the Kriegsgetreideverkehrsanstalt (war-grain traffic institute); the creation of the new central authority for food, the Amt für Volksernährung; the administrative apparatus for securing the food supply; a survey of the laws and decrees by which the state food economy was carried out; the Wirtschaftszentralen as the second principal form of the war-economy organisation, extended over the whole field of industrial production and trade, with their statistics, types and tasks; the character of the state war economy as a whole and its practical consequences for the social condition; its nature as a price-police, the principle of maximum prices first fixed in the grain economy; the securing of raw materials for the war industry; the rise of the industrial war economy and the step-by-step abolition of free economic intercourse, first in the cotton and then in the metal industries; the rise of the special war associations (Kriegsverbände) of the several branches of industry and trade; and the progressive militarisation of industry through the administration of the Zentrale.

VII. Die Kriegswirtschaft und der Staatssozialismus des Krieges in Österreich

The war economy and the state socialism of the war: the middle position of the Austrian government between the Armeeoberkommando and the war ministry on one side and industrial production and the consuming population on the other; the enormous extension of the whole war-economy apparatus and its capacity; the new organisations created in the war ministry; the militarised civil bearers of this war economy, and the double organism of military and civil war-economy leadership; the difficulties and defects of the civil administrative authorities in face of the war economy; this practical war-state-socialism as a theory developed in Germany and Austria; the further formation of this theory in the doctrine of the necessity of a transitional economy (Übergangswirtschaft) at the coming of peace; the creation of the Generalkommissariat für Kriegs- und Übergangswirtschaft as the high point of the intellectual development of the war economy in Austria; the retrospect on the rise and development of the war economy in the Denkschrift of the ministry of commerce of 1917; the connection of its ideas with the movement founded by Friedrich Naumann for a Mitteleuropa on a political and economic unity under Prussian-German leadership; and the failure of the war economy in Austria as a consequence of the overstraining of the physical, moral and spiritual forces of the population and of the excessive length of the war.

VIII. Die zweite Periode der Kriegsregierung

The second period of the war government: the death of the Emperor Francis Joseph and the accession of Emperor Charles; the weakening of the army command’s influence on the Austrian government and on the shaping of home politics; the ministry of Koerber as the transition from the “unpolitical” to a political war government; the formation of the ministry of Count Clam-Martinitz for the purpose of a dictatorial remodelling of the constitution in a German-national sense; the programme of the German-nationals for the future home and foreign policy of Austria and the peace-of-victory policy after the outbreak of the Russian revolution; the convocation of parliament and the appearance of separatist strivings among Czechs and South Slavs; the return of the Clam-Martinitz government to the system of officials’ ministries and its permanent difficult position in parliament; the retirement of the Kriegsüberwachungsamt and the softening of the war dictatorship; the political standing of the several nations, the struggle of the Slav parties and of the social democracy against the absolutist war government and against militarisation; the uncertain attitude of the Polish club toward the government; the conflicts between the two houses of the Reichsrat; the reaction of the army command’s early measures against the non-German peoples on their political conduct; the amnesty granted by Emperor Charles and the deep resentment of German-Austrian public opinion at it; and the juxtaposition of dictatorship with a house of deputies in its majority hostile to the war, the rapid sinking of the government’s authority, the growth of anti-dynastic currents, the successes of foreign propaganda among the non-German peoples and the recognition of the Czechs as a belligerent nation, the October Manifesto of the Emperor as the last attempt to preserve the Reich, the paralysis of parliament and its dissolution into purely national groups, the beginning of the catastrophe in September 1918, the collapse of the state food economy in the war zone and in the hinterland alike, the peace offers of the foreign minister Baron Burian and the ministry of Lammasch, the increasing paralysis of the central government and the dissolution of the Austrian state — the collapse of Reich and state.

IX. Der Untergang des Reiches, die neuen Nationalstaaten und das altösterreichische Amtentum

The fall of the Reich, the new national states and the old-Austrian officialdom: the adaptation of the old-Austrian civil service to the new conditions created by the collapse; the survival of the bureaucratic institutions in the national states and the quiet continuance of the officials’ work; the nationalisation of the old-Austrian officialdom in the provinces during the last two decades of the Reich; the reaction of the war economy on the independence of the provincial administration; the reaction of the political forces set free by the war on the officials’ attitude toward the idea of the unitary state; the growing democratisation and nationalisation in the disposition of the state’s officials long before the war, at length penetrating the German officialdom too; the loosening of the whole administrative apparatus, wholly so during the war government; the retrospect on the principal moments in the dissolution of the central state organisation; and the extinguishing of the state idea at length in the bureaucracy as in the free intelligence and the masses of the several nations. Redlich concludes that the October Manifesto of Emperor Charles was the formal, legally valid dissolution of the historic unitary state: by his word the Emperor himself — exercising for the last time the right of autocratic constitutional legislation that the house of Habsburg had always claimed — called the complex of new national states into being, and thereby released the officialdom of every nationality for its share in the revolutionary steps that followed. The dissolution of Reich and state went forward on a formally legal path; the last act of the dynasty gave the new states, ready formed in the person of their national officials, the organs with which to begin independent government, and so made the parting and the first independent steps of the peoples easier than they would otherwise have been.

Literatur-Angaben

A brief account of the material on which the work draws, disclaiming any exhaustive bibliography: the daily press of Vienna, Prague and the other provincial capitals as a principal source for the war period (its value lowered by the strict censorship), the radical press as the richest source for the historian; the weekly Der österreichische Volkswirt of Walter Federn and Gustav Stolper as an unequalled treasury and running commentary for all the economic phenomena of the war years; and the works and reports of the war administration itself — the printed emergency statutes and decrees, the handbooks of the war-economy organisations and their activity reports, the Denkschrift of Richard Riedl on the transitional economy, the report of the commissions that examined the war-economy organisations, and the protocol of Friedrich Adler’s trial before the exceptional court of May 1917.

Anhang

The plan of the Wirtschafts- und Sozialgeschichte des Weltkrieges: the editors and national editing committees (Austria-Hungary under Wieser, Riedl and Schüller with Shotwell as chairman; Germany under Melchior, Bauer, Bücher, Duisberg, Mendelssohn Bartholdy and Sering; Belgium under Pirenne; France under Gide; Great Britain under Beveridge, Davis, Jones, Keynes, Hirst and Scott; Italy under Einaudi; Jugoslavia under Bajkitsch; the Netherlands under Greven; Rumania under Mitrany; Russia under Vinogradoff; Scandinavia under Westergaard and Heckscher), and the list of publications of the series — the Austrian volumes on the government and administration (Redlich’s own), on industry, on the regulation of food and agriculture, of labour, on the railways, on coal supply, on crime and war, on the influence of the war on the spirit and manners of society (Seipel), the volumes on the war costs, on foreign economic policy and the exhaustion and dissolution of the Monarchy, and the corresponding Hungarian and public-health volumes.
