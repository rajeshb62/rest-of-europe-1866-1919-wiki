# Hanotaux — *Histoire de la France contemporaine*, Tome III (*La Présidence du Maréchal de Mac Mahon — La Constitution de 1875*)

Gabriel Hanotaux, *Histoire de la France contemporaine (1871–1900)*, tome troisième. Paris: Ancienne Librairie Furne, Société d'Édition Contemporaine, 5, rue Palatine (VIᵉ); no imprint year on the scanned copy (Internet Archive metadata: *n.d.*; publisher field also records Paris, Combet for this issue). On the Internet Archive as [histoiredelafran03hanouoft](https://archive.org/details/histoiredelafran03hanouoft) (University of Ottawa / Robarts–University of Toronto copy; ABBYY FineReader OCR; 774 images). The half-title names the subject **La Présidence du Maréchal de Mac Mahon** with the band **La Constitution de 1875**. Series framing (IA description and peer volumes): I *Le gouvernement de M. Thiers*; II–III *La présidence du maréchal de Mac Mahon* (two volumes); IV *La République parlementaire*. This wiki holds [Tome I](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t1.md), [Tome II](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t2.md) (*L'Échec de la Monarchie*), [Tome III](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t3.md), and [Tome IV](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t4.md).

The narrative runs from the ministerial crisis of **May 1874** (fall of the first Broglie cabinet; Cissey–Fourtou) through the constitutional laws of **February–July 1875**, the Buffet ministry and the European “alerte” of 1875, the dissolution of the National Assembly, the senatorial and legislative elections of **1876**, the first parliamentary cabinets under Mac-Mahon (Dufaure, then Jules Simon), and closes on the presidential letter of **16 May 1877**. Dominant years in the text are **1874–1877**.

## Chapitre premier — L'Assemblée nationale et le Suffrage universel

Three years after Bordeaux: the National Assembly has made peace and repressed the insurrection, proclaimed itself constituent, yet still has given no constitution. The right is torn among the three monarchical parties; the Republic survives by their divisions. **24 May 1873** overthrows Thiers; a year later (**16 May 1874**) the duc de Broglie falls in turn. Failure of the Goulard combination; formation of the **cabinet Cissey–Fourtou** (12 May 1874); the Bonapartist peril and universal suffrage; the dissident centre droit. The Lefts accept constituent power; constitutional proposals including **Casimir-Perier**; municipal electoral law of **11 July 1874**. The septennat bars the road to royalty; failure of the Cissey cabinet. Project of the **commission des Trente**; rejection of Casimir-Perier’s motion to establish the Republic; adjournment of the constitutional debate; maintenance of the state of siege; prorogation of the Assembly from 5 August to 30 November 1874. Thiers urges dissolution and appeal to the country; the right recoils from the suffrage and clings to the septennat as a term operation.

## Chapitre II — Le Septennat, la France et l'Europe

Practical work of the Assembly beside the constitutional struggle: budget of 1875; resignations of Magne and de Fourtou; **Mathieu-Bodet** at Finance and baron de Chabaud-La Tour at the Interior; military laws; public works; the phylloxera; social questions and the parliamentary inquiry on labour; the **Roussel law** on protection of infants; reform of the baccalauréat. Foreign policy under German diplomacy in 1874: the Kissingen attentat and the ultramontane question; cardinal Guibert’s mandement; recall of the *Orénoque*; Spanish affairs and fear of German intervention; Alphonsist restoration; rivalry of Gortschakoff and Bismarck; Eastern affairs and first lineaments of a Franco-Russian understanding; British dispositions; the Suez Canal and Egypt; the Tonkin affair; the United States and international arbitration; the Brussels conference and the law of nations; international sanitary police — the policy imposed on France. Society in 1874; the Marshal’s journeys; complementary legislative and local elections; presidential message on the Assembly’s return; higher-education law in first reading; parliamentary inquiry ordered on Bonapartist manœuvres.

## Chapitre III — La République fondée

The decisive session of January 1875. Initiative of the commission des Trente; conferences at the Élysée; presidential message of **5 January 1875**; the government asks priority for the Senate law and is refused; ministerial resignation, then provisional maintenance. First reading of the law on the organization of the public powers; first deliberation on creation of a Senate. Second deliberation: the three sittings of **28–30 January**; solemn debate on article 1; **Laboulaye** amendment developed and rejected after Louis Blanc’s intervention; **Wallon** amendment; negotiations of the Lavergne group; royalist sentiments; Desjardins sub-amendment rejected; the Wallon amendment carried by **one vote** and its consequences. Continuation: dissolution of the Chamber and revision of the constitutional laws; seat of the public powers remains at Versailles; second deliberation of the Senate law; **Pascal Duprat** amendment; declarations of the Trente and the government; dissolution projects; extreme confusion. The right offers dictatorship to the Marshal; Broglie refuses to form a ministry; the right against Buffet; the Lavergne group mediates between centre gauche and centre droit; the Marshal abandons nomination of the irremovables; agreement concluded; vote of the Senate law and of the law on the public powers.

## Chapitre IV — Le Cabinet Buffet et l'Alerte de 1875

Epilogue of 25 February: Bonapartism on the bench after the constitutional votes. Parliamentary inquiry on the Bonapartists; partial elections; retirement of the de Cissey cabinet; formation of the **Buffet ministry**; duc d’Audiffret-Pasquier president of the Assembly; Buffet’s uncertain policy; adjournment 20 March–11 May; Gambetta’s speech at Ménilmontant. The **alerte of 1875**: continuation of the Kulturkampf; German press campaign against France; war fears; duc Decazes appeals to the powers; Gontaut-Biron and Radowitz; Decazes’s attempt to rally Europe; Schouwaloff’s démarche at Berlin; Hohenlohe with Decazes; a *Times* article; intervention of England, Austria, and Italy; change of front in Germany; the Tsar at Berlin; Russian circular — peace assured; what to conclude from the incident. Resumption of the Assembly session: suppression of partial elections; complementary constitutional projects; new commission des Trente; law on higher education; election of the Nièvre and the *comité de l’appel au peuple*; Buffet and the Left; vote of the organic law on the relations of the public powers and of the senatorial electoral law; budget of 1876; adjournment 4 August–4 November 1875.

## Chapitre V — Théorie de la Constitution

General character of the Constitution of 1875; state of mind of the constituents; analogies with the American constitution. Precedents: French constitutions since the Revolution; the republican aspiration; precursors (Montesquieu, Rousseau, Condorcet); revolutionary experiments, direct government, power of committees, imperial dictatorship, return toward legitimacy; popular sovereignty imposed on the Restoration; electoral census and the directing classes; antinomies of 1848; advent of democratic Caesarism; the “liberal Empire”; the nineteenth-century constitutional circuit; democracy and the Republic. Doctrines: Auguste Comte, Proudhon, Tocqueville, the elder duc de Broglie, Prévost-Paradol; theorists of parliamentarism; influence of political literature on the National Assembly; “decentralization.” Theory of the constitution: sovereignty of the people; law of majorities; universal suffrage; rights of minorities; national unity; conciliation of unity and liberty; hatred of personal power; representative regime; the parliamentary Republic; two Chambers with preponderance of the popular Chamber; the presidency; the cabinet; government in office; the reign of opinion; the Constitution of 1875 before the history of France; merits and defects. Hanotaux stresses the anonymous, improvised character of the work — three constitutional laws (Senate 24 February; public powers 25 February; relations of the powers 16 July) and two organic laws (senatorial elections 2 August; deputies 30 November) — viable despite the disdain of many of its authors.

## Chapitre VI — L'Agonie de l'Assemblée

With the constitution voted, the Assembly has only to yield its place. Vacations of August–November 1875; Buffet affirms a conservative policy; ministerial dissensions; Bardoux and Léon Say incidents; instructions of the comte de Chambord; Rouher’s speech; Thiers at Arcachon; Gambetta’s letter to the Lyon democracy. Last session: legislative electoral law; *scrutin de liste* versus *scrutin d’arrondissement*; Gambetta breaks with the centre droit; the Assembly chooses *scrutin d’arrondissement*; Buffet reconstitutes the majority of 24 May; vote of the law of **30 November 1875** and the electoral circumscriptions. Election of the **75 irremovable senators**: first ballot neutralizes Lefts and right; pact among extreme right, Bonapartists, and Left; the seventy-five. Agony of the Assembly: commission of graces; amnesty proposal; state of siege and the press regime; parties take position for the legislative and senatorial elections; last days; **dissolution**; Hanotaux’s judgment on the National Assembly.

## Chapitre VII — Le Pays et les Élections de 1876

The French people may at last, in calm and under constitutional rules, make its will known. Dispositions of universal suffrage; the nation and the parties; electoral organization; the ministry and the elections; the Marshal’s proclamation. **Senatorial elections** and composition of the Senate. **Legislative elections**: candidates and programmes; Gambetta leads the campaign — speeches at Lille, Bordeaux, and Paris; the poll of **20 February 1876** favourable to the Republic. Buffet leaves power; Dufaure charged with the interim presidency of the council; Gambetta’s “discours-ministre” at Lyon; ballottages; composition of the Chamber of Deputies; ceremony of the transmission of powers; first sittings of Chamber and Senate.

## Chapitre VIII — Le premier cabinet Dufaure

From March 1876 France inaugurates the parliamentary regime: two Chambers coexist; the executive has constitutional authority. Both sit at Versailles — the Senate the National Assembly prolonged, the Chamber largely new men impatient of the royal town. Portrait of **Dufaure** (born 1798): character, situation, redingote and Gallican eloquence of 1830; formation of the cabinet; ministerial declaration; divisions in the majority; verification of powers; extreme Left demands amnesty; Gambetta elected president of the finance commission; Universal Exposition decreed for 1878; first republican laws. Administrative movement; death of Ricard, replaced at the Interior by de Marcère; amnesty debate; complementary elections; in the Senate, scope of the right of revision; Chamber modifies the higher-education law as to the collation of degrees; Buffet elected irremovable senator. Party excitement; mayors’ law voted in the Chamber; disagreement between Jules Ferry and Gambetta; Senate rejects the collation-of-degrees bill; conflict of the two Chambers; budget of 1877; resignation of general de Cissey, replaced at War by general Berthaut; close of the ordinary session. Vacations: partial elections; the Marshal at army manœuvres; Vatican incident; France and Italy; political speeches; Paris workers’ congress; extraordinary session of 1876; cessation of Commune prosecutions; tax reform; budget; Chesnelong irremovable senator; Senate rejects the Commune-prosecutions bill; **fall of the Dufaure ministry**.

## Chapitre IX — Le Cabinet Jules Simon et le 16 Mai

Dufaure’s resignation letter (2 December 1876): caught between Chamber and Senate, he can no longer govern; the presidency as instituted by the law of 20 November does not fit the parliamentary regime. Last efforts toward conjunction of the centres; ministerial crisis; permanence of the Chamber; constitution of the **Jules Simon ministry**; Simon and the Chambers; reserved welcome of the Left; Simon and Gambetta; financial rights of the Senate; vote of the budget of 1877. First acts; administrative personnel; return of the Chambers; passing calm; politics of the centre gauche; Gambetta re-elected president of the budget commission; tactics of the extreme Left; Simon’s difficult position, embarrassed by his past; ministerial incidents; the republican programme. The religious question: Pius IX and Jules Simon; Easter recess. Interpellation on ultramontane manœuvres; speeches of Jules Simon and Gambetta — **« Le cléricalisme, voilà l'ennemi ! »**. The Marshal decides to break with the Left majority; municipal organization law and press law; the **letter of 16 May**; resignation of the ministry; “the politics of the president.” Hanotaux treats 16 May as a foreseen, deliberated act of authority — constitutional corollary of the septennat compromise rather than a mere boutade — yet imprudent and bound to fail against the national movement that had already imposed itself on the National Assembly; the letter poses the problem for the nation to resolve.

## Series note

Tome III ends at the act of 16 May 1877. Continuity of the crisis — Broglie and Rochebouët cabinets, the elections of 14 October 1877, the second Dufaure ministry, Eastern Question and Congress of Berlin, Mac-Mahon’s resignation and Grévy’s presidency — is the matter of [Tome IV](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t4.md) (*La République Parlementaire*). The government of Thiers through **24 May 1873** is [Tome I](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t1.md); early Mac-Mahon, the monarchical failure, and the fall of Broglie (**16 May 1874**) are [Tome II](hanotaux-histoire-france-contemporaine-t2.md) (*L'Échec de la Monarchie*).
