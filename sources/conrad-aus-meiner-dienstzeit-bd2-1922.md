# Conrad von Hötzendorf — *Aus meiner Dienstzeit 1906–1918*, Band II: Die Zeit des libyschen Krieges und des Balkankrieges bis Ende 1912

Franz Graf Conrad von Hötzendorf (1852–1925), *Aus meiner Dienstzeit 1906–1918*. Zweiter Band: *Die Zeit des libyschen Krieges und des Balkankrieges bis Ende 1912*. Wien, Berlin, Leipzig, München: Rikola Verlag, 1922. The second volume of the memoirs of the chief of the Austro-Hungarian general staff carries the account from 1910 through the end of 1912, treated year by year: the receding of the annexation crisis, the army budget conflict and Conrad's dismissal in November 1911, the outbreak of the Italian war over Tripoli, his years as army inspector, the first Balkan War, and his reinstatement as chief of the general staff in December 1912. The raw text in this wiki is the scanned copy (University of California, Berkeley library; Rikola Verlag 1922), 484 pages, already OCR'd, concatenated in page order.

## 1910

### Allgemeine Lage

Conrad characterises 1910 as, on the one hand, the outward calming of the annexation crisis and the appearance of peaceful conditions, on the other hand the increasing thickening of the ring closing around Germany and Austria-Hungary, the visible war preparations of their opponents, and the demand this raised to bring one's own forces to the greatest possible height. This it was his business to promote. The diplomacy of the Central Powers was chiefly concerned with plastering over the cracks; the diplomacy of the Entente powers, and of Italy, seized upon the great moving forces and used them for their own ends. In Germany Bethmann Hollweg had on 14 July 1909 followed Prince Bülow as imperial chancellor, after the passage of the great imperial finance reform. Conrad traces the political and military position of the monarchy in this year, the normal work of the general staff, the organic development of the Wehrmacht, and his personal employment.

## 1911

### Normale Arbeiten, Ausbau der Wehrmacht, Konflikt in der Budgetfrage

The account of 1911 treats Conrad's normal work, the development of the armed power of the monarchy, and the conflict over the budget. He pressed for the creation of six army inspectors as future commanders-in-chief and for the reinforcement of the army, and came into conflict with the minister of foreign affairs and with the authorities over the means he held the exposed position of the monarchy to demand.

### Ausbruch des libyschen Krieges (Tripolis)

The action against Tripoli ripened in the summer of 1911. Conrad quotes the correspondence of the Austro-Hungarian military attaché in Rome, Lieutenant-Colonel Mietzl, on the position of the Italian minister of foreign affairs, San Giuliano, who in the debate on the foreign budget was attacked for his weakness and in particular over Tripoli, gave himself the credit that Italy had a claim to a special position there, but set right the exaggerated and invented reports which the Italian press had for months been putting into the world about Tripoli, about the treatment of the Italians by Turkey and about the penetration of the other states, in order to work up public opinion. Whether that speech answered to San Giuliano's conviction or only served to veil Italy's intentions might be left open; the signs multiplied that military preparations were in train in Italy for an action abroad, with the participation of the fleet, starting from southern Italy — whether against Albania or against Tripoli was at first doubtful.

### Der Konflikt mit Graf Ährenthal

Conrad recounts the deepening of his conflict with Baron (by then Count) Ährenthal, minister of foreign affairs. The divergences between the military requirements he pressed and the conduct of the foreign ministry led to growing friction, which he set down at length and which finally brought about his dismissal.

### Meine Entlassung von der Stelle des Chefs des Generalstabes

On the afternoon of 29 November 1911 the new wing-adjutant of the archduke-heir, Lieutenant-Colonel Dr. von Bardolff, told Conrad on the archduke's behalf that on the following day he was to appear in audience before the Kaiser, who would announce his dismissal from the post of chief of the general staff with simultaneous appointment as army inspector. Conrad replied that he had no thought of sulking, that he had done his service as chief as well as he could, and was glad to be rid of a post that had in any case been forced upon him and to come again nearer to the troops. On 30 November 1911 he appeared at Schönbrunn. The Kaiser said it grieved him, but after mature reflection he was obliged to remove Conrad from his present post and appoint him army inspector; the reasons were known to him. The Kaiser spoke appreciative words, and had the grace to say that their personal relation had become a friendly one and that he had had Conrad called to tell him of his dismissal himself, because his upright manner had won his respect.

## 1912

### Rein militärische Tätigkeit als Armee-Inspektor

Conrad divides his professional activity in 1912 into his purely military activity as army inspector and his politico-military activity in that office. As army inspector he had been assigned the command of the 3rd army in case of war. He describes the application of the army manoeuvres and instructional journeys, and his concrete proposals for the reinforcement of the forces in the light of the threatening situation.

### Ausbruch des Balkankrieges

With the outbreak of the first Balkan War in October 1912 the whole situation of the monarchy was changed. Conrad describes the military and political position, the concentration of the armies of the Balkan states against Turkey, and the considerations that the monarchy had to weigh in the face of the possible involvement of Russia.

### Meine Mission in Rumänien

The difficult situation created by the Balkan War, which forced Austria-Hungary to reckon with the possibility of warlike complications, and also with Russia, made it advisable to secure Rumania for this case. It was a question, however, of not letting the thread with Bulgaria — Rumania's jealous rival — break, that is, of smoothing out the differences between Bulgaria and Rumania and offering one's good offices for this. Count Berchtold was unremittingly concerned to shape the policy of the monarchy in this sense: to bring Rumania and Bulgaria into accord and to have Rumania actively at its side with all its forces for the eventual war against Russia. In mid-November 1912 Conrad was charged by the archduke-heir to prepare himself for a mission to Bukarest to work in this sense. As an outward pretext, he was to convey to King Carol the condolences of the Kaiser on the death of the Countess of Flanders, the king's sister. From Berchtold he received his instructions; he met the king and the leading Rumanian statesmen, and sought to secure Rumania's adherence to the monarchy.

### Meine Wiederernennung zum Chef des Generalstabes

On 6 December 1912 Conrad received through the head of the military chancellery of Archduke Franz Ferdinand the written order to present himself in audience with the archduke on 7 December. The archduke received him at the Belvedere; on the tables lay the maps of the various theatres of war with the concentration plans for the several war-cases. The archduke declared that he wished to go over all this once more with him, since the plans had remained almost wholly unchanged since Conrad's time as chief of the general staff, and that with the present situation one must be prepared for surprises. When the discussion was ended, the archduke turned to him suddenly: "So, now I must tell you something more; you must become chief of the general staff again." Conrad was astonished, hesitated, and made objections, in particular that it would not be right to remove again his successor who had been appointed barely a year before. The archduke replied that this removal would take place with all honour for the person concerned, but that he, as presumptive supreme commander of the army, must lay weight on having at the head of the general staff, should it come to war, the man from whom all the preparations for war proceeded. The Kaiser, he added, was in agreement and would receive him in audience that very day. On 12 December 1912 Conrad received the hand-written warrant of the Kaiser reinstating him as chief of the general staff, and he took up again his activity in that office.
