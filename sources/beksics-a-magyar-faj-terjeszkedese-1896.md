# Beksics — *A magyar faj terjeszkedése és nemzeti konszolidácziónk*

Gusztáv Beksics (1847–1906), *A magyar faj terjeszkedése és nemzeti konszolidácziónk, különös tekintettel a mezőgazdaságra, birtokviszonyokra és a népesedésre*. Második kiadás. Budapest: Az Athenaeum Részvénytársulat Kiadása, 1896. A Hungarian national-political and demographic treatise of some one hundred and thirty pages, drawn from a scanned edition of the second issue (page 1 carries the Barcode 3 1761 08137940 6) obtained via Anna's Archive; the raw text in this wiki was obtained by OCR of the scanned images (the PDF carries no embedded text layer; Hungarian). The book examines the means and prospects of Hungarian national and state consolidation at a moment — three decades after the Compromise of 1867 — when the linguistic assimilation of the nationalities was visibly failing and the Hungarian population's growth in the purely Hungarian counties was stagnating.

Dominant years in the text are **the 1890s**, with the three decades since 1867 as its constant point of reference.

## Preface — Előszó a második kiadáshoz

Beksics declares the book's two fundamental theses. First: linguistically we are incapable of assimilating the nationalities — even counting those, from among the peoples of Hungary other than the Germans, who have acquired the Hungarian tongue, their number dwindles to insignificance. Three decades of constitutional rule, self-determination and a cultural policy directed by the nation itself have produced, so far as linguistic absorption is concerned, very poor results; if the towns had not grown into Hungarian cultural points, one would have to say that the cultural means of national and state consolidation had failed. The language frontiers have not been pushed forward; in places there has been positive regression — at the borders, in Upper Hungary and in Transylvania, the Slovak and Romanian tongues have defeated Hungarian at the points of contact; in Árva, Trencsén and Liptó the small Magyar population is on the verge of extinction. Second: the relation of agriculture and of landed property to population growth. The dependence is not simple but complicated, crossing society and the most different regions of the country; but where industry is not yet developed enough to complicate it, the effect of agriculture on population is everywhere clearly visible. The demographer finds no more favourable field for uncovering the relation between population and agriculture than the Hungarian Dunántúl and Alföld, where industry as yet plays the least part. The generalisation of English conditions was the greatest error of Malthus's theory; the study of agriculture as the chief cause of population, though attempted by Lavergne in the sixties, remained neglected for want of data. Against Malthus, Carey, Ricardo, Wappäus and the syncretic Oettingen, Beksics maintains — on statistical grounds — that in certain circumstances agriculture and the division of land are the first-rank factor in promoting or hindering population growth. Up to a certain point of development agriculture is the most powerful multiplier of population (in Austria itself the agricultural Galicia and Bukovina multiply faster than the industrial Czech provinces); beyond a certain density industry takes over the leading role. When the land needed for expansion is wanting, population growth stops; and here the difference between races is decisive — the same economic conditions see different races expand in different proportions, a racial propagative force that science and practice alike have unduly neglected. The preface also notes that, contrary to the Western view that Catholics multiply less than Protestants, in Hungary the Catholics show almost the greatest growth.

## I. Fejezet — Nemzeti konszolidácziónk és eszközei (National consolidation and its means)

The national policy — if it is not mere phrase — must be founded on the recognition that consolidation cannot be achieved through language alone. The examination of what national consolidation requires and of the true means by which the Hungarian nation can consolidate itself and expand within the lands of the Crown of St. Stephen.

## II. Fejezet — A magyar faj terjeszkedésének bizonyítékai (The proofs of the expansion of the Hungarian race)

The great rate of expansion of the Hungarian race is made indubitable by the older and the newer data alike. From the Turkish conquest onward the proofs of Hungarian expansion are marshalled — where the Magyar has advanced and at what rate, and the regions in which the race has shown its greatest force.

## III. Fejezet — Aggasztó jelenségek a magyar faj terjeszkedésében (Alarming phenomena in the expansion of the Hungarian race)

In the very twenty-five counties that display the tremendous force of the Hungarian race and stamp their character on the country — the phenomenon that made Hungary one of the most multiplying lands of Europe — the number of births is now falling, or with few exceptions stagnating. This is not the effect of the agricultural crisis; low grain prices have no influence upon it. The chapter weighs these alarming symptoms against the record of expansion.

## IV. Fejezet — Mezőgazdaság és nemzeti politika (Agriculture and national policy)

Agriculture as an instrument of national policy. The cultivation of the soil and the conditions of the countryside as a means of strengthening and expanding the Hungarian element against the nationalities.

## V. Fejezet — Népszaporodás és agrikultura (Population growth and agriculture)

What effect has agriculture upon population growth? The relation is examined statistically — how the agricultural character of a region governs the multiplication of its inhabitants, and the stages at which agriculture gives way to industry as the chief agent of growth.

## VI. Fejezet — Népszaporodás és földbirtok (Population growth and landed property)

The relation of population growth to the ownership of the land — how the distribution and tenure of landed property promote or hinder the multiplication of the race, and the demographic consequence of the great estate.

## VII. Fejezet — Az alföldi agrár-szoczializmus (The agrarian socialism of the Alföld)

Twice in Sicily the agrarian revolt broke out in arms — first in September 1866, when the insurgents held Palermo for seven and a half days; again at the end of 1893 and the beginning of 1894, when a whole army had to be mobilised against the rebels. Both outbreaks took the Italian official circles and public opinion by surprise, and a whole literature grew up about the woes of the Sicilian peasant. Beksics turns from the Sicilian to the Hungarian Alföld: the agrarian socialism that has stirred the great plain of Hungary, its causes in the land-hunger of the peasantry, and the danger it poses to the national and state order.

## VIII. Fejezet — A magyar parasztság (The Hungarian peasantry)

The condition of the Hungarian peasantry — the survivors of the Hungarian race upon the soil, their relation to the land they till, the history of their emancipation, and the forces that make them conservative holders or restless aspirants after land.

## IX. Fejezet — Bérlet vagy tulajdon (Tenancy or property)

The alternative between tenancy and ownership as the basis of the peasant economy and of Hungarian expansion: whether the peasant should hold the land he works as a tenant or as a proprietor, and which arrangement best serves both the multiplication of the race and the national consolidation of the country.

## X. Fejezet — A népszaporodás gátjainak megszüntetése (The removal of the obstacles to population growth)

Before passing to the remedies for the causes that hinder the multiplication of our race, Beksics briefly recapitulates what has been said, then sets out the means by which the obstacles to population growth are to be removed — chiefly through the division of the great estates and the creation of a numerous landed peasantry.

## XI. Fejezet — Az uj földbirtokviszonyok és az uj Magyarország (The new land-tenure relations and the new Hungary)

The connection between the new distribution of the land and the regeneration of the nation. Beside agriculture the population can no longer multiply as it should; the chapter draws the national consequences of the new land-tenure relations and the new Hungary that they are to call into being.

## Befejezés (Conclusion)

To what has been said Beksics has little to add. He declines to repeat himself, but closes by pointing once more to the fundamental considerations. The future of the Hungarian nation, its state and national consolidation, can only be assured if the forces latent in the race unfold freely and every obstacle is removed from before the expansiveness of the Magyars; otherwise the nation will be compelled to lower its national ideal and the level of its existence. In the expansion of the Hungarian race alarming phenomena appear. In the twenty-five counties that show the tremendous force of the race the number of births is falling, or with few exceptions stagnates. This phenomenon is not the effect of the agricultural crisis; low grain prices have no influence upon it; the data adduced are, he believes, completely convincing. As applied to Hungarian conditions, the Malthusian doctrine — that the proletariat must be allowed to starve, that relief only multiplies the poor — proves erroneous; the land-hunger of the peasant and the division of the great estates, far from increasing a menace, are the very means by which the race multiplies and the national consolidation is secured.

## Series note

This volume sits within the 1866–1919 period covered by this wiki, at the point where the Austrian–Hungarian thread turns from the constitutional settlement (the Compromise of 1867) to the national and demographic question of the Hungarian kingdom in the last decade of the century. Written by a Hungarian publicist in the 1890s, it is a contemporary Hungarian account of the nationality and land question — the internal counterpart to the constitutional theme of Beksics's own *A dualismus* (1892) in the same wiki. Chronologically it precedes the works of Theodor von Sosnosky on the politics of the Habsburg realms.
