Ollivier — L’Empire libéral, Tome XIV (Livre XXII)

Émile Ollivier, L’Empire libéral; études, récits, souvenirs, tome quatorzième. Paris: Garnier frères, 6, rue des Saints-Pères, 1909. On the Internet Archive as lempireliberal14olli (series dating in metadata from 1895; this volume’s imprint is 1909). The half-title band names the subject La Guerre: explosion du complot Hohenzollern; déclaration du 6 juillet; retrait de la candidature Hohenzollern; demande de garantie; soufflet de Bismarck; réponse au soufflet; déclaration de guerre. The body opens Livre XXII (the table of contents OCR mislabels it “Livre XIV”).

The volume is Ollivier’s continuous memoir-history of the July 1870 crisis in which he served as head of the liberal ministry. It runs from the premature revelation of Leopold of Hohenzollern’s acceptance of the Spanish crown through the French Chambers’ vote of war credits. An avant-propos frames the whole as a defence against the postwar charge that he went to war “au cœur léger.” The narrative is organised as five “moments,” then a conclusion and a block of éclaircissements.

Livre XXII — La Guerre

Avant-propos

Ollivier opens with a July 1870 café telegram that once paired his name with Cavour and Bismarck, then with the reverse judgment after defeat: a mediocre talker, “au cœur léger,” whose presumptuous ineptitude lost two provinces. He cites Machiavelli and Guicciardini on judgment by outcome rather than by the wisdom of counsel, and sets the volume as justification of the ministry’s conduct in the Hohenzollern affair and the decision for war.

Chapitre premier — Le guet-apens Hohenzollern éclate — son effet en France et en Europe

Salazar reaches Madrid with Leopold’s acceptance (telegraphed 26 June); Prim is away hunting; the news leaks through Zorrilla to la Época and the Puerta del Sol before the Cortes can be packed for an immediate vote. Prim returns in the night of 30 June–1 July and already judges the candidacy compromised: the plan with the King and Bismarck had been secrecy until a Cortes vote that would stun the Emperor. Prim tries to recover by charming Mercier; Mercier’s language remains correct. A letter of Prim to Olózaga. The news bursts on Paris like a bomb (3 July); Mercier’s dispatch; Gramont informs the Emperor; the Emperor’s surprise; Gramont’s letter to Ollivier; Ollivier’s despair; the government resolves to turn aside the candidacy. Prim answers French protests by summoning the Cortes for 20 July to elect Leopold; at Berlin, Thile tells Lesourd the Prussian government knows nothing and evades; Bismarck organises a press campaign. Unanimous French indignation — Doudan, Jules Favre, Jules Simon, Thiers, Gambetta — mirrored among foreign statesmen. The French press; this state of opinion does not discharge the Emperor’s responsibility alone.

Chapitre II — Les principes du droit international — le cabinet décide de les appliquer à la candidature

After 1815 each great power had bound itself not to seize exclusive influence by placing a family member on a vacant throne without European consent; examples; protest is addressed to the reigning family, not to the electing nation. The rule weighs still more on Spain and on Leopold, given that prince’s dynastic Prussianism. France therefore had reason to stir. The French cabinet resolves to confine the quarrel to the Hohenzollern candidacy and to set aside older grievances; German allegations of personal motives in the Emperor and especially the Empress are denied.

Chapitre III — Premier moment : déclaration ministérielle du 6 juillet

The aggression being plain, the ministry forgoes immediate military reprisals and tries to undo the trap by diplomacy. Conference and direct negotiation with Prussia both look impossible before Prim’s 20 July election date; the cabinet decides to speak from the tribune. Cochery, on Thiers’s advice, asks to interpellate (5 July); the government accepts. Le Bœuf is asked whether the army is ready and affirms that, acting without delay, France can win. Alliances: the Emperor holds out hope of Austria and Italy. Drafting of the declaration; Ollivier corrects it; reading in the Chamber (6 July); the Left pretends to hear a declaration of war; Ollivier restores the true sense. Universal enthusiasm in the French and English press. The ministry redoubles efforts for peace against Prim’s manœuvres; the Emperor approaches Serrano; Gramont approaches the powers; Olózaga approaches the Hohenzollern princes. In the Chamber the Left hampers pacific action; in the Senate warlike excitement; Parisian effervescence; bellicose language in le Pays, la Liberté, le Moniteur universel.

Chapitre IV — Suite du premier moment — Négociations avec les puissances et avec Serrano

Russia (Gorchakov) shows little eagerness and even malice — “France needed a lesson” — though the Tsar writes moderating counsel to King William. Beust pays in words; Italy is circumspect; the South German states unfriendly; the English cabinet withholds help it might have given. Demarches at Berlin and Madrid fail; the Emperor’s personal negotiation with Serrano yields a result: Serrano sends his nephew López Domínguez to the Hohenzollerns.

Chapitre V — Suite du premier moment — La négociation à Ems

Thile’s attitude forces the sending of Benedetti to negotiate at Ems; portrait of Benedetti; the ministry refuses to make him and Mercier scapegoats for not having pierced the plot in March 1870. Bismarck sees with anger the King’s reception of Benedetti. The King will not order Leopold’s renunciation but tries to obtain it by hint; French instructions all ask the same thing. Interviews of Benedetti and the King. The King’s delays look like duplicity; the Emperor takes military measures with Le Bœuf. Thiers’s demarche through the marquis de Massa and the duchess de Mouchy offering his help to the Emperor (10 July) is not received as it should have been. The King continues to let Benedetti hope for withdrawal without ordering it. In the Chamber Emmanuel Arago allows that the 6 July declaration was not a declaration of war; the Right grows more bellicose; Benedetti handles Ems with flexibility; the King commits himself officially by sending Werther to Paris.

Chapitre VI — Second moment — La renonciation — 12 juillet

Strat, Olózaga’s envoy, goes to Sigmaringen; Prince Anthony renounces for his son; Leopold resists; Strat has Anthony send three telegrams (to Prim, to Olózaga, to the Berlin papers) announcing renunciation (11 July). Prim’s envoy, come to offer the crown officially, still demands Leopold’s own official withdrawal; violent scenes between father and son; Leopold submits and hands his renunciation to the admiral. Bismarck’s wrath against the authors of the renunciation; his despair; he offers the King his resignation; the King persists in accepting Leopold’s withdrawal and closing the affair.

Chapitre VII — La demande de garanties — 12 juillet

The Emperor learns of the withdrawal during the morning council and conceals it from the ministers; Ollivier hears it on the way to the Chamber; Olózaga confirms; Ollivier tells the deputies and rejoices. The war party is discontented; Clément Duvernois asks to interpellate on guarantees for the future. The Emperor would have Ollivier present the renunciation as the King of Prussia’s work; Ollivier prefers to wait; the Emperor admits the King had no part in the withdrawal and, still for peace, promises nothing will be done before the next day’s council. Gramont receives Anthony’s telegram through Olózaga, is struck by its gaps, and in the interview with Werther tries to draw an admission that the King contributed; Werther denies energetically; the supposed letter of apology demanded of the King. At Saint-Cloud the Court is over-excited at the insufficiency of the renunciation; the Emperor forgets his promise, decides with Gramont alone (no other minister present) to send a demand of guarantees, dispatched about seven in the evening — the work of the Right, lacking serious grounds. Ollivier learns of the demand by chance; the Emperor orders Gramont to harden it; Ollivier advises the reverse — attenuate and confine it to the present. Ollivier is violently tempted to resign; he stays in order not to harm the country and to neutralise the demand’s effects.

Chapitre VIII — Quatrième moment — Le soufflet de Bismarck

Benedetti receives the order to ask guarantees; he disapproves but fails to send his objections and presses the King on the promenade (13 July); the King receives him ill yet remains courteous. In Paris a pacific Constitutionnel article affirms Ollivier’s pacific resolutions; Le Bœuf, furious at the demand of guarantees, wants the reserves called out — immediate war; Ollivier, backed by seven colleagues, has that rejected and decides that rejection of the guarantees by the King will not be a casus belli, to be read the same day in the Chamber. Violent scene with Le Bœuf; impolite attitude of the Empress; Gambetta to Mitchell: “Votre satisfaction est scélérate”; the Chamber receives the declaration with discontent; Thiers confers with Mège, Richard, Louvet, Segris, Chevandier and fears above all a general conflagration; bellicose Senate. Bismarck summons the King to receive Benedetti no more; the King is already so disposed; Werther’s report is not the cause; Abeken’s telegram to Bismarck; the King has Radziwill draw two reports; Abeken’s telegram poisons the narrative and authorises Bismarck’s perfidious disclosure. Bismarck seeks in vain in Werther’s report and in the French declaration of the 13th a pretext for war; he reworks Abeken’s dispatch until the falsified telegram becomes an unbearable outrage for France — a soufflet (13 July). He has it posted and published; all Germany treats war as inevitable. The French ministry still works for peace; Cassagnac, l’Univers, the Gazette de France, Girardin, Albuféra push war; Gramont continues to ask Benedetti for guarantees; the powers disapprove. Propitious news from Spain; Ollivier with Mitchell declares the incident finished; the Right organises a coalition with the Left — Gambetta and Clément Duvernois at its soul. On the morning of the 14th Ollivier believes peace sure; Gramont announces Bismarck’s soufflet; council at the Tuileries at half-past noon; the ministers still seek a pacific issue and decide on an appeal to a congress. The appeal is reprobated; council at Saint-Cloud (four ministers absent); no final decision, but war is recognised as inevitable after dispatches from Berne and Munich showing Germany is informing foreign powers of the King’s refusal to receive Benedetti; the congress appeal is dropped. Lyons foresees that France cannot bear the Prussian outrage; spontaneous violent manifestations of opinion; Benedetti offers himself to a further impertinence by seeking reception at the Ems station.

Chapitre IX — Cinquième moment — Notre réponse au soufflet de Bismarck — Déclaration du 15 juillet

Friday 15 July, nine in the morning: council at Saint-Cloud with the Empress present; all ministers free; no irrevocable public act yet done. Gramont reads the draft declaration framed so that the decisive grievance is only the public outrage of the posted Ems telegram — not Prague, not Luxembourg, not the refusal of future guarantees as such. The Emperor and all the ministers without exception decide for war. Despite the Constitution they will not declare war without the Chambers’ assent. Meeting with Benedetti. Ollivier reads the declaration in the Chamber of Deputies; general acclamations except sixteen deputies of the Left. Thiers’s speech: right not to tolerate the Hohenzollern candidacy, wrong to make war on a futile circumstance. Ollivier’s reply — le cœur léger. The Chamber, all bellicose, hears further explanations impatiently. Thiers speaks again: justly attacks the demand of guarantees, repeats that this occasion to avenge Sadowa is not propitious. Refutation; Gramont and Kératry; Buffet and J. Favre demand communication of the agents’ dispatches; a commission is named. Ollivier is absent when Gramont communicates dispatches to the commission; Gramont’s very innocent error on one dispatch and the calumnious use made of it. Guyot-Montpayroux for war; Gambetta’s equivocal speech still demanding public communication of dispatches; diplomatic impossibility of that; vote of the war credits — the Chamber was mistress to prevent war by refusing them; an imposing majority decides it. Gramont’s declaration in the Senate meets only acclamations; the Senate’s deputation to Saint-Cloud organised by Rouher; Rouher’s compromising speech. Explanation of the opposition’s attitude; Thiers had warned no minister that he thought the army unready. Gambetta, in reality bellicose, dared not separate from his party; the people of Paris and the provinces show themselves ardent patriots. The government notifies the declaration of war to Prussia expecting immediate entry into campaign. The King of Prussia re-enters Berlin amid warlike ovations; Lesourd’s conversation with Bismarck; Bismarck’s Reichstag speech of 19 July, his lies, the Reichstag’s patriotic unanimity.

Chapitre X — Conclusion et résumé

Historians who make Bismarck innocent of the war diminish him; those who restore his premeditation enlarge him; Bismarck’s own admissions confirm the latter. Ottokar Lorenz, Delbrück, Scherr, Schultze and serious historians generally lay responsibility on Bismarck. The German thesis that if Bismarck wanted war it was because France was herself preparing it is empty; Prussia alone needed the war to make her unity. Bismarck alone organised the Hohenzollern candidacy and falsified the Ems dispatch, without which there would have been no war. Summary of the French government’s conduct — correct and loyal against the Prussian government’s provocative disloyalty; impossibility for the French government of bearing the Prussian outrage. What would have followed had France borne it. Justification of the war; Ollivier acted with knowledge and solely in his country’s interest.
