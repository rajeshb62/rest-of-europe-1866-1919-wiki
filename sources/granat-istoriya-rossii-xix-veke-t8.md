# Granat — History of Russia in the 19th Century, Vol. 8

**История России в XIX веке.** — St. Petersburg: Partnership of the Brothers A. and I. Granat & Co., 1907–1911. — 9 volumes. **Vol. VIII. Part 4. End of the Century [1892–1900]. Section One.** — 1910. — [5], 262 pp.; 15 plates.

A collective multi-volume course issued by the firm of the brothers A. and I. Granat. Vol. VIII opens the fourth part of the series (“End of the Century”) and constitutes the first section of that part. The series frame on the title page gives 1892–1900; the essays in the volume in fact carry the narrative **to 1903** (the eve of the Far Eastern crisis and the Russo-Japanese War). The volume fills the gap after [Kornilov, Part 3](kornilov-kurs-istorii-rossii-ch3.md) (the reactionary period to the famine of 1891–92) and provides a systematic survey of the “end of the century”: state economy in the Witte era, industrial boom and the labour movement of the 1890s–early 1900s, and public medicine in connection with the living conditions of the people.

**Composition of the volume (three principal essays):**

| Author | Essay | Scope |
|--------|--------|--------|
| M. I. Bogolepov | State economy (1892–1903) | finances, taxes, monopolies, railways, gold currency |
| L. Martov | Development of industry and the labour movement from 1893 to 1903 | boom 1894–99; crisis 1899–1903; strikes; Zubatovism |
| V. Ya. Kanel | Public medicine in connection with the living conditions of the people | prikaz and zemstvo medicine; sanitation; factory medicine |

**Character of the edition.** A collection of essays by different authors, not a single-author course. Bogolepov writes as a financier-critic of the “brilliant period” of state economy; Martov as a Social Democrat, with detailed strike statistics and analysis of government policy on the labour question; Kanel as a public-health physician linking medicine to economy and everyday life. Pre-reform orthography. OCR quality — medium (Ѣ/Е, І/И confusion, “XX” for “XIX”, distorted surnames and years, e.g. “Боголфповъ”, “1892—9083” / “до 1908” for 1903 in the table of contents).

**Raw volume:** ~1.35 MB, 27,347 lines. Internet Archive: `istorijarossiivxixvekespb19071911t20` (the suffix `t20` in the identifier is **not** the volume number; the text itself says “Vol. VIII”).

## Chapter I. M. I. Bogolepov — State Economy (1892–1903)

The essay opens with the famine of 1891–92 as the starting point and is carried to the threshold of the Russo-Japanese War. The author examines the Treasury’s “free cash,” budget growth, the policy of Vyshnegradsky and especially **S. Yu. Witte**, railway construction and redemption, the spirits monopoly, indirect taxes and excises, customs protectionism, the sugar industry, credit, and the introduction of the **gold currency**. The running thesis: the “brilliant period” of Russian state finances was achieved at the cost of extreme strain on taxable capacity, irregularities of state economy, and the ruin of the propertyless; by the beginning of the 20th century the country inherited a huge debt, a loss-making state economy, an overstrained tax press, landowner agriculture amid a hungry peasantry, and factory production working for the Treasury and for an exhausted internal market. The conclusion directly links this legacy to the Russo-Japanese War and the revolution.

### Thematic lines of the essay

- **Aftermath of 1891–92 and “free cash.”** The famine and its fiscal consequences; accumulation of Treasury cash alongside growth of state debt and expenditure (including the non-military budget).
- **Witte and predecessors.** Transition from Bunge and Vyshnegradsky to the Witte system; intensified railway construction, breakdown of the natural economy, the redemption operation, and the money economy.
- **Indirect taxation and monopolies.** Excises (spirits, tobacco, sugar, matches, etc.); introduction of the **spirits monopoly**; raising of customs duties (including on cotton); the sugar industry as an example of state-protectionist policy.
- **Railways, credit, gold currency.** The railway business and its financial return; credit as a means of mobilization; gold currency — “costing the country” heavy sacrifices in the author’s account.
- **Taxable capacity, mutual responsibility, land captains.** Strain on taxable capacity; link of the fiscal system to mutual responsibility (*krugovaya poruka*) and the administrative-peasant regime; critique of unaccountability of power in state economy.
- **Result of the “brilliant period.”** Separated from “current contemporaneity” by the Russo-Japanese War and the revolution; heavy legacy — debt, loss-making state economy, tax overstrain, agrarian and industrial weakness of the internal market.

## Chapter II. L. Martov — Development of Industry and the Labour Movement from 1893 to 1903

Martov’s essay is the largest in the volume. It is built as a connected narrative of the industrial cycle of the 1890s–early 1900s and the parallel growth of the mass labour movement, with abundant strike statistics, geography of struggle (Petersburg, Moscow, Ivanovo-Voznesensk, Łódź, Baku, the Donbass, the South, Poland, the Baltic), and analysis of government tactics (factory inspection, Witte’s circulars, repression, Zubatov experiments, laws of 1886, 1897, and 1903). Three clear subdivisions:

### 1. Culminating industrial boom and flowering of the mass labour movement (1894–1899)

From the mid-1890s — “unprecedented industrial animation,” coordinated with the world boom. Growth of the strike movement; distinction of offensive and defensive strikes and their outcomes (on the author’s data, success of full/partial concessions is higher in defensive struggle; strikes over fines and administrative actions more often end in defeat for the workers). Economic character of mass struggle in the boom years; attitude of Social Democracy to political and purely professional tasks; in Poland and Lithuania — greater receptivity of the masses to political agitation. Mention of Witte’s 1895 line on maintaining “patriarchal” relations between masters and workers as an attempt to preserve government tutelage over the bourgeoisie. Toward the end of the subsection — accumulation of discontent while predominantly economic demands remain in the “immediate” struggle of the masses.

### 2. Economic crisis of the last years of the 19th century and the turning point in the labour movement

Autumn 1899 — beginning of the industrial crisis (harbinger — the stock-exchange crisis of preceding years). Fall in iron prices; closures and cutbacks in Łódź, Białystok, Ivanovo-Voznesensk, Nizhny Novgorod, Sormovo, Petersburg (Putilov, Obukhov), the Donets basin, Baku, Mariupol, Tula, and elsewhere; lengthening of the working day with falling piece rates; unemployment 1900–1901 (oil crisis in Baku 1901, etc.). The “protracted crisis of 1899–1903” is interrupted by local revivals and complicated by the agrarian crisis and village famines throwing labour onto the urban market. Against the background of crisis — politicization of the movement: demonstrations, collisions with police and troops, growth in the use of firearms against workers (1901–1903), corporal punishment of strikers, shift from administrative exile to the courts. The Rostov strike and southern strikes of 1902–1903; summer 1903 — wave of strikes (Petersburg, Kostroma, Nikolaev, Vyshny Volochyok, etc.); general strikes in the South (including Baku). The September strike marks, for the author, the end of the “Zubatov” period; with the appearance of the Far Eastern crisis, Russian public life and the labour movement enter a new phase — beyond the 19th century.

### 3. Government and the labour question in the early 1900s — Attempts at legal organization of workers

The emergence of the working class onto the arena of political struggle forces the authorities to combine hardened repression with experiments in “legal” organization. Zubatov and related experiments in Moscow (mutual-aid funds, professors’ “talks,” the “Council of Workers of Mechanical Production of the City of Moscow”), Odessa (the “independents,” Shaevich’s metalworkers’ union), and elsewhere; suspicion of Social Democracy and the radical intelligentsia toward police tutelage. Factory legislation as meagre “social reforms” of absolutism under pressure of the masses: laws of **1886, 1897, and 1903** (including the 1903 law on compensation for accidents — with reservations limiting liability, “gross carelessness” of the worker, calculation of “actual earnings,” etc.). Conclusion: reformist attempts within the autocracy break against the political interest of the regime, above which it cannot rise even for the sake of self-preservation.

## Chapter III. V. Ya. Kanel — Public Medicine in Connection with the Living Conditions of the People

The essay opens with a numbered introductory section and then proceeds in continuous prose (without subheadings as clear as Martov’s), covering pre-reform (“prikaz”) medicine, zemstvo medicine, sanitary condition of town and village, epidemics, and, at the end, factory medicine.

### 1. Care of public health before the introduction of the zemstvo

Before the zemstvo institutions, public healthcare was distributed among the boards of public charity (*prikazy*), committees of public health, medical boards, the medical council, and the departments of state domains and appanages. Formalism, clerical reporting, and “paper” medicine predominate: on paper — hospitals, doctors, feldsher, and struggle with epidemics; in fact — lack of funds, embezzlement, unsuitable premises. The *prikazy* (1775–1865) opened hundreds of establishments (hospitals, asylums, almshouses, feldsher schools), but they bear the stamp of fictitiousness. Committees of public health (from 1845, expanded 1852) are composed of local official notables. Rural aid to state and appanage peasants — on the departments with negligible budgets.

### Zemstvo medicine and sanitation

With the introduction of the zemstvo — collision of medical demands with the estate zemstvo, stingy with medicine; role of congresses of zemstvo physicians; critique of Prince Vasilchikov’s view (*On Self-Government*) of the doctor as a narrow specialist without right to general direction. Zemstvo medical organizations early link morbidity and mortality with economic, legal, and social conditions and lay the foundation of public sanitation (prevention, study of epidemics, enlightenment). Themes of the essay: the district system; feldsherism; sanitary congresses; struggle with cholera (1892–1893 and the Pirogov congresses); nutrition and “famine bread”; medical-food points for migrant workers; housing conditions of the peasantry; comparison of Russian mortality with Western European (general and infant mortality; references to Erisman, Oesterlen, and others).

### City and factory medicine

Urban self-government, in the author’s view, copes with sanitation even more weakly than the zemstvo: most cities have no permanent sanitary organization; work livens only during epidemics. Dwellings of the poorest layers of the capitals (Petersburg, Moscow) as foci of infection. **Factory medicine** — a “fragment” of the general order: interests of entrepreneurs and workers collide; the authorities in practice defend the strong. Factory medicine and labour protection arise under external pressure (labour unrest at the end of the century, epidemics); attempts at improvement are weak because of manufacturers’ resistance. Examined are the law of 1866 on hospitals at factories and its erosion by obligatory regulations of the boards (replacement of hospitals by outpatient clinics, patchwork of norms); projects of the early 1900s (including a memorandum on the 1905 draft) and the 1903 law on compensation for the maimed. Comparative tables of fatal industrial accidents show Russia ahead of Western countries. Conclusion: the 19th century leaves the 20th a heavy sanitary legacy; the health of the people is the “question of the day” against physical and spiritual degeneration of the masses.
