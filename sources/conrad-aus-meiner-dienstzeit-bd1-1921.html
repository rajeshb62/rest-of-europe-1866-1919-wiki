Conrad von Hötzendorf — Aus meiner Dienstzeit 1906–1918, Band I: Die Zeit der Annexionskrise 1906–1909

Franz Graf Conrad von Hötzendorf (1852–1925), Aus meiner Dienstzeit 1906–1918. Erster Band: Die Zeit der Annexionskrise 1906–1909. In zwei Teilen, mit einem Anhang und einer ethnographischen Karte. Wien, Berlin, Leipzig, München: Rikola Verlag, 1921. The memoirs of the chief of the Austro-Hungarian general staff from his appointment in 1906 to his relief from office in 1918. This first volume, published in 1921, covers the period from autumn 1906 to the end of 1909, the years of the annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina and the crisis that followed; the raw text in this wiki is the scanned copy (University of Michigan library; Rikola Verlag 1921, oclc 656540), 694 pages, already OCR’d, concatenated in page order.

Einführung

Conrad explains the origin and character of the work. He recalls that on his appointment as chief of the general staff in 1906 he answered a comrade’s congratulations with the remark that whoever attains an exposed official position is first congratulated, then observed, then criticised, then abused, and finally pelted with stones — stages through which he has since passed. He had never kept a diary; only where official need required a precise dating of events did he employ his wing-adjutants as trustworthy confidants to take down what passed at the more important conferences, dictating to them the essentials afterwards where their presence was excluded. After his removal from office in July 1918 he had closed his military career and withdrawn into private life, at first with no thought of writing memoirs; the collapse of the old fatherland and its glorious army, and the many publications and requests that reached him, moved him at last to set down his experiences. The work bears, as its title says, not the character of a general historical work but of purely personal records. He sets out his philosophical conviction — that not individual men make their time, but that the time makes its men; that the men who stand by chance at the head of great epochs act by obeying the impulses dictated by the great drift of the age, and are commonly identified with successes and failures that are in truth caused only by the great course of historical development. States and peoples, like trees in a forest, germinate, strike root, grow and spread, and when their lifetime runs out they break down to make room for new formations.

Erster Teil

1. Allgemeine Lage Österreich-Ungarns

Conrad opens with the view that governed his whole activity in peacetime: his whole work as chief of the general staff was shot through with conflicts arising from the fact that his fundamental views on policy and its conduct stood in contradiction to those of the decisive personalities. He saw the monarchy threatened by unmistakable dangers to its existence; his opponents in opinion either did not estimate these at their full magnitude or believed they could escape them by temporising, whereas he was convinced they could be met only by preventive action. The dangers were the disintegrating national movements within, the aggressive tendencies of Serbia, Russia and Italy, and the indirect dangers flowing from Austria-Hungary’s alliance with Germany. In these he saw the determining factors for the conduct of internal and external policy, and the necessity of reckoning with war as their means. He gives a sketch of the complicated internal structure of the monarchy for the foreign reader, and of the deliberate misleading by which Austria-Hungary’s hostile agitators deceived foreign opinion.

2. Die Berufung zum Chef des Generalstabes

Conrad recounts his path to the highest military post. In spring 1899 he was colonel and regimental commander in Austrian Silesia; on 9 April 1899 he was appointed brigadier at Trieste, promoted major-general three weeks later, and soon, as the senior general there, took over the military station command when the senior admiral left the post of sea-district commander. The position imposed on him political as well as purely military duties. From Trieste he gained an early picture of the situation whose essential conclusion was that it was a utopia to count on Italy as an ally; one had rather to be prepared to be deserted by Italy, and in all likelihood attacked, as soon as a favourable opportunity offered. He traces the development of his views and the circumstances of his summons to the office of chief of the general staff.

3. Die Vorgeschichte der Annexion

Conrad turns to his official activity as chief of the general staff. The first and most important thing on entering office was to establish accord with the minister of foreign affairs, Baron Ährenthal, because he considered the intimate connection between external policy and the work of war preparation incumbent on the chief of the general staff the essential thing. He wrote to Ährenthal a courteous letter setting out this view and asking his support in common working; the reply was a formally correct and obliging letter. But in time many divergences of opinion led to it that the accord he held so indispensable was not reached on all questions. He sets out the considerations he urged — for a state, as the example of Spain and Italy showed, could restrict its war preparations to its few limited war-cases, whereas the monarchy, with its manifold frontiers and its exposed position, was differently placed — and the memorial of 30 December 1907 which he laid before the Kaiser.

4. Die Entwicklung der Annexionskrise

For the development of the annexation crisis the conditions in the South Slav lands and on the Balkans were chiefly determining. Croatia was united with Hungary by the Ausgleich of 1867/68; at its head stood a banus, but the Croatian questions were represented and decided in Budapest, and the banus was named by the emperor only with Hungary’s consent. The aim of the leading Hungarian politicians was to restrict Croatia’s already narrowly measured special rights and gradually to extend the sphere of power of the Hungarian state over Croatia and Slavonia, and also over Dalmatia, Bosnia and the Herzegovina, for which they invoked long-faded historical rights. All this met resistance not only from the Croats but from the remaining South Slavs, all the more as the Hungarian government provoked it by administrative measures — Magyar speech on the Croatian railways, exploitation of the forests, hindrance of railway building favourable to Croatia, one-sided finance whereby some fifty million crowns a year were said to flow from Croatia to Hungary, the Magyarising of Fiume, school policy and the like. Conrad describes the party struggles within Croatia, the pure Croatian Starčević party against the Hungarian-oriented National party and against the Serbs, and the whole process by which the annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina came to be proclaimed.

5. Der Verlauf der Annexionskrise

Conrad narrates the course of the crisis through its phases and the friction it produced, not shunning apparently irrelevant detail, for the picture must be clear. He quotes the telegrams that came in on 6 October 1908, when the extra sheet of the Belgrade Politika announced that the annexation of Bosnia was to be proclaimed, and the Serbian foreign ministry demanded immediate mobilisation and war to the death against the monarchy. That mood, he notes, was not first called forth by the annexation question; it was the permanent keynote, breaking out spontaneously at the least occasion. He recounts the diplomatic and military measures, the attitude of Russia and of the other powers, the war preparations for 1909, and the receding of the crisis as the monarchy asserted itself.

Zweiter Teil

Politischer Abschnitt

The political section of the second part treats the political side of the period, the relations with the ministry of foreign affairs, the party conditions and the intrigues, and the political frictions of Conrad’s tenure as they bore upon the army and upon the conduct of the monarchy.

Militärischer Abschnitt: Einführung, I. Entwicklung der Wehrmacht

The military section treats the development of the armed power of the monarchy during Conrad’s years of office. The introduction is followed by an account of the organic development of the Wehrmacht and the system of defence, of training, manoeuvres and regulations, of the spirit of the army, of personal matters, of the general staff, and of the war navy; of concrete preparations for war; of fortifications; of the means of communication; and of the intelligence service. Conrad dwells on the inadequacy of the means granted the army and on his unceasing labour to raise it to the level the exposed position of the monarchy demanded.

Schlußwort

Herewith, Conrad concludes, closes the account of the events in the time from autumn 1906 to the end of 1909. For the following period down to the outbreak of the world war the occurrences that concern him are to be related year by year and within each year in their chronological succession, so that the reader may have the picture of the gradual development. He welcomes it if the reader has gained the impression that many statements in these pages repeat themselves wearily; that impression may make clear over how long periods the execution even of the most insignificant measures was drawn out, and how many of these measures, despite unceasing admonition, remained unfulfilled.

Anhang (Anlage 1 bis 42)

The appendix contains forty-two documents from the two parts: the studies on South Tyrol and on the war preparations (Studien I–IV), the memorial of 6 April 1907 with its additions, the proceedings of the Skupština at Sarajevo in November 1907, the conferences with Baron Ährenthal in November and December 1907, the memorial of 31 December 1907, the exchange of letters with the archduke-heir Franz Ferdinand and with Baron Ährenthal through 1908 and 1909, the audience of 16 December 1907, the conversation with King Carol I of Rumania at Sinaia on 22 June 1908, the correspondence with General von Moltke, chief of the German general staff, the bases of the concrete war preparations for 1909, the memorial of 6 March 1909 on the war-case against Serbia, the data for orientation on Austria-Hungary’s Wehrmacht in 1906, and the revelations from the Serbian side.
