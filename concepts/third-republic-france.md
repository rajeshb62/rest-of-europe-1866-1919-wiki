# Third Republic, France (1870–1940)

The Third Republic was the French regime proclaimed on 4 September 1870 after the fall of the Second Empire and the disaster of Sedan, finally consolidated by the Constitution of 1875, and lasting until the defeat of 1940. Its founding was a twelve-year "heroic age" — in the phrase Hanotaux attributes to Gambetta — spanning the German war, the Commune, the failure of the monarchical restoration, the Constitution of 1875, the *Seize Mai* crisis, and the establishment of the parliamentary Republic. This corpus documents that founding through the four volumes of Hanotaux's *Histoire de la France contemporaine*, and its later meaning through the essays of Péguy and Sabatier.

## From the fall of the Empire to the government of Thiers

The Republic was born of defeat and civil war. With the Emperor captured at Sedan, the revolution of 4 September 1870 proclaimed the Republic in Paris, but the war continued until the armistice and the elections of 8 February 1871, dominated by the great question of war or peace. The National Assembly, meeting at Bordeaux, turned to Adolphe Thiers — elected in twenty-six departments with nearly two million votes as, in Hanotaux's phrase, "administrator of the national misfortune" — and named him chief of the executive power. Under Thiers the Republic made the peace, signed the Treaty of Frankfurt, crushed the Commune, and began the work of paying the indemnity and liberating the territory.

Thiers governed under a provisional title, the *Rivet* constitution of 31 August 1871 making him President of the Republic responsible before the Assembly. He sought a Republic that would be "conservative" and that would reassure the country — at first sparing even the name of Republic — while the monarchical majority in the Assembly, unable to agree on a single pretender, let the provisional regime continue. By his message of 13 November 1872 Thiers pronounced explicitly for the Republic, and by the great speech of 23–24 May 1873 he had "taken his party on the Republic." On 24 May 1873 a coalition of the right put him in a minority, and he resigned; Marshal Mac-Mahon succeeded him, pledged as a soldier to continue the liberation and to restore *ordre moral*.

## The failure of the monarchy and the Constitution of 1875

The National Assembly, elected with a monarchical majority, failed to restore a king. Hanotaux's second volume tells the double failure of the monarchical restoration: the *fusion* of the two branches of the Bourbon house and the *comte de Chambord's* letter of 27 October 1873, which insisted on his conditions and broke the campaign. After the Septennat of 20 November 1873 gave Mac-Mahon seven years of power, and after the fall of the duc de Broglie on 16 May 1874, "the monarchical past of France is liquidated. There is a clear place for the Republic."

The Constitution of 1875 was voted by a majority of one voice on the *Wallon* amendment of 30 January — an anonymous, improvised work, as Hanotaux stresses, of three constitutional laws (Senate, 24 February; public powers, 25 February; relations of the powers, 16 July) and two organic laws. It established a parliamentary Republic: two chambers with preponderance to the popularly elected one, a presidency, a cabinet, and ministerial responsibility. Hanotaux's theory of the constitution emphasises the sovereignty of the people, universal suffrage, the rights of minorities, national unity, the conciliation of unity and liberty, and a governing hatred of personal power — the whole erected against the memory of the Empire and of democratic Caesarism.

## The Republic of the Republicans

The parliamentary Republic was consolidated through a series of crises and ministries chronicled in Hanotaux's fourth volume. After the elections of 20 February 1876 gave the Republic a majority, President Mac-Mahon's attempted coup of the *Seize Mai* (the letter of 16 May 1877) — the dissolution of the Chamber and the Broglie ministry — was answered by the union of the republican deputies, the famous "363," and by the republican victory in the elections of 14 October 1877. Gambetta's cry, that the Marshal must "submit or resign," set the terms of the struggle. Mac-Mahon's resignation in January 1879 and the election of Jules Grévy as President completed the republican conquest of the executive; Grévy, Hanotaux judges, conceived the presidency as the ideal of France ought to be, though his election was a fault.

Under Waddington, Freycinet, and above all Jules Ferry, the Republic turned to the work of consolidation and laicisation: the amnesty, the "purification" of the administration, the return of the Chambers to Paris, the first 14 July festival, the Ferry school laws making primary education free, obligatory, and secular, the expulsion of the religious congregations, and the colonial expansion into Tunisia. Gambetta's "grand ministry" of 1881–82 — the ministry of the man who had been the incarnation of republican energy — failed in a few months, cut short in 1882 by the Chamber's rejection of constitutional revision, and Gambetta himself died the same year. Hanotaux closes the Republic's heroic age there, with the parliamentary Republic established and tested.

## The Republic as a lived faith and as a regime

Beyond the institutional history, the Third Republic carried a moral and spiritual meaning that Péguy and Sabatier articulate in this corpus. For Péguy, writing in *Notre jeunesse* (1910), the Republic had once been a *mystique* — "when one died for the Republic" — borne by ordinary republican families, and the Dreyfus affair was its last surge of heroism. By his own day, he laments, the Republic had ceased to be lived and become a thesis; the intermediate generation had lost the instinct of republican mystique, and the Republic, from organic, had become logical. Sabatier, in *L'orientation religieuse de la France actuelle* (1911), treats the Republic's laic school as a new incarnation of a secular religious effort — "by the laic school our country has sought to conquer itself" — and reads the Republic's religious orientation as essentially French, a movement of religious reconstitution rather than of mere anticlerical negation. The Third Republic was thus both a constitutional regime and a faith: the durable political form that answered the century's demand for liberty, unity, and the reconciliation of the citizen with the state.
