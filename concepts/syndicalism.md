# Revolutionary Syndicalism

Revolutionary syndicalism was the doctrine of the autonomous workers' movement (*mouvement ouvrier*) that arose in France in the decades around 1900 and found its most systematic theorist in Hubert Lagardelle. It held that the working class must emancipate itself through its own organisations — the trade unions, or *syndicats* — by direct action and the general strike, independently of parliamentary democracy and of the bourgeois state. Lagardelle's *Le socialisme ouvrier* (1911), a collection of essays written from 1898 to 1908, documents both the formation of the movement and the ideas that defined it.

## The movement independent of parliamentary democracy

The starting point of syndicalism was the refusal to identify socialism with democracy. Lagardelle poses the question directly — "Socialism or Democracy?" — and argues that the working class, having won political citizenship, finds itself excluded from economic power: as citizens the proletarians may administer the state's budget, but as workers in the workshop they remain "a passive multitude" with no part in the direction of enterprises, dominated by a class that makes them pay dear for a tutelage at once useless and arbitrarily prolonged. The irresistible tendency of the proletariat, he writes, is to carry into the economic order the democracy partially realised in the political order — to possess and direct in common the means of production as they possess and direct the political power. But this emancipation was to be the work of the class itself, through its unions, not of the parliamentary parties.

The political experience of the 1890s confirmed the syndicalists' distrust of parliamentarism. Lagardelle recounts the Dreyfus affair, the amnesty, and the socialists' entanglement in ministerial politics — the ministry of Waldeck-Rousseau with Millerand as the first socialist minister — and the phenomenon of "ministerialism" by which socialism was absorbed into democratic government. Against this, the syndicalist current insisted on the autonomy of the workers' movement, its separation from the parties, and its refusal to surrender the class struggle to electoral combinations.

## Anti-statism and direct action

The central ideas of syndicalism, as Lagardelle expounds them in "Les idées syndicalistes," were anti-statism, revolutionary evolution, the syndicate and the class struggle, direct action, and the general strike. The state was the great enemy: "the state is the greatest of all" the adversaries of the workers, watching the syndical organisation with an administrative and police eye, seeking to restrict the autonomy of all groupements and to extend indefinitely its own activity. Against the encroachments of the state and the temptations of "state socialism," the syndicalists set the autonomous organisation of labour and the direct action of the workers themselves. The working class is "the revolutionary class par excellence," and its revolution must be effected by revolutionary means — "not by democratic means" — through the class struggle preparing the triumph of the organised proletariat.

The general strike held a central place in the doctrine as the instrument of this revolution. Direct action — the strike, the boycott, the sabotage of the workshop — replaced the ballot as the workers' means of making their power felt, and the general strike was conceived as the culminating act by which the organised working class would take possession of production. Lagardelle's syndicalism was a "revisionism of the left," insisting that the crisis of socialism was due to the divorce of theory and practice and that the syndicate, rather than the party, was the true organ of the working-class movement.

## The CGT and the politics of the movement

The doctrine was embodied in the French general confederation of labour, the *Confédération générale du travail* (CGT), and the syndical congresses — notably Bourges in November 1904 — recorded the formation of the movement's collective consciousness. Lagardelle's later essays, written to 1908, address the relation of the socialist party to syndicalism. He argues that the party, carried by the idealism that issues from the syndicalist working masses, must present itself before the non-working masses as "the only party of political cleanliness and of anti-statist action," making its own the essential claims and fundamental ideas of syndicalism — a programme of reduction of the state and of financial morality. The movement, in this vision, was the seed of a new society: the workers' organisations themselves, the future organs of a self-governing economy, prefigured within the old order the free association that was to replace it.
