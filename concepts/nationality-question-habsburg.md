# The Nationality Question of the Habsburg Monarchy

The nationality question (*Nationalitätenfrage*) was the fundamental internal problem of the Habsburg monarchy: how a state composed of some ten or twenty nationalities could be governed and held together in an age when, as Sosnosky puts it, the national principle had become "the governing force of the age." Within the Austrian half (Cisleithania) it pitted Germans against Czechs, Poles, Ruthenians, Slovenes and Italians; it fed the movements of pan-Germanism, pan-Slavism and Italian irredentism; and it strained the monarchy increasingly toward the war of 1914. It is the Austrian counterpart to the Magyarization question of the Hungarian half, and together the two form the internal history of the Dual Monarchy.

## The national principle and language

Sosnosky compares the Habsburg monarchy, after Helfert, to "a settled-down migration of peoples." In Cisleithania lived not fewer than ten nations — Germans (about 35.8 per cent), Czechs, Poles, Ruthenians, Slovenes, Croats and Serbs, Italians and Ladins, and Romanians — to which the whole monarchy added several more. Given this national motley, frictions and even collisions between the peoples were "not strange, indeed almost inevitable"; but the *permanent* national quarrel that had raged through the monarchy for more than half a century was neither founded nor justified, for several nations could live together in peace, as Switzerland and the United States proved. What was needed, Sosnosky writes, was "only the honest will; but that is wanting, at least in those who have the power to exercise it." The identification of nation with language — *Nationalität und Sprache* — was the deepest cause: each nation made a monstrous cult of its own nationality and demanded of the others recognition of a precedence it had usurped, while the press and parliament, far from extinguishing the consuming fire, fanned it as their chief political task.

## The national currents

The national principle broke into distinct currents. Pan-Germanism (*Alldeutschtum*), in the broad sense of the whole German-national movement in Austria, was the attempt of the German element, which had once held constitutional power, to defend its leading place against the other nationalities. Pan-Slavism (*Panslawismus*) found its principal exponents in turn among the Czechs, the South Slavs, the Poles and the Ruthenians; within the Austrian state it appeared above all as the Czech national movement, which demanded the recognition of the Czech language and resisted what it saw as German overlordship, and it shaded by the end of the period into a neo-Slavism. The Italian irredenta (*Irredenta*) in the littoral and Tyrol looked across the frontier to the kingdom of Italy, which claimed the unredeemed lands. Sosnosky sets out the symptoms and causes of the national malady: the symptoms were the ceaseless parliamentary obstruction and street violence — the Czechs' obstruction of the Reichsrat, the riots and clashes over language; the causes lay in the very structure of a state whose peoples each believed itself the first, in which no single nation could claim the hegemony that the Germans had once exercised and had lost "through their own fault."

## The system and its failure

The Austrian half was governed within the frame of the *Reichsrat* and the December constitution of 1867. Redlich's account of the pre-war system shows how far the nationality conflict had already corroded it: the ministry in Vienna long exercised the legislative power in practice through the emergency-notification provision (Article 14) by imperial decree, and the Reichsrat had been sent home in the spring of 1914 on the renewal of obstructionist tactics among the Slav parties. The universal suffrage of 1907, expected to heal the national discord, had disappointed; the government sat as "officials' ministries" before a house elected under it, and Redlich describes "the unceasing accession of national radicalism among the Czechs, South Slavs and Ruthenians," the growth of an anti-parliamentary mood at court and among the generals, and the reflux of all this upon the separatist and dissolving currents among the South Slavs, Poles and Ruthenians. The nationality question, in short, was not solved but deepened by the parliamentary system itself.

## The possible solutions

The nationality question was at the root of the alternative futures that Sosnosky surveyed on the eve of 1914. The most radical was the "Greater Austria" (*Groß-Österreich*) of Aurel Popovici, who proposed to dissolve the monarchy on the basis of its ethnographic map into some fifteen national states under a common emperor and a central government for the common affairs, with German as the mediating language of the Reich — a fusion of the old centralist unitary state with the modern idea of national autonomy, which Sosnosky calls "a juster solution of the apparently insoluble nationality question than can be imagined," though he notes the conservative and Magyar resistance it provoked. Trialism, the addition of a South Slav unit, was the more practical proposal; the revision of the dualism and a greater Hungary were the others. All these, Sosnosky argued, were variations of one goal, which for so many-peopled a realm could be no other than "to attain unity through multiplicity," and so to embody the watchword of its ruler — *viribus unitis*, "with united forces." The war dissolved the question not by solution but by dissolution: the national states that replaced the monarchy, as Redlich shows, took up their ready-formed officials and organs from the last legal act of the dynasty.
