# Mystique and Politique (Péguy)

"Mystique and politique" is Charles Péguy's famous distinction, drawn in *Notre jeunesse* (1910), between the spiritual faith of the Dreyfusards and its degeneration into political calculation. For Péguy, every great cause begins in *mystique* — a lived, disinterested faith for which men are willing to die — and ends in *politique* — the parliamentary, calculating language of parties that translate and betray what was done in the name of faith. His distinction became one of the defining ideas of the French intellectual tradition and the key to his own reading of the Republic and the modern world.

## The faith of the Dreyfusards

Péguy's essay is an "examen de conscience" of the generation that had fought for the revision of the Dreyfus trial. The Dreyfusard cause, in his account, was a *mystique*: a faith held for its own sake, without calculation. The Dreyfusards refused to accept an official injustice because — in a deep Christian movement — "a single injustice, a single crime suffices to break the whole social pact, the whole social contract." They spoke the language of eternal rather than temporal salvation, refusing that France should be "constituted in a state of mortal sin." Péguy opposes the *hommes du salut éternel* to the *hommes du salut temporel*: the adversaries spoke the language of reason of state, that one must not sacrifice a whole people for one man; the Dreyfusards answered that the honour of a people is "of a single piece." Bernard-Lazare, whom Péguy honours as the prophet of the affair, embodied this faith — defending the returned captain even in his imperfections, for "he is innocent, that is already much."

The affair, Péguy writes, was "the last of the operations of the republican mystique," the supreme surge of the heroism of his generation. His own, he says, is "the last of the generations which possess the republican mystique" — the rear-guard of the republican faith, its tradition borne by ordinary families, the "tissue of the Republic."

## The degeneration into politique

The distinction that gives the essay its fame is the passage from faith to calculation. "The republican mystique — that was when one died for the Republic; republican politique — that is now when one lives from it." The political parties, Péguy holds, can speak only the political, parliamentary language, and they translate — betray, *traducunt, tradunt* — everything that is done for the mystiques. Since the degradation of the Dreyfusard mystique into Dreyfusard politique, he and his collaborators have known fifteen years of poverty defending Christian consciences and republican liberties, only to find reactionary politicians forbid them being republicans and republican politicians forbid them being Christians.

Péguy's maxim is universal: "Everything begins in mystique and ends in politique. Everything begins with mystique, with one's own mystique, and everything ends in politique." The question is not whether a given policy is good or bad but whether it springs from a living faith or from calculation. He compares the degradations — what "clerical politics" has made of the Christian mystique, what "radical politics" has made of the republican mystique, what the clergy has made of the saints, what the parliamentarians have made of the heroes — and insists that one must compare like with like, mystique with mystique and politique with politique, never a mystique with a politique.

## The modern world against culture

The source of the degeneration, for Péguy, is what he calls *le monde moderne* — those who believe in nothing and glory in it, "not even in atheism." The de-republicanisation of France is the same movement as its de-Christianisation, a single movement of "demystification," by which the people no longer believes either in the Republic or in God. This is "modern sterility," which dries up equally the political and the Christian city. The modern world, he writes, opposes not only the old régime but every old culture together: "it is the first time in the history of the world that a whole world lives and thrives, appears to thrive, against all culture."

Yet Péguy does not despair: "this race has seen many others." The Republic has ceased to be lived and has become a thesis — "when a regime, from organic, has become logical, and from living historical, it is a regime that is on the ground." But he hopes the following generation will be "at last a mystical generation," and that the two mystiques, republican and Christian, will flower again together in a single deep movement. Against the royalists of the *Action française*, he concedes that the only force of a traditional monarchy is that the king is loved — "the only force, the only value, the only dignity of everything is to be loved" — and finds in their "we would be ready to die for the king" the one thing that speaks to him, for it is a truth of faith rather than an argument. Yet one founds no culture on derision and sarcasm. The essay closes with the word that the royalists are but "a vague and theoretical menace," and "the day they become a real menace they will see what we are still capable of doing for the Republic."

## The distinction in the corpus

The mystique/politique distinction runs beneath the other texts of this corpus. Sabatier's *L'orientation religieuse de la France actuelle*, which cites Péguy among the French authors shaping the religious movement, shares his sense of a present *mystique* — a religious aspiration awakening amid indifference, seeking the foundations of a new temple in sentiments of Catholic origin. And the distinction illuminates the whole arc of the Third Republic: the republican mystique of the founders and its degeneration into the politique of the party men, the transformation of a lived faith into a set of propositions. Péguy's idea thus became, for the generation before the war, the means by which contemporaries understood the passage of their own great causes from heroism to calculation — and the criterion by which they judged every institution, party, and faith of the modern world.
