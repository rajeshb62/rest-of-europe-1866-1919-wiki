# Bonapartism

Bonapartism is the political tradition of Napoleonic authority — a plebiscitary Caesarism that joins democracy to authoritarian rule, claiming to embody the sovereignty of the people through the will of a single man. Rooted in the first Napoleon and revived by the Second Empire of Napoleon III, it offered France the promise of order, glory, and national greatness in exchange for the abdication of parliamentary liberty. In the decades after 1870, Bonapartism survived as a party of the fallen dynasty, a recurrent menace to the nascent Republic, and a temptation that the Republic's founders explicitly rejected.

## Plebiscitary Caesarism and the imperial idea

The core of Bonapartism was the appeal from the assemblies to the people directly — the plebiscite. Hanotaux, describing the constitutional theory that preceded the Republic, writes of the "advent of democratic Caesarism" in the nineteenth century, the form in which the Emperor rested his title not on divine right but on the repeated consent of universal suffrage. This was the essence of the *régime personnel*: the sovereign as the popular tribune invested with absolute power, standing above parties and parliaments, deriving his legitimacy from the nation rather than from any intermediary body. It was a democratic doctrine turned against democratic liberty.

Delord's republican history treats the Second Empire as this doctrine in action. The imperial cult — the centenary of Napoleon I celebrated in 1869, the pictures restored to the Louvre, the Saint-Mandé banquet — renewed the family's appeal to glory and to the memory of the first Emperor. Ollivier, who ultimately broke with the system, portrays the authoritarian Emperor as caught between the logic of his own plebiscitary title and the demand of an increasingly self-conscious public opinion for constitutional guarantees.

## The Bonapartist party in the Republic

After the fall of the Empire, Bonapartism did not vanish. Hanotaux's volumes on the early Republic return again and again to the "Bonapartist peril" as a shadow over the National Assembly and the monarchy's failure. The *appel au peuple* group — the name itself a Bonapartist formula for the referendum — agitated for a return to the imperial system; Bonapartist manifestations broke out around the majority of the Prince Imperial; the parliamentary inquiry on Bonapartist manœuvres was ordered in 1874. In the constitutional struggle of the Assembly, the Bonapartists formed one of the three monarchical parties whose divisions saved the Republic, refusing fusion with either Bourbon branch and keeping alive the hope of the imperial restoration.

Hanotaux stresses how the Bonapartists' presence fragmented the monarchical right and thereby unintentionally cleared the way for the Republic. In the electoral politics of 1873–1876 the Bonapartists recur as a permanent, active element — campaigning, propagandising, and allying now with the extreme right, now even with the left, in the intricate combinations of the Assembly. The Prince Imperial, the heir of the dynasty, stood at the centre of these hopes until his death in 1879; thereafter the imperial party faded into the background of French politics, surviving as a tradition rather than a threat.

## The Republic's rejection of the Napoleonic inheritance

The Third Republic defined itself precisely against Bonapartism. Its hatred of personal power — Hanotaux's theory of the Constitution of 1875 emphasises the "hatred of personal power" as a governing sentiment of the constituents — was the direct antithesis of the Napoleonic doctrine. The parliamentary Republic built assemblies, ministerial responsibility, and a ceremonial presidency precisely so that no single man could again place himself above the law as the embodiment of the people. The fall of the Second Empire, the Prussian war and the Commune, and the long consolidation of the Republic were read by contemporaries as the definitive judgment on Bonapartism's promise of order and glory: order had ended in defeat, glory in national disaster.
