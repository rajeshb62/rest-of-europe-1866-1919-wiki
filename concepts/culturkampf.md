# The Kulturkampf

The Kulturkampf ("culture struggle") was the conflict between the government of [[otto-von-bismarck]] and the Catholic Church in Prussia and the new German Empire in the 1870s, fought over the limits of the state's authority against the claims of Rome. The term, meaning literally "culture struggle," was applied to the battle Bismarck and his Liberal allies waged against the influence of the clergy in the life of the state — a battle waged through a series of laws, known after their May enactments as the *Maigesetze* (May Laws), that brought the training, appointment, and discipline of the clergy under state control. The struggle opened in 1871, reached its height in the middle years of the decade, and ended, without victory, in Bismarck's retreat after the turn toward protectionism and the break with the Liberals. For this corpus it is the German expression of the wider anti-clerical conflict that runs through the period, comparable to the anticlerical politics of [[second-empire-france|France]] and the [[liberal-empire|liberal empire]].

## The enemy within the new Reich

Lenz's account of the Kulturkampf treats it as the great inner crisis that followed the founding of the Empire, the opposition that arose "out of the nation itself, which had just gone out unanimously against the hereditary enemy of the German name." An opposition formed, so stormy in attack, so unshakable in defence, and so fundamental in its hostility to the newly created state, that all the opponents Bismarck had previously met shrank before it. It was split into two camps — the clerical and the social-democratic — which differed in origin, aim, and character but were bound by "the common hatred of the new Reich and the internationality of their policy," a hatred great enough to bridge the gulf between them. Even their leader of the social democrats, August Bebel, had risen as an agitator of the Catholic journeymen's associations, so closely did the two oppositions touch at the root.

The clericals were the first and best armed. Gathering even while Germany's finest sons still fought in France, they entered the first Reichstag in March as a firmly closed band of fifty-seven deputies. Their ranks joined Poles and Guelphs, Prussians and Rhenish confederationists, feudal lords and democrats — "the vanquished, the subjugated, who gathered round the banner of the church militant." At their head stood, beside Reichensperger and Mallinckrodt, the old Guelph minister, the Prussian diplomat who in June 1866 had delivered the letter of defiance to the German Confederation and its Catholic leading power — Bismarck's own youth-friend, Charles Savigny, of Huguenot and Brentano blood, driven by wounded ambition into the arms of his country's mortal enemies. They were a party that denied even its own name — "We are not confessional," their leader declared — while their programme, colourless and many-hued, could have been claimed by other parties too.

## Bismarck's reluctance

Bismarck did not seek the struggle, and entered it only when he saw no other way. Lenz insists that "Bismarck never thought of renewing the battles of the confessional age": his counsel had always been to avoid unnecessary concessions, never to give the impression of needing help, and otherwise to wait for the attack behind the bulwarks of the state, resolved to defend every inch of possession even against seemingly reasonable demands. Through the years in which the war with France was preparing, and even when the ghibelline spirit threatened to break into the church itself, he resisted the temptation to influence the course of the Council through which the Pope sought to lead the opposing elements of his church to victory. He turned back the request of his Roman envoy, Count Harry von Arnim, to send plenipotentiaries of the governments to Rome — partly because the measure was impractical, but chiefly because such an interference in the life of the church would contradict "the Protestant character of Prussia itself."

It was only when Rome itself laid down binding norms for the relation of state and church without admitting the interested state as an equal factor, or when the German bishops sought help, that Bismarck was resolved to bring the power of the state to bear. And so it happened: while Germany flung itself into its war for freedom and greatness, Rome proclaimed the dogma that gave the last consecration to the theocratic absolutism of the Tiber — the dogma of papal infallibility. The German bishops dared not leave their Roman root, and their inner resistance shattered against the "ruthless offensive of the Jesuit-led Curia." Academic Germany protested loudly against the new dogma as a falsification of the old and true Catholicism; religious teachers in the Catholic gymnasia and the theologians in the faculties refused to obey their bishops. In Bavaria the movement was even stronger than in Prussia: the greatest scholar of the Catholic world, Ignaz von Döllinger, turned away from the church and placed himself at the head of its opponents. "As a Christian, as a theologian, as a historian, as a citizen," he declared on 28 March, "I cannot accept this doctrine." He was excommunicated, but his university elected him rector, and the minister and King Ludwig himself showed him sympathy.

## The struggle breaks out

Even then Bismarck hoped at first to avoid the war. He tried the Curia directly, and it almost seemed Rome might side with the victor; the negotiations that Ledochowski conducted at Versailles in November were followed in March by a letter of congratulation from the Pope to the Kaiser expressing the hope of friendship between Reich and Church. But the antagonisms to be reconciled were far too great for diplomacy to help. The Curie followed its own interest and the spirit of the church over which it ruled: it wanted help against the "robber-king" who had made the Holy Father a prisoner in his own house, and demanded the undoing of the Italian conquest of Rome. Bismarck could not give what the Curia wanted, any more than he could grant the Centre party's demand to transfer the vague provisions of the Prussian constitution on church and state to the Reich — both contradicted the principles on which the new Reich rested.

And Prussia's government could not refuse its own subjects' requests for protection against their ecclesiastical superiors, in their consciences and their rights. Before the end of 1870 the cultus minister, von Mühler — himself anything but a liberal — met the archbishop of Cologne's edicts against the Bonn theologians with a rescript insisting that the legal position of professors of Catholic theology was to be measured only by the statutory provisions the state itself had erected. The minister stood by the contested teachers at Breslau and Braunsberg too: the pupils must accept the instruction their teachers offered or leave the school, since the withdrawal of the *missio canonica* had no effect in state law. In that the sum of the conflict already found expression.

When battle became unavoidable, it was not Bismarck's way to let his opponents strike first. Already in April he announced that the aggressive tendency of the new fraction might force the government, for the sake of effective defence, to become aggressive in turn; in June the moment seemed to him to have come. On 8 July, four days after the bishop of Ermland had pronounced the great excommunication over one of the resisting teachers, came the first blow: the abolition of the Catholic department in the ministry of cultus. Respect for public opinion was never Bismarck's strength, and he had proved that he did not shrink from conflict with it; but he had never wanted to make the conflict a regular state institution. He had taken up the struggle because he could reach his goal no other way.

## The May Laws and the height of the fight

The war was fought, in Lenz's account, not by Bismarck alone but by Bismarck at the head of an alliance he did not love. "Had the Conservatives remained true to him, he would certainly have entered it with a gladder heart — he would have had the ground that was agreeable to him." But the party of the old friends left its former leader in the lurch and forced upon him the alliance or, as Roon called it, the *mésalliance* with the Liberals. The Liberal influence found its expression in a part of the May Laws, from the school supervision law down to the requirement of a three-year course of study for Catholic theologians at German universities and their submission to a state examination in the historical sciences. Bismarck's own aims were not set so far — not because he stood nearer the Roman spirit, but because they lay too far off for his realist eye, and because he feared the liberal flood might reach further and overflow his own field. He would later complain that the liberal jurists had spoiled his concept, and almost disowned his share in the laws; but Lenz is firm that he gave the direction, that "Falk set the pace that Bismarck prescribed him." The individual details may have been worked out by Falk and his counsellors, but the goals were set by their master and lord. Some laws he regarded, like his allies, from the first only as weapons of war (*Kampfgesetze*). "For a generation," he announced at the height of the conflict, he had calculated the struggle.

The consequences were terrible. The shepherds of the Catholic flock — a whole series of bishops and many hundreds of priests — were deposed or went to prison; their pulpits stood empty, their faithful were left orphaned, and ever higher swelled the flood of hatred and accusation against the "diocletian" persecution of the church. Law hung upon law; ever deeper the political power pressed into the fabric of the Catholic church, its dogma, cult, and constitution; and ever more defiant, more unbending became the force it met. It availed nothing to repeat a thousand times that government and majority of the Reichstag did not want to touch the faith, that Catholic subjects stood as near to the Kaiser's heart as the evangelical, that his chancellor claimed nothing but the right of the state to draw its own boundary and to secure itself against arbitrarily begun attacks. It was no tolerance anymore, Lenz writes, and had never been, and could not be. It was the inner contradiction between the political power that had united Germany and the Roman Catholic world of ideas that had been subjected to it, breaking forth with elemental force: "the spirit of the Counter-Reformation remained alive in our people, and it was this that rose up against the heretic-state that the weapons had made victor." Ever more clearly the Protestant character of the struggle came to light.

## The crumbling of Bismarck's world

The war cost Bismarck his old friends. Already in December 1872 he wrote to Roon, almost in despair, of a trade "in which one gains many enemies but no new friends — rather loses the old ones if one carries it on honestly and fearlessly for ten years." The shield-bearers of his first campaigns as minister — men like Kleist, Blanckenburg, and the old Thadden — joined the long-since fallen Gerlach and Senfft-Pilsach. Twice, at the end of 1872 and again a year later, Blanckenburg refused to join the ministry; "Bismarck," he wrote to Roon in November 1873, "has broken every bridge in Pomerania that I might have rebuilt." It was the Prussian particularism that spoke from these words, the "junker spirit" to which the German idea had always been foreign and uneasy. Roon himself and even the Kaiser grew uneasy; the Kaiserin, once the patroness of liberal interests, drifted into the catholicizing current, and Bismarck's own health broke, driving him into the long seclusion of Varzin — yet he remained chained to his office, "carrying his Promethean fate," for the work he had created demanded its master.

On the other side the Liberal allies sought to turn the wave to their own advantage. They were themselves among the vanquished, but the glory of 1870 had served them better than any other party; their strength lay in Prussia's new provinces and in the federal states, from which came their most brilliant leaders. In the elections to the first two Reichstags the National Liberals held an overwhelming majority, still carrying in their programmes a remnant of the ideals of 1848 — a Reich ministry, the guarding of popular rights, the enlargement of parliamentary power. They wanted to support the chancellor but also to force him into their direction, and the friction began again immediately after the war. In the summer of 1873 came a violent clash when Lasker, the eloquent, spoke of popular rights in the debate on the military law; Bismarck answered this "declamatory digression" — "These are speeches from a past time, which I am entitled to call declamatory" — under stormy protests. In 1874, at the great military debate, the two sides came close to the "crash"; the breach was patched by the compromise that fixed the strength of the army for seven years, but the alliance was already far loosened. "Bismarck wants to be rid of the Liberals," Blanckenburg reported to Roon in the autumn.

## An unavailing struggle

Lenz's verdict on the Kulturkampf is that it was, in the end, a failure — "a war that at last ran into the sand." He asks directly: has Bismarck suffered his first defeat in it? Did he achieve what he wanted? "The answer will differ according to whether one asks what Bismarck, and what the parties that stood in battle with him, expected of it." The leaders of the national movement, the Liberals of every shade, hoped from the outset for more than the chancellor: that "the destruction of the two Catholic great powers" would be followed by the demolition of the Roman spirit in the fatherland of Martin Luther. None wished to rob Catholic countrymen of the form of their worship and faith, but they hoped to reshape the spirit of German Catholicism and subject it to the high ideas that had fructified the German mind for centuries. Bismarck's own aims were more modest and more political: he wanted to remove "the foreign body that had lodged itself in his work," to break the fraction that contrasted more sharply than any other party with the Prussian state-idea and with the principle of the new Reich itself. That the time would come when precisely that fraction would possess the decisive power in national politics — even that he would one day have to treat with it — he certainly did not expect when he summoned the national genius to battle against it.

The struggle thus failed of every aim. The state neither destroyed the Roman spirit nor broke the Centre party; it emptied pulpits and filled prisons, but the resistance it met only stiffened. When Bismarck turned, after the crisis of the later 1870s, to protectionism and the new economic policy, he abandoned the Liberal alliance and with it the Kulturkampf, moving toward a settlement with Rome. The *Kulturkampf* thereby closes a chapter: it had been the first great inner test of the new Reich, the moment when the anti-clerical energy that in [[second-empire-france|France]] and the [[liberal-empire|liberal empire]] shaped politics found its German and Protestant form — and, like its French parallels, it ended in retreat, leaving the church embattled but unbroken.
