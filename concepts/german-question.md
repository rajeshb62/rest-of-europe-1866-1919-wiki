# The German Question

The German Question (*deutsche Frage*, "the German question") was the central political problem of central Europe in the middle of the nineteenth century: who was to unite Germany, and on what terms — by which state, under what constitution, and embracing which territory. From the collapse of the old German Confederation through the wars of unification it was the question of whether the many German states and lands should be drawn into a single national state, and whether the lead should fall to the Habsburg monarchy of Austria or to the Hohenzollern kingdom of Prussia. The answer, settled by war in 1866 and completed in 1871, was the *kleindeutsch* ("little German") solution — a Prussian-led Germany from which Austria was excluded. The concept underlies the founding of the [[north-german-confederation]] and the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]].

## A question of the century

The German Question was not born in 1866 but reached back to the era of the French Revolution and Napoleon, when the thousand-year old Holy Roman Empire dissolved and the German lands were reorganized into a loose bundle of sovereign states. After the Congress of Vienna these states were bound only in the German Confederation, a defensive league of more than thirty members presided over alternately by the two great powers, Austria and Prussia. It was a structure of princes, not of peoples, and its loosening only sharpened the underlying question: whether the German nation, whose national idea had been kindled in the struggle against [[second-empire-france|Napoleonic France]], could ever find a single political home.

Lenz, writing the biography of the statesman who finally answered the question, frames it as the inheritance of 1848. The revolutionaries of that year, he recalls, had set the goal of gathering "the strength of the nation in one centre" — of founding a united fatherland. Yet the Frankfurt Parliament failed, and its failure left the German Question open and unresolved through the 1850s. The Confederation remained, in effect, a diplomatic holding pattern, while underneath it two rival conceptions of unity competed for the loyalty of the German public.

## Two answers

These two answers, the *kleindeutsch* and the *grossdeutsch*, defined the field of debate. The *grossdeutsch* ("greater German") solution would have brought all the German-speaking lands, including the German provinces of Austria, into a single state under Habsburg leadership — a Germany that stretched from the Alps to the Baltic and the North Sea. The *kleindeutsch* ("lesser German") solution would have built a smaller German state under Prussian leadership, deliberately excluding Austria so that its non-German millions could not dilute the national character of the new state. (See [[kleindeutsch-grossdeutsch]].)

Lenz observes how profoundly the question divided even those who agreed on the goal. All the German parties, he notes — "even the Kleindeutschen" — stood with their front toward the Rhine rather than toward the Danube; they saw France rather than Austria as the enemy. The peculiarity of Bismarck was that he alone located the decisive adversary in Austria, and dreaded above all that Prussia might be drawn into a war against France that would leave the Habsburg power intact. While the conservative friends of the *Kreuzzeitung* preached the duty of defending Austria's "legitimate rights," and Moltke wanted to strike against France, Bismarck saw that the German Question could only be resolved against Austria, on the battlefield or in its shadow.

## The Confederation proves unworkable

By the 1860s the German Confederation had become the chief obstacle to every solution. The project of the Frankfurter Fürstentag (the Frankfurt Congress of Princes) of 1863 showed the futility of reform through the old order. There the Austrian minister Schmerling, urged on by the emperor, put forward a plan to reorganize the Confederation with an imperial presidency, a house of princes, and a delegated parliament — a scheme that would have entrenched Habsburg predominance. Bismarck, then Prussia's minister-president, treated the whole enterprise with contempt, calling it "a foam-wave" arranged more for the inner politics of Austria than as a serious move, and urging the king simply to efface himself and let the Bundestag friends make what they could of their own new building. The Congress collapsed, proving that the German Question could not be answered within the existing Confederation, because Austria and Prussia could not share a lead that each claimed for itself.

## Settlement by war

The resolution came through force. In the [[austro-prussian-war-1866]] Prussia defeated Austria at Königgrätz, dissolved the old Confederation, and excluded Austria from Germany. The [[peace-of-prague-1866|Peace of Prague]] left the German Question, for the moment, with the kleindeutsch answer enforced by arms. Bismarck then erected the [[north-german-confederation]] over the states north of the Main, a Prussian-led federal state with a directly elected parliament, and — crucially — framed it from the outset as the foundation that could later be extended to the south. Lenz emphasizes that the constitution Bismarck drafted in December 1866 was "in its outlines already the constitution which Bismarck gave the North German Confederation and, after the victory over France, transferred to the German Empire." In it there was no longer any place for Austria.

## The South and the completion

The German Question was not yet fully closed, for the four south German states — Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, and Hesse-Darmstadt — remained outside the northern federation, left by the Peace of Prague a merely international position that neither prescribed nor forbade their union with the North. This was the great question of the years 1867–1870. Bismarck himself wished to postpone its answer, for he saw clearly that as soon as the southern states entered, the international crisis — the great European war that had been happily avoided in the German conflict — would break. He wanted the foundations of his power to rest secure in German soil before he challenged the fate that was gathering on every horizon.

The completion came with the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]]. The French Emperor Napoleon III, in whose calculations the perpetuation of German division had been a pillar of French greatness, challenged Prussia, and the resulting war drew the south German states into a common cause with the North. At the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]] the German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles, and the German Question received its final answer: a Prussian-dominated *kleindeutsch* empire under the Hohenzollern crown, with the Habsburg lands forever outside. What the revolutionaries of 1848 had demanded, Lenz concludes, was achieved by the very power whose ministry Bismarck directed — "the strength of the nation gathered in one centre," but a centre fixed by Prussian might rather than by the national assembly of Frankfurt.
