# Socialism in France

**Socialism in France** in the last decades of the century passed through the divisions the corpus documents from within: parliamentary and ministerial socialism on one side, the autonomous workers' movement (*mouvement ouvrier*) of revolutionary **syndicalism** on the other. The question posed by **Lagardelle's** essays of 1898–1908 is whether socialism should seek power through democracy and the Republic or constitute itself as an independent class force against the state.

## The parliamentary question

Lagardelle's first part examines socialism's relation to democracy: the formation of the socialist party and its entry into republican politics, the case of Millerand and the Waldeck-Rousseau ministry, the Dreyfus affair's pull on the socialists, and the meaning of ministerialism. The split is between those who would carry the Republic forward from within the parliamentary game and those who held that the working class must act on its own, in its own organs, without compromising with a bourgeois state.

## Syndicalism and the workers' movement

The second part advances the syndicalist answer: the labour syndicate, not the party, as the autonomous and creative force of the future; the general strike and direct action as the means; the political and agricultural policy of the syndicalist movement. This is the doctrine of the CGT and of the theorists who made France the homeland of revolutionary syndicalism before 1914.

## The mystical counter-current

**Péguy**, in the same years, supplied the moral and religious temper of the same quarrel: his *Notre jeunesse* defended the "mystique républicaine" and the Dreyfusard faith against its degeneration into "politique," and reserved its deepest contempt for the "monde moderne" that prospers against every culture. Together Lagardelle and Péguy map the spiritual and organisational crisis of the French Left in the years before 1914 — the Republic that could not hold its own revolutionaries.
