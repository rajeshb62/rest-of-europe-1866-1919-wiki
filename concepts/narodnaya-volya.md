# Narodnaya Volya (The People's Will)

*Narodnaya Volya* (Народная Воля, "The People's Will") was the Russian revolutionary terrorist organization of the late 1870s and 1880s that made the assassination of the emperor the centre of its programme. Breaking out of the populist movement of the intelligentsia that had gone "to the people" in the hope of raising the peasants, the party concluded that the autocratic state was the sole obstacle to the people's welfare and that the revolutionaries, who could not command the masses, must strike at the state through its head. Its campaign of bombs and mines against [[alexander-ii]] culminated in the catastrophe of 1 March 1881, when the emperor was killed on the eve of the very reform he seemed about to grant. Within this corpus the party stands as the answer of despair to the reaction that followed 1866, the direct cause of the reaffirmation of [[autocracy-russia]] under [[alexander-iii]], and the archetype of the revolutionary terrorism that runs through Russian history to 1917.

## The revolt against the peasant-based movement

The party grew out of the failure of the earlier populism that Kornilov's lectures chronicle. Through the 1870s the radical intelligentsia — the Chaikovtsy, the followers of Bakunin and Lavrov, the society *Zemlya i Volya* ("Land and Freedom") — had put its faith in the peasantry, going "to the people" to preach socialism and raise the village against the state. The movement ended in disillusion: the peasants did not rise, the propaganda of the intellectuals met indifference or hostility, and the police, as Pahlen's memorandum recorded, rounded up the agitators in droves. The conviction spread among the revolutionaries that the state, not the people, was the true enemy, and that a small, disciplined, conspiratorial organization could by violence shake the autocracy that the masses could not or would not move. Out of this reasoning, by the end of the 1870s, *Zemlya i Volya* split, and from its terrorist wing arose the party that set the assassination of the tsar as its first and governing task.

## The campaign against Alexander II

The party's central committee — the *Ispolnitelny komitet* ("Executive Committee") — organized a series of attempts on the life of Alexander II. The record is one of audacity and near-misses. There were shots and plots on the emperor's routes; there was the celebrated attempt of the young nobleman who fired in the Palace Square; and there was the spectacular explosion in the Winter Palace itself, when a bomb planted by a carpenter named in the palace stores blew up a dining-room that the emperor, by a change of plan, had not entered. The campaign threw the highest circles into confusion and vacillation. The emperor, warned repeatedly by his ministers, was kept from his customary Sunday outings; the government answered with the establishment of the Supreme Administrative Commission under Count Loris-Melikov, who combined the repression of the terrorists with an approach to the liberals — the beginnings of the constitutional movement that pointed toward a share of representative government. The revolutionaries watched this, and their design matured: they had determined not merely to strike, but to strike at the very moment that would make the blow decisive.

## 1 March 1881

The plot that closed the reign was prepared with the greatest care. The Executive Committee stationed its agents along the route the emperor would take through the capital to the Winter Palace: the conspirators Zheliabov and Perovskaya directed the bomb-throwers, and mines were laid under the streets against a possible escape. On 1 March 1881 the emperor, who had been persuaded to drive out again after a long confinement, was returning from the Mikhailovsky Palace when the first bomb was thrown, wounding the escort and stopping the carriage. As the emperor lingered over the wounded — the fatal hesitation — a second assassin hurled the grenade that struck him down. He was carried to the palace to die that same day. The party had achieved its aim, and had achieved it with terrible irony: at the very hour of his death, Alexander II was on the point of accepting Loris-Melikov's project for a representative commission — the constitution that the terrorists, by killing the reformer, did more than anything else to destroy. The assassination of the Tsar-Liberator closed an era and opened the era of reaction.

## The myth of regicide and its cost

The act that was meant to free Russia bound it faster. The death of Alexander II handed the throne to [[alexander-iii]], resolved against the liberalism of his father, and to his tutor [[konstantin-pobedonostsev]], who had already prepared the counter-reform. In the days after the catastrophe the revolutionaries found that the people did not rise to welcome the regicide; the peasantry, as Witte records in his account of the reaction, was horrified rather than liberated, and the "Holy Brotherhood," a league of loyalists formed to oppose the revolutionary conspiracy, gathered the support of the propertied classes. The party itself was destroyed by the police in the years that followed, its leaders hanged or imprisoned. But the myth of regicide outlived the organization: the image of a handful of devoted revolutionaries striking down the tyrant at the centre of his power passed into the legend of the Russian revolutionary movement, and the tactical question the party had raised — whether a bold blow could shatter the autocracy — haunted the revolutionaries of the next generation. The [[revolution-1905]] and finally the [[february-revolution-1917]] completed, by mass movement, the destruction that *Narodnaya Volya* had hoped to achieve by the assassin's bomb.

## Current state: the archetype of revolutionary terror

In the frame of this corpus *Narodnaya Volya* appears as the hinge between the populism of the 1870s and the revolutionary politics of the new century. It is the subject of the [[assassination-alexander-ii-1881]] and the moving cause of the reactionary reign that followed; its terrorist method was the seed of the strike of violence that runs through the later revolutionary movement; and its tragic logic — that the autocracy would yield only to a blow at its head — stands in sharp contrast to the social-democratic and mass movements that the industrialization of the 1890s called forth. Related to it stand the [[assassination-alexander-ii-1881]] it accomplished, the [[autocracy-russia]] and [[zemstvo-great-reforms]] it shaped and undid, and the [[revolution-1905]] and [[october-revolution-1917]] that inherited, by different means, its aim of overthrowing the autocratic state.
