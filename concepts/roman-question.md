# Roman Question

The **Roman question** (*questione romana*) is the conflict between the Italian Kingdom's claim to Rome as its capital and the Pope's temporal power over the city — kept open through the 1860s by the French garrison under **Napoleon III**, and resolved only by the breach of Porta Pia in 1870. In this corpus's frame (1866–1919) the question moves from the frustrating impasse of Mentana (1867) to its violent solution and its long constitutional aftermath in the Law of Guarantees.

## Two claims on one city

Unitary Italian writers treat Rome as the necessary capital of the nation and the temporal monarchy as a medieval residue to be swept away; papal and Catholic legitimist speech treats temporal power as the guarantee of the Pope's independence from any national cabinet. **Tivaroni's** third volume narrates the whole contest from the Syllabus through the ministries of Rattazzi, Menabrea and Lanza-Sella, the conspiracy in Rome, and Mentana — the knot that bound Italy's claim to the presence of the French regiment.

## The French guarantee and its withdrawal

The guarantee of the Pope's temporal power rested on the French troops in Rome. After Mentana (3 November 1867) the position seemed secure — until the Franco-Prussian War of 1870 forced Napoleon III to withdraw the garrison. Tivaroni and **Oriani** both present the war's collapse as the moment the Roman question passed from the hands of diplomats into the power of Italian arms, as the fall of the French Empire removed the one protector who had held the city for the Pope.

## The breach of Porta Pia and the Law of Guarantees

With the French gone, the Italian government's hesitation yielded to the demand for Rome. The breach of Porta Pia on **20 September 1870** took the city; the Law of Guarantees of 13 May 1871 sought to regulate the Pope's position — the inviolable person, the Vatican palaces, the freedom of the conclave — while the Pope, refusing to recognise the seizure, confined himself to the Vatican as the self-styled prisoner. The question thus entered its long "Vatican" phase, unresolved for the rest of the century.

## Beyond pure diplomacy

The Roman question is simultaneously diplomatic, military and moral. It structures French domestic politics as much as Italian foreign policy, and it binds the church thread of the corpus (the Pope's refusal of the *Non expedit*, the Roman Syllabus) to the secularising story of the new Kingdom.
