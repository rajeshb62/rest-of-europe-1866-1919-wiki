# Anticlericalism and the Separation (Laïcité)

Anticlericalism was the doctrine and the policy of the Third Republic that sought to reduce the influence of the Catholic Church in public life and to establish the separation of the spiritual and the temporal powers. From Gambetta's cry — "clericalism, that is the enemy" — through the Ferry school laws and the expulsion of the congregations to the Separation of Church and State of 1905, the laic programme defined the religious character of the Republic. Sabatier's *L'orientation religieuse de la France actuelle* (1911) reads the whole movement from inside its culmination, while Péguy adds a corrective: the laic and the religious were not simple opposites.

## The religious question in the early Republic

The religious question was central to the Republic from its birth. The *ordre moral* ministries had bound the Republic's Catholic and monarchical opponents together; the republicans answered by making anticlericalism a test of loyalty. Gambetta's speech of 1872 at Grenoble, naming clericalism "the enemy," supplied the formula; in the 1877 campaign and after, the charge of ultramontanism was levelled at the republicans' opponents, and the Vatican and the *Seize Mai* were linked in republican suspicion. Hanotaux records the growing conflict between the Republic and the Church: the religious manifestations, the episcopal mandements, and the republicans' resolve to break the Church's hold on education and on public life.

## The Ferry school laws and article 7

The decisive assault came under the republican ministries of the late 1870s and early 1880s. Article 7 of the higher-education bill, proposed in the Waddington ministry and pressed by Jules Simon, aimed to close the Jesuit schools; rejected in the Senate, it was followed by the decrees of 29–30 March 1880 expelling the religious congregations. Jules Ferry's school laws then made primary education free, obligatory, and secular — the Republic's great constructive act in the religious field. Sabatier stresses that in 1882 the laic school "was far from being anticlerical": it was a new incarnation of a secular religious effort, an attempt by the nation to form its own citizens, not merely a war on the Church. Yet the conservative parties preached a crusade against it, and the school became the field of a long and bitter struggle over the soul of French childhood.

## The Separation of Church and State (1905)

The culmination of the anticlerical programme was the law of 9 December 1905 separating the churches from the state, ending the Concordat regime that had governed the relations of the Church and the state since Napoleon. The measure completed the laic project: the state would no longer recognise, endow, or regulate any religion; the churches were to organise themselves through the *associations cultuelles* ("worship associations") that the law provided. Sabatier, writing six years later, treats the Separation as a fact to be understood rather than fought: he notes with some irony the "Cultuelles, attempted toward 1905," the new church launched with the favour of the ministry and the great newspapers — a sign of the religious experiments the age produced. His analysis of the post-Separation situation stresses not a simple decline of religion but a profound re-orientation.

## The religious re-orientation

Sabatier's central argument is that the laic movement and the Separation were not the death of religion in France but the condition of its transformation. He distinguishes free thought from anticlericalism, warning against confounding the two, and observes the "rapid progress of indifference and an unexpected awakening of religious aspirations — often to be observed in one and the same individual." The Church, he holds, retains "a unique, incomparable place" in the conscience of the coming generation because alone it has taken consciousness of the unity and eternity of its life — even as its political power has been broken. The current religious orientation is "essentially French," a movement of reconstitution in which the sentiments of the present generation — the sense of the mystery, of unity and solidarity, of tradition, of disinterested duty — are of undoubted Catholic origin though no longer bound to the clerical order. The Separation thus ended the Concordat's political entanglement and opened, in Sabatier's hopeful reading, a new epoch of religious liberty and revival.

## The moral and spiritual stakes

Péguy's *Notre jeunesse* adds a further dimension. He insists that the de-republicanisation of France and its de-Christianisation are one and the same movement of "demystification," by which the people no longer believes in the Republic or in God — the sterility of the modern world that "prospers against all culture." He observes how "clerical politics" has made of the Christian mystique what "radical politics" has made of the republican mystique, comparing the degradation of the saints by the clergy to that of the heroes by the parliamentarians. For Péguy the struggle over the Church and the Republic was at bottom a struggle over faith itself, and the laic school — by separating the spiritual from the political in a single movement of unbelief — risked drying up both the political and the Christian city. Anticlericalism, in this reading, was never merely a political programme but a spiritual event, and its legacy was ambiguous: it broke the Church's power while carrying forward the very faith it thought it had abolished.
