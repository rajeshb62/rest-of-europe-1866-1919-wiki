# The War Plans of August 1914

The strategic schemes of concentration and attack with which the great powers of Europe entered the war in the summer of 1914: the German plan of the great wheeling offensive against France through Belgium, the French Plan XVII, the double deployment of Austria-Hungary against Serbia and against Russia, and the reluctant Russian general mobilisation that set the whole machinery in motion. Together they defined the first weeks of the war — and, in the judgement of the soldiers who lived them, its very decision. For the decision of the war lay, as the chief of the Austro-Hungarian general staff [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]] put it, in the success or failure of the agreed first action; what followed was only a stubborn struggle to make good the consequences of that first action. The plans were thus not merely technical arrangements for the movement of armies but the instruments by which the war was, almost automatically, set loose upon Europe.

## The German problem of the two fronts

Germany, wedged between France and Russia, faced the problem of a war to be conducted on two fronts, east and west, almost from the founding of the Empire. Almost on the morrow of the victories of 1870 the elder Moltke, the victor, had reflected on it, seeing in it the most formidable test that could threaten the existence of the new German Empire. But the military weakness of France — which, despite the miraculous speed of its recovery, took a long time to reconstitute its forces — and the close friendship uniting the two empires, Russian and German, made the risk of a double war appear at first very light, almost non-existent. As soon as France had rebuilt its strength, and above all after the conclusion of the Franco-Russian alliance, the problem had to be taken up again and solved.

Germany was not strong enough to attack on both sides at once, east and west, and so must confine itself to the defensive on one front while seeking a rapid decision on the other. Moltke had first foreseen the defensive against the French and the offensive against the Russians, on whom important successes could be won quickly; his successor Waldersee kept that plan. When Schlieffen took direction of the great general staff the data of the problem had changed. The strength of the French army had grown, and the Russian army appeared difficult, if not impossible, to surprise and put out of action by a sudden attack. For these two reasons Schlieffen resolutely reversed the earlier conceptions: one would defend in the east and attack in the west, violently, irresistibly.

## The Schlieffen plan

Schlieffen, the type of the Germanic staff officer with his qualities and defects, steeped in military history acquired by methodical and obstinate labour, joined audacity and daring of design to a certain automatic rigidity. The ideal battle he meant to deliver and win against the principal adversary — the *Vernichtungsschlacht*, the battle of annihilation — he conceived on the model of Cannae, the victory of Hannibal over the Romans. The great Punic general, though his army was inferior in numbers, enveloped the Roman troops on both wings by the rapidity of his movements and cut them to pieces. This model seduced the German staff; its spirit was literally obsessed by the prestige of that famous day, regarded as the ideal victory, capable of ending the war in a single stroke. In these conceptions there was a certain dose of pedantry and even of puerility: what relation, after all, between an episode of war more than twenty centuries old, fought with very reduced forces, and the battles fought by millions of men that were to mark a modern war?

The offensive thus decided in the west, what form was it to take? The fortresses with which France had covered its eastern frontier appeared an obstacle difficult to overcome quickly, so Schlieffen decided to turn them, to carry his principal attack toward the north, where there were no fortresses, where the great plains of Flanders offered an excellent terrain for the deployment of armies, and where the rivers converging in the direction of Paris marked the natural, historic road of invasions. The German armies would cross Belgium from side to side and, by a vast enveloping movement as wide and overflowing as possible, would bring down all enemy resistance. *France must be regarded as a great place of strength*, he wrote in 1905, *whose outer enceinte, the sector Belfort–Verdun, is almost impenetrable; the front Mézières–Maubeuge–Lille–Dunkirk is certainly fortified, but with breaches, and at present almost unoccupied. It is there that we must attempt the irruption into the fortress.*

For the plan to succeed, the German right wing had to be as powerful, as formidable as possible. This was obtained by confining the whole extent of the front to a strict defence, so as to assign the maximum of forces to the right armies charged with the decisive blows. The forces provided for the right wing stood in the ratio of seven to one with those destined for the left wing in Alsace-Lorraine. Schlieffen desired to give the greatest possible extension to his enveloping movement, pushing his manœuvre, if necessary, to outflank Paris to the west, to cross the Seine downstream of its confluence with the Oise, and so to encircle the capital not only from the west but from the south. The plan was characterised by the hardiness, the audacity, of its conceptions — a hardiness perhaps too great, the ratio of one to seven appearing excessive, for it supposed an almost complete inertia on the part of the enemy in Lorraine.

## The German method and its fateful assumptions

The conception rested on a psychology drawn from Prussia's own past. Prussia had acquired its prestige in the Seven Years' War, in 1866 against Austria, in 1870–71 against France, and in each of these three wars it had to do with adversaries almost entirely passive. From these successive experiences, always turning to its advantage, its staff had drawn certain habits of mind leading it to believe that the enemy would always be as inert. The German method, moreover, was that of the elder Moltke, which consisted in giving the executants general directives derived from an over-all plan and then letting them fend for themselves as best they could — a method that Foch had condemned in his lectures at the École de Guerre, demonstrating its errors and dangers as leading to a disordered, incoherent battle not directed by its chief. *The German strategy makes bankruptcy*, Foch said, in one of his lapidary phrases; *it is saved only by the tactics.*

Moltke the nephew, who in 1914 applied the same principles and methods on a far greater scale, let the bridle slip to his lieutenants, who soon came to do as they pleased — above all the most enterprising of them, von Kluck, charged with the essential mission of turning and beating the left wing of the French armies. There was, in the German spirit, something mechanical, automatic, frozen, that accommodated itself ill to the complex and shifting realities of life. The application of these methods would lead to the same errors as in 1870, on a far vaster scale. Only then, whereas in 1870 the French high command had been incapable of profiting by the German errors through its own insufficiency and mediocrity, in 1914, on the contrary, it profited by them and drew victory out of them. The whole moral, the whole explanation, the whole contrast of the two wars lay there.

## The French Plan XVII

On the French side the plan bore the name of Joffre, who as generalissimo was its author. Invested in the summer of 1911, during the Agadir crisis, with the two functions of chief of the general staff and vice-president of the superior council of war, he had carried responsibility for the strategic plan entirely when the war broke out. The plan XVII, so called after the series that preceded it — plan XVI, plan XV, and so on, as with the kings of France — did not, as the public supposed, regulate the operations and the offensive of the armies, but almost exclusively their mobilisation first, their concentration afterwards. From 1870 to 1914, as France increased its military power, the plans felt the direct effect of this increase. Purely defensive at first, they took on an ever more offensive character, especially after the conclusion of the Franco-Russian alliance; and from 1900 to 1911, under the impulse of Foch at the École de Guerre, the partisans of a reasoned, reflective offensive won the day over those of the defensive.

All the efforts of the high command since 1870 had borne on the rapidity of mobilisation, permitting the concentration to be made as close as possible to the frontiers, in order to avoid, from the very beginning of hostilities, the invasion of the national soil. Joffre, an engineer by mind and temperament, an excellent organiser who possessed to the full the handling of railways, attached himself to rendering mobilisation more rapid still, to carrying the concentration forward, and above all to better preparing the rapid use of the reserves. These considerations led him to propose, on 18 April 1913, a new plan — plan XVII — which, approved by the superior council of war and by the government, came into force in the spring of 1914. It provided, in its great lines: on the right a mass (the First and Second Armies) between the Vosges and the Hauts-de-Meuse; a central army (the Third) in the region of the Hauts-de-Meuse; on the left a mass (the Fifth Army, extended eventually by the British) in the region of Rethel and Mézières; and a second-line army (the Fourth), concentrated initially toward Bar-le-Duc, called upon to reinforce, as circumstances dictated, the mass of the Ardennes or the mass of Lorraine.

The intention of the French commander-in-chief was to take up the offensive when all forces were gathered. But he took no definitive decision as to the zone of the principal effort; the modalities of the offensive were to be fixed according to the information that came in on the disposition realised by the enemy and the intentions of his high command. Thus the offensive conception of Joffre — reserved and opportunist — contrasted in striking fashion with the audacious and imperative schema of Schlieffen.

## The French knowledge of the German plan

The invasion of Belgium was perfectly foreseen by the French high command. Indeed, from 1904 an officer of the German great general staff, the *Vengeur* — the name by which he signed his letters — had sold the German strategic plan, providing for the passage of the German armies across Belgian territory, to the French for sixty thousand gold francs. The episode was dramatic: the plan was bought for cash from a traitor of the German staff. The violation of Belgium was therefore foreseen; what was debated was how far the enveloping movement would extend — whether it would stop at the Meuse or overflow beyond it.

The French staff did not believe that the Germans would extend their movement beyond the Meuse. The forces at their disposal from the beginning, it was thought, did not permit them to give so great an extension to their front. Nor did the French staff take into account the German reserve corps, estimating that the enemy would not use them immediately, on the same footing as the active corps, of which they had neither the training nor the power in artillery. Wilhelm II had once said: *No fathers of families in the front line!* The French staff had excellent arguments for not believing in the immediate use of the German reserve corps — though the use presented inconveniences as well as advantages, and the Germans themselves were not all agreed on the point. They decided finally to employ them from the first days, which struck the calculations of the French staff with error. It was this that Joffre, a good loser if ever there was one, himself acknowledged, writing in his memoirs: *It must be confessed: the use the Germans made in August of their reserve army corps was a surprise for us, and this surprise is at the origin of...* — the sentence breaking off at the very point where the consequences of the error began.

## The Austro-Hungarian deployment against Serbia and Russia

For Austria-Hungary the plans were complicated by the two-fold object of the war. The diplomatic action aimed only at the war against Serbia; for this, mobilisation and the deployment against Serbia and Montenegro, the full mobilisation *B*, had to be ordered. Yet the danger existed that Russia would fall upon the Monarchy, threaten it with war and itself wage that war. Militarily it would have been the most desirable to meet this danger initiative-ly, to treat the war against Serbia as a side-issue and to open that against Russia; but to do this on the bare possibility of Russian intervention was impossible, for in such a course Austria-Hungary would have forfeited the duty of its allies to participate, and would have drawn upon itself the grave reproach of unleashing the world war, whereas the war against Serbia was only an act of brutally provoked self-defence.

For the diplomat the position was clear; not so for the chief of the general staff, who must have in view a war quickly and decisively conducted against Serbia, and yet be ready suddenly to turn everything to the war against Russia. It was a dilemma that carried the most oppressive responsibility and created the operationally most unfavourable position, all the more as the anxiety arose that the drawing-out of the diplomatic action, and with it of the beginning of the war, would give Serbia time for extensive preparations. To all this was joined the complete uncertainty over the attitude of the allies: it was cleared up favourably as regards Germany, but as to the behaviour of Italy and Rumania there were grave doubts. The changes made to the originally fixed deployment against Russia recorded the effect of this double object: the army had first to be strong against Serbia, then, with the turn of events, to face the Russian mass in Galicia. It was this double plan, and the later obligation to carry the main strength to the Russian front while the war against Serbia went on with reduced forces, that Conrad held to have vitiated the agreed first action from the outset.

## The Russian mobilisation

The Russian mobilisation, upon which the German historians made all their efforts bear, could not and must not, in all justice, be separated from the events that preceded and provoked it. It was only one fact in a complex web of facts, only one part in a whole. On the afternoon of 29 July the tsar, yielding to the reiterated demands of his general staff, justly alarmed at the military preparations of Austria and Germany, let the order for general mobilisation be torn from him, informing his ally France at once. That measure decided, he returned in the course of the evening on his resolution and ordered it to be postponed, following a personal telegram from Wilhelm II that seemed to leave him some hope of peace. The next day, 30 July, in the presence of the very bad news arriving from Vienna and Berlin, all his hopes vanished and he consented to sign, at four o'clock, the order for general mobilisation.

But was it then Russia that had begun, as the German historians would have it believed, the series of military decisions? Not at all. The Austrian government, fully in agreement with Germany, had sent to Serbia on 23 July an ultimatum of an unusual stiffness and brutality, leaving the Serbs only forty-eight hours to reply, although they had accepted on all points save one all the demands of Austria, however hard. The Austrian minister left the Serbian capital and diplomatic relations were broken off. After mobilising eight army corps without delay, the Austrian government declared war on Serbia two days after its minister left Belgrade — a declaration decided by Berchtold, the minister of foreign affairs, against the advice of the military, who wished to wait a little longer until their preparations were complete. Berchtold would not wait: he wished to put Europe as soon as possible before an accomplished fact, fearing that if he delayed he would no longer be able to evade the increasingly pressing demands for mediation from London, Paris and even St Petersburg.

## The chain of the plans

It was not the Russian mobilisation — dated only 30 July — but the Austrian declaration of war on Serbia, dated the 28th, that in the succession of events leading to the general conflagration marked the important, capital fact. Austria, deliberately rejecting all mediation and all pacific arrangement, meant to settle its quarrel with Serbia by arms alone, and Germany did nothing to hold it back but gave it, on the contrary, its full support, knowing of and approving both the ultimatum and the declaration of war. And it was this declaration of war that decided Russia to mobilise. Russia, a partial mobilisation presenting almost insurmountable technical difficulties because of the immense extent of its empire and the insufficiency of its railways, chose general mobilisation. The German plan, the French Plan XVII, the Austro-Hungarian double deployment, and the Russian mobilisation were thus not independent schemes but links in a single chain: each assumed and answered the other, and the clash of their timetables produced the war of masses that none of them, in the event, had calculated correctly. The war that was to have been short, by the exact calculation of the plans, instead embarked upon a long duration from the moment the German machine was half-dismantled at the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]].
