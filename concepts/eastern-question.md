# The Eastern Question

The Eastern Question — *die orientalische Frage* — is the European problem of the decline of the Ottoman Empire and the disposition of its territories, above all in the Balkans: the great standing issue of European diplomacy from the eighteenth century down to the world war. It is the umbrella concept of this period's eastern history, the thread that runs unbroken from the Turkish wars of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, through the Crimean War and the Congress of Berlin, to the annexation crisis of 1908 and the [[balkan-wars-1912-13]] that set Europe on the road to 1914. Within its frame the Powers of Europe, and above all the Habsburg Monarchy and Russia, contended for the inheritance of the sick man of Europe — a struggle that the Austrian historian Sosnosky took as the very subject of his account of the Monarchy's Balkan policy.

## Definition: the problem of the sick man of Europe

The Eastern Question arises from the circumstance that the Ottoman Empire, which once menaced Vienna itself, had for centuries been in retreat before the two empires that pressed it from the north, Austria and Russia. Its European provinces — the Balkans — were inhabited by Christian peoples increasingly conscious of their nationality and restive under Turkish rule; its government was weak, its finances broken, its hold on its lands precarious. The problem for Europe was what was to become of these lands. No single Power could be suffered to divide them alone: the whole history of the Eastern Question is the history of the efforts of the other Powers to prevent Russia, in particular, from seizing Constantinople and the Straits, and to keep the Ottoman state alive as the least of evils. Sosnosky's first volume opens with the Turkish wars in this exact frame — the age of Turkish attack to 1718, the age of Turkish defence from 1719 to 1792, and the century of Austrian policy from Kaunitz to Beust — establishing that the question of the Balkans is as old as the standing of the house of Austria.

## The Habsburg share: from the Turkish wars to Berlin

The Monarchy's part in the Eastern Question was peculiar. Thrown back by Königgrätz in 1866 out of Germany and Italy, it was left, as Sosnosky put it, only the Balkan as a field for its political activity; henceforward the Eastern Question was the Monarchy's question, the one great issue on which its future hung. Under Andrássy it held to a conserving policy, keeping the Ottoman Empire alive as long as possible and waiting for the hour of its death to claim a share of the inheritance. When Russia's victory in the Russo-Turkish war of 1877–78 threatened to partition Turkey at San Stefano, Andrássy intervened, saved the Porte from destruction, and carried the [[congress-of-berlin-1878]] which gave the Monarchy the mandate to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina. That settlement was the pivot of the Eastern Question in the century's last quarter: it acknowledged the principle that the disposition of the Turkish lands was a matter for the European concert, and it planted the Monarchy firmly in the South-Slav world.

## The new century: the question comes to a crisis

Through the decades that followed, the Eastern Question passed through the crises that Sosnosky's second volume chronicles — the Bulgarian crisis of 1885, the annexation crisis of [[bosnian-annexation-crisis-1908]], and the [[balkan-wars-1912-13]]. Each marked a further step in the decay of the Ottoman power and of the concert that had policed its decline. The annexation of Bosnia by Aehrenthal broke the letter of the Berlin settlement and embittered Serbia; the Balkan Wars completed what the congress had postponed, driving the Turks almost wholly out of Europe and doubling the size of Serbia. The old conserving answers of the Powers — the status quo, the concert, the keeping of the sick man alive — were exhausted, and the Eastern Question resolved itself into the Serbian question, which the Monarchy could no longer escape. When the shots of Sarajevo fired in 1914, the Eastern Question had become the world war.

## Current state: the question at the outbreak of war

In the frame of this corpus the Eastern Question is the governing problem of the years 1866–1919 in the east. Its solution, in the sense of a peaceful settlement, never came; what came instead was the violent liquidation of the Ottoman power in Europe and the collision of Austria-Hungary and Russia that ignited the world war. The concept is the wide circle within which the specifically Habsburg programme — the [[balkan-policy-austria]] — is the smaller, national ring: the Eastern Question is the problem of Europe as a whole, of which the Monarchy's rivalry with Serbia, and its confrontation with Russia, are the decisive expression. Related to it stand the [[congress-of-berlin-1878]], the [[occupation-of-bosnia-1878]], the [[bosnian-annexation-crisis-1908]], and the [[balkan-wars-1912-13]], with the [[july-crisis-1914]] as its terrible conclusion.
