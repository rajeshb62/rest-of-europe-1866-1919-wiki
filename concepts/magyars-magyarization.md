# The Magyars and Magyarization

The Magyar national policy, and the linguistic Magyarization (*magyarizálás*, "Magyarizing") that accompanied it, was the attempt to consolidate the Hungarian kingdom as a Magyar national state within the lands of the crown of St. Stephen, pressing its subject peoples — Germans, Romanians, Slovaks, Ruthenians, Serbs and Croats — toward the Hungarian language and nation. Two contemporary witnesses frame the question from opposite sides. Gusztáv Beksics, the Hungarian publicist, wrote to defend and advance the Magyar national consolidation; Theodor von Sosnosky, the German publicist in Vienna, wrote to condemn the Magyar national state and its treatment of the non-Magyar peoples. Together their works give the fullest contemporary account of the theory and practice of Magyarization, and of the nationalities question that strained the Hungarian half of the monarchy toward 1914.

## The Magyar national state

Sosnosky opens his account of Hungarian politics with a portrait of the "Magyar political nation" — its passionate, domineering temper, its self-absorption, and its attitude toward the non-Magyar peoples of the kingdom. The conception of the "Magyar national state" (*magyar nemzeti állam*) was, he argues, the short-sighted, perverse and intolerant idea that governed all of Hungary, standing in the sharpest contrast to the far-sighted, wise and tolerant view that had originally prevailed. He recalls that the founder, St. Stephen, ruled on the maxim that a kingdom of one language and one custom is weak and fragile, and had encouraged the immigration of foreign, chiefly German, elements to bring culture into the land; the successors and even the later dynasties had done the same. Only toward the end of the eighteenth century did an expressly national movement arise in Hungary, and the irony of history, as Sosnosky notes, was that the Germanising measures of Kaiser Joseph II strengthened the Magyar national feeling — whereupon the same Magyars who had resented the attempt to rob them of their nationality began to do to their non-Magyar fellow-citizens exactly what had been done to them.

## The instruments of Magyarization

The pressure operated through the school, the courts, the electoral system, and the administration. The Nationalities Law (Act XLIV of 1868) had, in Sosnosky's phrase, subjected the non-Magyars of Hungary to an "arbitrary lumping together of the five peoples of Hungary into one indivisible unitary Hungarian nation," an "assault on their national individuality." It provided some hope of a dam against the Magyar flood; but the dam proved not tight enough, and the Magyar high flood, seeping through slowly but irresistibly, "washed away one national possession after another." The Magyars, with the "artful petifogging" that was their habit in national questions, reinterpreted and circumvented the inconvenient law, or where that was impossible violated it. In the schools the non-Magyar languages were pressed back in favour of the Magyar; officials ignorant of the languages they served had to sign the most important documents in Hungarian; pastors who had grown grey in their German or Slav service had to issue baptismal and other certificates in a language they did not understand, while Magyar priests were set over German or Slav congregations whose tongue they did not know, and every attempt at resistance was punished in a truly barbarous fashion. The result was that Hungarian patriotism degenerated into a "linguistic monomania." Those who had only imperfectly acquired the Magyar tongue — the half-Magyarized, whom Sosnosky calls the *Talmi-Magyaren* (from the gilded imitation gold) — he numbers at about a million. The principle of the identification of nation with language governed the whole policy.

## Beksics and the agrarian foundation

Beksics, writing from within the Hungarian national camp in 1896, approached the question from a different and striking direction. His *A magyar faj terjeszkedése* (The expansion of the Hungarian race) argued that linguistic assimilation had failed: "linguistically we are incapable of assimilating the nationalities," he declared — three decades of constitutional rule and a national cultural policy had produced, so far as linguistic absorption was concerned, very poor results, and in places there had even been positive regression. The national consolidation, in his view, could not be achieved through language alone; it must rest on the free expansion of the Hungarian race, and above all on agriculture and the creation of a Hungarian peasant proprietorship. He pointed to the alarming phenomenon that in the very counties that showed the tremendous force of the race the number of births was now falling or stagnating, and argued for the division of the great estates to create a numerous landed peasantry as the means of expansion. The two works thus represent the two poles of the question: the language-policy that the national state had pursued, and the demographic and agrarian foundation that a reformer within the national camp believed was the true basis of survival.

## The working of the national state

Sosnosky treats in turn each of the subject peoples — the Germans, the Romanians, the Slovaks, the Ruthenians, and the Serbs — and the working of the Magyar national state upon each, together with the position of Croatia-Slavonia under the *nagodba* of 1868. He insists that the Magyarizing system, by the measureless hatred it had roused in the peoples who suffered under it, had prepared the downfall of the very Magyar domination it was meant to secure: "at the day when Hungary tore itself loose and won its full independence, its ruin would also begin," for the peoples would seize the favourable moment to free themselves. It was, he wrote, a tragicomic spectacle to see the Magyars "desire nothing more hotly, strive after nothing more vehemently than their own downfall." Redlich, writing after the war, echoed the judgement from the Austrian side, describing the perpetual keeping-down of the nationalities by the Magyar gentry and the "complete political helotisation" that the Romanian people of Transylvania had learned to regard as its permanent fate, and judged that only an unsuccessful war or the dynasty's own action could have broken the supremacy of the Magyars — a supremacy the war itself at last dissolved.
