# Second Empire, France (1852–1870)

The Second Empire was the regime founded by Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte after the coup of 2 December 1851 and proclaimed empire in 1852, overthrown by the catastrophe of the war with Prussia in September 1870. For the Republic that followed it, the Empire was the negative term against which the whole of the new regime defined itself: a dictatorship of personal power, of plebiscitary appeals to the people, and — in its last years — of a liberal transformation that came too late to save it. Taxile Delord's opposition *Histoire du second empire* and Émile Ollivier's *L'Empire libéral* memoir record the regime from inside its arc of authoritarian strength and liberal decay.

## The authoritarian Empire and its contradictions

The Empire inherited the Napoleonic name and, with it, the promise of the first Napoleon's glory. Hanotaux, writing of the Emperor on the eve of the Prussian war, calls Napoleon III "a reduced copy of the first Bonaparte" — the dynastic heir who ruled by a mixture of plebiscitary democracy and personal authority, appealing over the heads of assemblies directly to universal suffrage. The authoritarian system rested on the *Corps législatif* as a docile instrument, on a controlled press, and on the figure of Eugène Rouher, whom Ollivier's republican enemies and even Ollivier himself dubbed the *vice-empereur*. The Senate was a conservative counterweight; the ministries answered to the sovereign rather than to the Chamber.

Delord's account of the years 1867–1869 shows the system beginning to crack. The universal Exhibition of 1867 brought the sovereigns of Europe to Paris in a display of imperial splendour, yet the same season brought the failure in Mexico, the execution of the Archduke Maximilian at Querétaro, and the first shots of a resurgent opposition. The regime's foreign-policy reverses — the Luxembourg neutralisation after Sadowa, the retreat from Rome's guarantee challenged at Mentana — combined with financial and food crises to produce what Delord calls the first symptoms of the Empire's *decadence*. A new "party of action" rose among republican youth, the revolutionary Commune of the Paris workers was styled in the agitation, and Henri Rochefort's pamphlet *La Lanterne* sold on the boulevards at ten centimes, prosecuted, condemned, and succeeded by an ever-wider hostile press.

## The authoritarian Empire's liberal turn

The years 1867–1869 were marked by a measured liberalisation that the Emperor hoped would reconcile the educated middle classes without surrendering real power. The letter of 19 January 1867 replaced the unvarying address with a regulated right of interpellation and announced the press and public-meetings laws, which came fully into force in 1868. The new press regime abolished discretionary control and let loose the torrent of hostile journalism; the public-meetings law opened the first political meetings of the Second Empire. In the session of 1868–1869 Thiers demonstrated, as Ollivier records, "the need to restore the constitutional regime," while Rouher protested and resisted every advance.

It was precisely the half-conceded liberties that exposed the regime's dilemma. Delord presents the election of 1869 as a condemnation of the authoritarian Empire represented by Rouher and a victory for the programme of the liberal Empire associated with Ollivier; the complementary elections of late 1869 and the extraordinary session at the Louvre saw the dissolution of the government majority and the collapse of the authoritarian system. The Empire could no longer stand still: it had to recede into pure authoritarianism or advance into parliamentary government. Out of that impasse came the negotiations between the Emperor and Ollivier through Clément Duvernois, the disguised journey to Compiègne, and the ministry of 2 January 1870 that inaugurated the Liberal Empire.

## The Republic against the Empire

When the Empire fell after Sedan and the revolution of 4 September 1870, the Republic that rose from the ruins defined its legitimacy in opposition to the Bonapartist practice of personal power. The Third Republic's founders insisted on a regime of assemblies, of ministerial responsibility, and of a presidency reduced to a figurehead — precisely the inverse of the imperial *régime personnel*. Hanotaux's history of the Republic is framed throughout as the liquidation of the Bonapartist past and the erection, in its place, of the parliamentary Republic. The Republic's consolidation through Thiers, through the failure of the monarchical restoration, and through the Constitution of 1875 was, in this reading, the definitive answer to the Second Empire's promise of order and glory through one man.
