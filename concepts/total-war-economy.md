# The Total War Economy

The *Kriegswirtschaft*, the war economy — the immense system of state direction and organisation of production, consumption and trade into which the belligerent nations of the First World War transformed their economic life. Recouly and the other observers of the war recognised the mass war as a new phenomenon, one that drew into itself the whole material and spiritual strength of the peoples; and the Austrian administrator and scholar [[josef-redlich]] gave the concept its sharpest form in his study of the war government. The war economy, he wrote, was the inevitable consequence of the modern popular war, and the scientific description of it was the true centre for the understanding of the modern phenomenon of the mass war. In Austria-Hungary it took the double shape of a state food administration and a network of industrial war organisations, bound together into what contemporaries came to call the *state socialism of the war*, and it issued, at the close, in the doctrine of the *Übergangswirtschaft*, the transitional economy, that must carry the state from war to peace.

## The war economy as the consequence of the mass war

At the opening of the war the peaceful system of world economy, in the first days of August 1914, was abolished. In its place arose a wholly peculiar condition, first of all Europe and soon of the overseas cultural states. The Central Powers and their two eastern allies were forced from the very beginning into a system of economic autarky, whose most characteristic feature was the hard necessity imposed by the blockade conducted by the Western powers, England at their head. Austria-Hungary, like Germany, was by the blockade and by the specifically economic manner of warfare developed under its pressure in the first war year thrown into a state of complete economic isolation. It was in the face of this isolation that the whole apparatus of the war economy was constructed, as the means by which the state undertook, from month to month more extensively, the care for the feeding of the population and the direction and leadership of the whole agricultural and industrial production for the feeding of the army and the supply of all kinds of war material.

## The blockade and the economic autarky

Redlich's account insists that Austria-Hungary and Germany, blockaded from without, had no choice but to organise their economic life under state direction. The blockade and the economic warfare of the Entente, together with the neutrals morally and politically dominated by them, cut the Central Powers off from the world market and their accustomed sources of supply. Czernin, from the other side of the war, records the same fact from the standpoint of the Central Powers' own experience: the hunger blockade of Austria was carried on throughout the war, and millions in the territories of the Central Powers perished by the blockade. It was the blockade, in Redlich's analysis, that formed the specifically central-power character of the war economy — an autarkic, planned economy of scarcity, built on the state's monopoly over food and raw materials.

## The food administration

The care for the feeding of the people was the government's concern from the very outset of the war, and it became, month by month, the largest of all the agendas of state administration in wartime. Austria's position was rendered the more difficult by the conduct of Hungary, which sealed its frontier against every export of foodstuffs to Austria, reducing Austria to a state of complete economic isolation by its own ally. The decisive step came in the spring of 1915, when, with the sealing of the Hungarian frontier carried out with full severity, Vienna resolved to establish the unbroken state monopoly over grain and over the whole procedure of the production of flour and bread and their distribution to the population — the first and most eminent type of that state-economic institution which characterised the whole war economy of Austria as well as of Germany.

By some decrees armed with the force of law, together with the declaration of the blockade over all grain and flour products and the taking of stocks, the principle of the rationing of bread and flour was fixed and the institution of the bread card created. At the same time the *Kriegsgetreideverkehrsanstalt*, the war-grain traffic institute, was called into being as the central organ of all these measures; it had to organise and carry through the now-introduced state monopoly of the grain and flour economy. The whole burden of this new administrative task, growing out of the war economy, was laid on the state administrative authorities in the individual crown-lands and their attached provincial branch institutions of the central institute on the one side, and on the local communes, as the organs legally called to the immediate administration, on the other. The whole edifice, which lasted to the end of the war and in Austria years beyond it, was complete in 1915. Alongside it was created the *Amt für Volksernährung*, the office for popular nutrition, the new central authority for the food system; and new legislation over market traffic in the most indispensable articles of consumption was built up, with the duty of delivery on the part of primary producers and the fixing of maximum prices as its foundations.

## The price policy and its consequences

The war economy was, in its very nature, a price-police. The principle of maximum prices was first fixed in the grain economy, then extended in Austria one after another to all agricultural produce — a policy of consumer protection that led inevitably to a continual reduction of production. This policy of fixing maximum prices, in combination with the state rationing of foodstuffs, drew after it very important, partly fateful consequences for the whole development of the war economy. For although the low price of flour and bread in favour of the masses was achieved, these prices sank after a time below the world-market price of the cereals and partly below the self-costs of the native producer of grain. The consequence was that the spur to the increase of grain production in Austria grew ever weaker, and a sinking of the agrarian production of Austria-Hungary set in. In connection with the fixing of maximum prices there were constantly ordered new economies, new aggravations of the stretching regulations — a progressive worsening of flour and bread.

## The industrial war economy and the Wirtschaftszentralen

Beyond the food administration, the war economy was extended in the course of the first war year, under the pressure of the blockade, over the whole field of industrial production and trade. The same process of a peculiar nationalisation of production and of the regulation of consumption was accomplished over the whole field of industrial, mining and purely commercial activity within the entire state and imperial territory, in essentially the same form of the so-called *Zentrale* — the central organisation. The securing of raw materials for the war industry gave rise to an industrial war economy and the step-by-step abolition of free economic intercourse, first in the cotton industry, then in the metal industry; and to the rise of the special war associations (*Kriegsverbände*) of the several branches of industry and trade, with a progressive militarisation of industry through the administration of the centrales.

The great pattern of this organisation was the *Wirtschaftszentrale*, of which an official statistic published after the end of the war counted no fewer than ninety-one in all. Twenty of these had to do with agriculture and food; fifteen with the textile industry; eight with paper and printing; thirteen with the chemical, oil and fat industries; six with hides and leather; thirteen with the metal, machinery and technical-requirements industries; four with the industry of stones and earths; three with wood and cork; seven with trade and traffic in general; one, the *Devisenzentrale*, with the traffic in foreign means of payment; and finally the war organisation of the interests of the organised consumers. By their organisation, thirty-four of these were state compulsory organisations (*Kriegsverbände* and *Wirtschaftsverbände*) embracing all the members of a branch; nineteen were centrales and commissions whose members were branch representatives appointed by the government; fourteen were committees whose members were government-appointed; twenty-one were commercial companies registered and state-empowered; and the rest, including the war-grain traffic institute, the fodder-stuff office and the vegetable-and-fruit office of the Amt für Volksernährung, held a special position, combining an administrative division staffed by public officials with a business division for the commercial agendas.

The *Kriegsverbände* of the several industries represented state compulsory cartels, founded by ordinance, of which all undertakings processing the same raw material were compulsory members. Their tasks were the keeping of a production and consumption statistic, the use, administration and distribution of raw materials and half-fabricates, the making of applications in the fixing of prices, the advising of the central offices in the awarding of contracts, and cooperation in a whole series of economic questions — all purely administrative agendas, with the exclusion of every commercial activity. In this way the Austrian war government gradually drew to itself the direction of the whole economic life of Austria, both production and consumption, until at last the principle of freedom of economic intercourse occurred in the Austrian state territory only as a rare exception, or as a widely spread but forbidden function of the illicit trade, the *Schleichhandel*.

## The state socialism of the war

Redlich's culminating conception is the *state socialism of the war*. The middle position of the Austrian government between the army high command and the war ministry on one side and industrial production and the consuming population on the other, and the enormous extension of the whole war-economy apparatus and its capacity, produced a double organism of military and civil war-economy leadership. The war ministry created a new *Kriegswirtschaftsamt* (war-economy office), divided into an executing group, a scientific committee for the war economy, and a statistical group; this was the military central organ for the whole war economy, the war-economy staff, under whose influence all other departments of the war ministry had to discuss and work out the war-economy arrangements with the allied and neutral states. The whole process of the daily war economy ran as an unprecedentedly powerful giant machine that admitted no delay and no resistance — a machine which, in its never-stopping activity, subjected men and things, personal achievements, raw materials and the end products of industry, all alike, to the commanding behest of the endlessly continuing war, and which at its height might be compared to a mechanism in which the army in the field was the great motive power, transmitting itself through the wheel-work of the war ministry to the civil governments of both states of the Monarchy — in Austria through the two great wheels of the ministry of commerce and the Amt für Volksernährung.

The theory of the war economy, developed in Germany and Austria, rested on an older Austrian tradition: the bureaucratic state socialism that had been built up in the later nineteenth century, in the years of the Taaffe regime, through the productive employment of the impulses coming from Germany, and which the war now extended enormously. What was new in the war was the principle that the state must direct the whole economic life for the ends of the war, and the confidence that the new machinery, once created, could be preserved and turned to the service of the peace.

## The Übergangswirtschaft

From the theory of the war economy there was developed, as the war drew on, the doctrine of the necessity of a transitional economy, the *Übergangswirtschaft*, at the coming of peace. It was recognised that the war had wrought such deep changes in the whole economic life of the peoples that one must reckon, after the close of the war, with a shorter or, in all probability, longer period of transition from the war economy to a newly established system of free economy. In this period of transition the state would have to reserve to itself, if not all the powers of wartime, then most of them, in the interest both of the state and of a healthy renewal of the peaceful economic life and the preservation of the social peace between the different classes of the population.

From this standpoint Richard Riedl in 1916 drew up a great plan of provision for the tasks of the transitional economy, laid down in his *Denkschrift über die Aufgaben der Übergangswirtschaft*, the memorandum on the tasks of the transitional economy. Its governing idea — adopted by the Austrian government — was that the complete reorganisation of industry brought about by the war economy should, so far as possible, be maintained for the purposes of the restoration of civil economic life after the war, and be made serviceable for the adaptation of industry and trade to the new conditions of returning to peaceful work. Above all, it was argued, the state power must continue to intervene regularly in price formation even after the war: in the war the free price formation of trade had been in wide measure replaced by official price-fixings, and from the foodstuffs outward, maximum prices had been decreed for an ever-growing number of articles, which the defects of this kind of price-determination then drove on to the seizure of stocks, the regulation of production and a uniform administration under the points of view of the public interest, until the whole system converged upon the goal of the transfer of ever-greater parts of economic life into a state of planned, regulated administration.

The high point of this intellectual development in Austria was the creation of the *Generalkommissariat für Kriegs- und Übergangswirtschaft*, the general commissariat for the war and transitional economy. The ideas developed in it were connected, as Redlich notes, with the movement founded by Friedrich Naumann for the creation of a *Mitteleuropa* — a central Europe resting on a political and economic unity under Prussian-German leadership, in which the war-economy organisation was to be preserved and extended into a permanent union of the Central Powers. The whole conception was set out in the Denkschrift of the ministry of commerce of 1917, which surveyed the rise and development of the war economy and proposed its continuation.

## The failure of the war economy

Yet the war economy in Austria ended in failure. Its collapse was the consequence of the overstraining of the physical, moral and spiritual forces of the population and of the excessive length of the war. The war loans, which the government raised with a wholly new mechanism of subscription drawn up in the widest possible extent, through the collaboration of the government with communes, chambers of commerce, insurance institutions, savings-banks, cooperative associations and above all the banks, and promoted by the whole apparatus of propaganda and agitation — ever more imposing subscription results being brought up to the last war loan of 1918 — drew after them, in connection with the inflation of the currency, the heaviest economic and social damages. The state war economy, which was to have been the instrument of victory, overreached itself: the population could no longer bear the price, and the *Kriegswirtschaft* of Austria perished with the Monarchy that had created it.
