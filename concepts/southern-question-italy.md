# The Southern Question (questione meridionale)

The Southern Question — in Italian the *questione meridionale* — is the term under which the post-unification Italian state and its intellectuals confronted the economic and social backwardness of the Mezzogiorno (the "South") as opposed to the centre and north of the peninsula. Born in the decades after 1860, when the Kingdom of Italy annexed the Two Sicilies and the Papal states and found itself governing regions of enormous diversity, the question was the running indictment of unification as a merely political act: the South had entered the national state by plebiscite and annexation, but its peasant agriculture, its communal tenures, its malaria, its emigration, and its crushing fiscal burden had not been integrated into a common prosperity. No single figure articulated the question more fully than [[giustino-fortunato]], the deputy for Melfi whose collected speeches, *Il Mezzogiorno e lo stato italiano* (1880–1910), gave the problem its classic statement in parliament; and the brigandage of the 1860s, described by [[alfredo-oriani]], supplied the violent first chapter of the Southern story.

## The tributary system and the burden of the South

At the centre of Fortunato's analysis stood the finances of the state. The unified kingdom adopted the Piedmontese fiscal and administrative system, and applied it to the South with a uniformity that the South's poverty could not bear. Fortunato devoted a famous discourse of 21 January 1882 to the *leggi d'eccezione nel sistema tributario*, the "exception laws in the tributary system" by which the taxation of the Southern provinces exceeded both their capacity and the services they received; and his appendix to the discourse *Il dovere politico* (9 October 1898) documented the tributary burden of the Mezzogiorno before and after unification, arguing that the South paid to the state more than it drew from it. The central government, with its seat in the north, spent on the army, the navy, and the administration, while the poor provinces of the South furnished the money without receiving the roads, the schools, the irrigation, or the credit that might have raised them.

## Brigandage and the demanial lands

The first and most violent form of the Southern problem was brigandage. Oriani, describing the reaction that followed the annexation of the South, records how the "bandit habit" of the *camorra* and the *mafia* favoured the organisation of the brigands, how the fallen Bourbon clergy and aristocracy encouraged them, and how the Papal state was a refuge and arsenal for the bands — a revolt that combined regional hatred of the new conquerors, feudal reaction, and the misery of the countryside. Fortunato, writing of the demanial lands, exposed the deeper cause: the great common and feudal domains of the South, the *demanio* "feudal or universal," had never been divided among the peasantry, and the new legislation kept them locked or sold them in ways that served the speculators rather than the peasants. The reduction of the local magistracies, the neglect of credit, and the ruin of the grain banks (*monti frumentari*) completed the picture of a countryside denied the instruments of improvement.

## Malaria and emigration

Two further themes round out the question as Fortunato posed it in the closing years of his collection. Malaria, the endemic fever of the lowlands and coasts of the South, wasted the population and the land; Fortunato's last published discourse, *Malaria e chinino* (30 June 1910), argued for the campaign of quinine and drainage as among the few remedies the state could bring to the Mezzogiorno. And emigration, the great current of the last decades of the century, was treated in *L'emigrazione meridionale* (30 June 1909) as an economic necessity rather than a moral evil — a natural exodus of the poor to the Americas and beyond, which relieved but did not solve the South's distress, and which Fortunato refused to blame or forbid. The question, in his telling, was structural: it would yield only to the reform of the state's finances, credit, and public works in the South, and not to colonial adventure or military glory abroad.

## The Southern Question in the state's politics

The Southern Question was also a political question. It framed the relationship of the South to the parliamentary regime, the *[[trasformismo]]* that turned southern deputies into courtiers of the premier, and the colonial policy that diverted the nation's strength to Africa while the South starved — the policy Fortunato opposed after the disaster of [[battle-of-adua-1896]], urging "absolute recollection" in Eritrea and reform at home. For Fortunato and the meridionalist school, the South was not a separate land to be annexed or punished but the measure of the state's failure: a unified Italy that left its poorest provinces tributary, malarial and depopulated had not truly become one nation. The question, first posed in these parliamentary decades, would outlast the period and become one of the permanent themes of Italian public life.
