# The Zemstvo and the Great Reforms

The Great Reforms (*Великія реформы*) are the complex of measures carried through by [[alexander-ii]] in the 1860s that transformed the old Russia of serfdom and seigneurial autocracy — above all the emancipation of the serfs (1861) and the creation of local self-government in the institution of the zemstvo (*земство*, "land assembly"). Within the frame of this corpus they are the given starting-point of Russian internal history after 1866: the works of the reforming era against which the reaction of the last reigns was directed, the institutions through which educated Russia learned the habit of public life, and the seedbed of both the constitutional movement and the medical and educational work that bound the intelligentsia to the people. Their character is double — at once a genuine grant of self-government and a strictly circumscribed one, and the story of the period is largely the story of how the government first created and then fought its own creation.

## Definition: local self-government in the reforming era

The zemstvo institutions, established by the statute of 1864, were elected local assemblies — provincial (*gubernskie*) and district (*uezdnye*) — entrusted with the economic and social business of the countryside: roads, public charity, education, medicine, statistics, and the raising of local rates and dues to pay for them. Together with the new judicial statutes, the reformed municipal government (the Municipal Statute of 1870), and the military reform of Miliutin (universal military service, 1874), they formed the institutional edifice of the Great Reforms. Their significance lay less in the power they held — which was narrow — than in the principle they embodied: that a part of the public business of the empire was to be conducted by elected representatives of local society rather than by officials of the crown. This was, in embryo, a breach in the autocratic monopoly of government, and it was the reason both the reformers cherished the zemstvo and the reactionaries distrusted it.

## The sphere of zemstvo activity and its limits

Kornilov's lectures on the zemstvo institutions of 1866–1878 lay out both the work and its boundaries. The zemstvos were given a sphere of activity and the means to pursue it — the right to levy dues on land and, increasingly, their own budgets — but their competence was confined to local economic matters and expressly excluded the political. From the outset a collision arose over money: the zemstvos, needing funds, sought to tax trade and industry, and here they ran headlong into the government, which would not see the state's fiscal monopoly invaded by elected bodies. The class character of the zemstvos was marked — the landowners dominated, and the peasant members were few and overshadowed. Yet within their sphere they were active: the zemstvo school spread elementary education across the countryside against the resistance of the Ministry of Public Instruction, and the zemstvo physicians built the public medicine that Granat's essay in this corpus chronicles. It was this productive activity, and the public spirit it fostered, that made the zemstvo at once the government's most useful auxiliary and its most dangerous rival.

## The government's struggle with the zemstvo

The reaction that opened in 1866 and hardened under [[alexander-iii]] was in large part a struggle against the zemstvo. The government distrusted the zemstvos' claim to tax trade and industry, restricted their field of activity, and fought the spread of the zemstvo school, which the Ministry of Public Instruction feared as a nursery of sedition. When the zemstvo movement, stirred by the [[russo-turkish-war-1877-78]] and the Eastern Question, began to address the government with demands for reform, the breach widened: the zemstvo addresses of 1878–79 were answered with displeasure rather than concession. Under Alexander III the reaction turned to reconstruction. The statute of 12 June 1890 recast the zemstvo to secure the preponderance of the nobility and tighten the governor's supervision over its proceedings; the peasants were reduced to indirect election through their own bodies; and the zemstvo, while it kept its name and much of its work, lost much of the independence that had made it the school of the constitutional movement. The land captains (*zemskie nachalniki*) of the law of 1889 carried the government's hand still deeper into the village, subjecting the peasantry to the supervision of officials appointed from above. The reforming creation of the 1860s had been bent to the service of the order it was meant to leaven.

## Current state: the seedbed of reform

In the frame of this corpus the zemstvo appears as the enduring institution through which the reform era lived on into the age of reaction. Though its political voice was suppressed, its practical work continued and grew — the zemstvo medicine that Granat's Kanel praises as the foundation of Russian public sanitation, the schools, the statistics, the famine relief of the disastrous early 1890s. And it remained the natural home of the educated society that, denied the political institutions of the west, found in local self-government the one arena of legitimate public action. From the zemstvo movement grew the constitutional agitation that in 1905 forced the [[october-manifesto-1905]] and the [[duma-constitutionalism]]; many of the men who sat in the Dumas had learned their politics in the zemstvo assemblies. The Great Reforms and their zemstvo stand, then, as the positive achievement of the period that the reaction could curtail but never undo — the foundation on which the liberal and constitutional Russia of the new century was built. Related to it stand [[alexander-ii]], the Tsar-Liberator who created it; its opposite and adversary, the [[autocracy-russia]]; and the [[duma-constitutionalism]] that at last gave Russian society a central representative body.
