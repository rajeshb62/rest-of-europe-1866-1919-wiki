# Russian Autocracy

The Russian autocracy — *samoderzhavie* (самодержавіе, "self-rule") — is the political principle by which the Emperor of All the Russias held his power in his own person, owing it to no constitution, no parliament, and no elected body, but directly to God alone. Within the frame of this corpus it is the central institutional fact of Russian politics from the reaction that opened after 1866 to the collapse of the throne in the [[february-revolution-1917]]: the object of the Great Reforms' cautious experiments, the creed that the reactionary counter-reformers reaffirmed with a new militancy, the thing the constitutional movement sought to limit, and the foundation whose undermining Denikin traced through the ruin of the army and the state. It is not merely a form of government but an ideology — an idea of Russia's fitness for monarchical rule defended with the fullest seriousness by its great spokesman [[konstantin-pobedonostsev]] and, at the very end, restated by loyal churchmen as a religious faith.

## Definition: power by the grace of God

Autocracy is the principle that the supreme legislative and executive power is vested, without limitation, in the person of the monarch. Unlike a constitutional monarchy, in which the sovereign rules within a law that stands above him, in Russia the emperor was held to be himself the source of law, answerable to God and his own conscience rather than to any earthly body. The conception rested on the idea, as Zhevakhov restated it in the monarchy's last years, that Russian autocracy was "not a political but a religious" idea — the people's faith in the anointed tsar as the anointed of God being the true basis of power. In this view the tsar was not one authority among many but the personal embodiment of the state, and to divide his power was to strike at the religious foundation of Russia itself. This is the doctrine in its purest form; and it is the standard against which all the compromises of the last reigns must be measured, for each concession — the zemstvo, the courts, the Duma — was resisted precisely as a breach of the sacred unity of power.

## The reactionary reaffirmation, 1866–1881

Kornilov dates the reassertion of the autocratic principle in its aggressive modern form from the reaction that followed the Karakozov attempt of 4 April 1866. The shot that narrowly missed [[alexander-ii]] turned the temper of the highest governing circles: the reform programme of the 1860s was not repealed, but it was increasingly hemmed in by ministers convinced that it had gone too far. Count D. A. Tolstoy at the Ministry of Public Instruction pressed the classicism that was meant to discipline the young against sedition; the zemstvo found its field of activity narrowed; the new courts were gradually brought under the procuracy's hand. The philosophy behind this policy was the conviction that representative and self-governing institutions, however harmless in appearance, were hostile to the autocratic state. The reaction reached its decisive moment in 1881. When the assassins of the [[narodnaya-volya]] killed Alexander II on 1 March, in the very hour when Count Loris-Melikov was about to lay before him a project of representative participation, the question of Russia's future course was thrown open; and it was settled by the reactionaries' triumph.

## Pobedonostsev and the manifesto of 29 April

The man who settled it was Pobedonostsev, Ober-Procurator of the Holy Synod and former tutor of the new emperor [[alexander-iii]]. At the conference of 8 March 1881 he denounced the whole course of the previous reign — the zemstvos, the new courts, the press — as the work of "talking-shops" (*govorilni*), and warned that the proposed representative commission would be a "supreme talking-shop" preparing the ruin of Russia. Autocracy, in his teaching, was the only form of government fitted to the Russian people; elected institutions imported from the west could only breed discord. With the Moscow publicists Katkov and Aksakov he worked on the young emperor's instincts, and in secret, behind the backs of the reforming ministers, obtained the commission to draft a manifesto. On 29 April 1881 it was issued: the emperor announced that he was summoned by the voice of God to stand firmly to the work of government, in the trust of the strength and truth of autocratic power. The words were the programme of a reign; the whole of the following counter-reform — the land captains, the zemstvo statute of 1890, the restriction of the courts and the press — flowed from this reassertion that the emperor governed of himself and for himself, unbounded by the representative principle.

## The limits of concession: the constitutional experiment

The autocracy so reaffirmed proved, in the generation that followed, the stubborn obstacle to every attempt to give Russia a share of western constitutional order. Under [[alexander-iii]] the principle was held intact and even hardened, in the conviction that the reforms of the father's reign had been a mistake. Under [[nicholas-ii]] the strain became unbearable. The industrialization of the 1890s, the [[russo-japanese-war-1904-05]], and the [[revolution-1905]] at last forced concession: the [[october-manifesto-1905]] granted civil liberties and a legislative Duma, and the empire seemed to pass from autocracy to [[duma-constitutionalism]]. Yet even this was made as little real as possible. The Fundamental Laws of 1906, framed under Witte's premiership, preserved to the emperor the supreme autocratic power alongside the new Duma; and when the Second Duma proved refractory, Stolypin dissolved it by the coup of 3 June 1907 and rewrote the electoral law to secure a pliant chamber. The constitutional experiment was real in form, but the autocratic principle survived beneath it, and by 1914 the emperor had recovered the personal rule his grandfather had surrendered on paper. It was this unresolved contradiction — a state that could neither be fully autocratic nor become fully constitutional — that Denikin saw collapsing in 1917.

## Current state: the collapse of the principle

In the frame of this corpus the autocracy is a principle already under siege through the whole period and finally destroyed. Denikin's first volume, *The Collapse of Authority and the Army*, takes its title from the process by which the three pillars of the old order — "faith, tsar, and fatherland" — gave way in the months of 1917. The emperor had undermined his own throne by taking the supreme command in 1915 under the influence of the Empress's circle; the February revolution swept away the dynasty almost without resistance; and the attempt to rebuild the state on new foundations ended in the [[october-revolution-1917]]. The White movement that Denikin chronicles was in a real sense a last defence of the old autocratic order, though its generals knew the throne could not simply be restored. The concept stands in the corpus as the negative image of everything the constitutional and revolutionary movements sought: the fixed principle against which [[zemstvo-great-reforms]], the [[duma-constitutionalism]], and the revolutions of 1905 and 1917 were all, in their different ways, directed. Related to it stand [[konstantin-pobedonostsev]], its great apologist; [[alexander-iii]] and [[nicholas-ii]], its bearers; and the [[assassination-alexander-ii-1881]] that set its reactionary form in stone.
