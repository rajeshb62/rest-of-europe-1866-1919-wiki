Witte’s Industrialization

The industrialization of the 1890s and early 1900s is the programme of state-directed economic transformation associated with sergei-witte, minister of finance from 1892 to 1903: the gold currency, the spirits monopoly, the great railway-building, the harnessing of foreign capital, and the protectionist tariff system that together remade the Russian economy in a single generation. Within this corpus it is the bridge between the reactionary Russia of alexander-iii and the revolutionary Russia of nicholas-ii — the economic dynamism that modernized the country, that financed the railways and the treasury, and that at the same time piled on the population a burden of taxes and debt which the critics of the age, above all Granat’s financier Bogolepov, laid bare as the price of the “brilliant period” of state finances. It is the material foundation beneath the political story: the transformation that created the industrial working class, swelled the cities, and prepared the strains that burst forth in the revolution-1905.

The state-directed programme

Witte inherited and carried to its height the state’s leading role in the economy. His predecessors — Bunge and Vyshnegradsky under Alexander III — had already begun the fiscal centralization and protectionism that his system perfected. Witte’s conviction was that Russia, coming late to industry, could not wait for the slow organic growth of the western countries but must build its factories and railways rapidly by the deliberate action of the state. The treasury was the engine: state spending, state-guaranteed loans, state-ordered railway construction, and a tariff wall that shielded Russian industry from foreign competition while drawing in the foreign capital needed to build it. The programme was inseparable from the political order — it was the work of an autocratic ministry that could plan over the heads of the nation — and it expressed the belief that economic strength was the foundation of the empire’s greatness, the premise of its standing among the Powers.

The gold standard and the monetary reform

The centrepiece of the financial system was the monetary reform that made the ruble convertible on a gold basis. Laid down in its preparation still under Alexander III, it was carried through under Nicholas II by the ukases of 1897. The credit ruble, which had floated with the fortunes of the treasury, was stabilized at a fixed rate against gold; a new gold ruble was established by a kind of devaluation, fixed at the most stable existing rate of the paper ruble; and the State Bank was charged with the unfailing conversion of its notes into gold. The effect was to give Russia, for the first time, a hard and trusted currency on the western model, and to make it attractive to the foreign investor. Bogolepov’s essay in Granat is critical even here: the gold currency “cost the country” heavy sacrifices, and the reserve that underwrote it was supplied above all by foreign loans — the great French loans that bound Russia to her creditor and financed the system. Yet the reform worked: Russia’s money became as good as any in Europe, and the capital that had shunned an unstable paper currency now flowed in to build the railways and factories.

The spirits monopoly and the railways

To feed the treasury and the programme, Witte turned to indirect taxation and to the state’s own monopoly. The spirits monopoly (vinnaya monopoliya), reorganized from 1896, made the state the sole seller of drink — one of the largest and steadiest revenues of the empire, financing a great part of the state’s new expenditure. Alongside it stood the excises on tobacco, sugar, matches, and the raising of customs duties, and the state’s protectionist care of industries like sugar that it had chosen to favour. The other great engine was the railway. Witte, coming to the finance ministry from the South-Western Railway, knew the railways as no other minister did; under him the state built and bought lines on a scale unknown before, and above all pushed forward the greatest monument of the programme, the Trans-Siberian Railway, which carried the empire’s ambition into the Far East. The railways, the gold currency, and the monopoly formed one connected system: the state borrowed and taxed to build, the railways earned and opened the country, and the convertible ruble drew the foreign capital that paid for it all.

The cost of the “brilliant period”

The other face of the programme is the indictment that the Granat volume sets against it. Bogolepov’s running thesis is that the “brilliant period” of Russian state economy was purchased at the price of extreme strain on the taxable capacity of the people, irregularities of state economy, and the ruin of the propertyless. The state’s finances grew brilliant while the mass of the population bore the burden: indirect taxes pressed hardest on the poor, the redemption payments of the emancipated peasants hung over the village, and the famine of 1891–92 had shown how fragile the peasant economy was. The industrialization created its own social facts — the industrial boom of 1894–99 drew hundreds of thousands into the factories of Petersburg, Moscow, the Donbass, and the South, and the economic crisis of 1899–1903 threw them into unemployment and strikes. Martov’s essay in the same volume chronicles the growth of the mass labour movement that the Witte years called forth, and the government’s uneasy combination of factory legislation, repression, and the police experiments of Zubatov. The system had modernized Russia, but it had also created the industrial proletariat and the strained, indebted village that would carry the revolutions of the new century.

Current state: the foundation of the new Russia

In the frame of this corpus Witte’s industrialization stands as the economic transformation that made the Russia of Nicholas II. It provided the treasury with its revenues, the state with its railways and its Far Eastern reach, and the empire with the financial strength to fight the russo-japanese-war-1904-05 — whose defeat and cost, with the great foreign loan of 1906 that liquidated its legacy, closed the era. It created the industrial working class and the swollen cities that the revolution-1905 and the february-revolution-1917 were to shake. And it left a legacy of taxation and debt that Granat’s essayists judged the ruin of the propertyless and the seed of revolution. Related to it stand sergei-witte its architect, the duma-constitutionalism that the social strains of his economy helped to force, and the revolution-1905 that marked the political reckoning of the economic change.
