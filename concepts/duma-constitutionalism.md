# The Duma and Constitutionalism

The Duma and constitutionalism describe the short-lived experiment in representative government that Russia attempted after the [[revolution-1905]]: the State Duma (Государственная Дума), the elected legislative chamber created by the [[october-manifesto-1905]], the Fundamental Laws that surrounded it, and the constitutional order that survived — in form — until 1917. Within the frame of this corpus it is the moment when the [[autocracy-russia]] of the Romanovs seemed at last to yield to the western principle of a share of power, and the demonstration of how little of the concession was meant or made real. The story runs from the granting of the manifesto through the four Dumas, and its turning-point is the coup of 3 June 1907, when [[pyotr-stolypin]] dissolved the Second Duma and rewrote the electoral law to secure a submissive chamber — the act that Witte, from exile, bluntly named a *state coup* (государственный переворот).

## Definition: the representative experiment

The Duma was the chamber of elected deputies that Nicholas II was forced to grant by the revolutionary upheaval of 1905. The October Manifesto promised civil liberties and a legislative Duma — a body that would not merely advise but take part in legislation — and the empire was, on paper, transformed from an autocracy into something like a constitutional monarchy. The earlier Bulygin Duma, projected in the summer of 1905 as a purely consultative body, had been swept away by the October events and the creation of a legislature with real powers. The change was the central fact of the new political order: for the first time in Russian history the emperor would share his legislative power with an elected parliament. The question that divided the next decade was how far that sharing would go — whether the Duma would be a genuine parliament or a screen behind which the autocracy continued to govern as before.

## The First and Second Dumas

The experiment began badly and worsened. The First Duma, elected in 1906 on a wide franchise, was dominated by the liberal Constitutional Democrats and proved refractory from the start: it demanded a responsible ministry, pressed the radical solution of the land question, and refused to work within the narrow limits the government allowed. Witte, who had presided over the granting of the manifesto, called it a "Duma of popular retribution" (*Дума народного гнева*); the government answered its demands by dissolving it after barely two months and by the agrarian legislation framed over its head. The Second Duma, elected in early 1907, was even more radical — its left flank swollen by the revolution's aftermath — and the confrontation with the new minister of the interior and then premier, Stolypin, grew acute. The Fundamental Laws of 1906, framed under Witte, had already preserved to the emperor the supreme autocratic power alongside the Duma, reserving to him the sole right to amend them and to govern by emergency decree when the Duma was not sitting. The stage was set for a decisive trial of strength between the elected chamber and the crown.

## The coup of 3 June 1907

The trial was decided by force. Accusing the Social Democratic deputies of conspiracy, the government on 3 June 1907 dissolved the Second Duma and simultaneously issued a new electoral law, altering the franchise by imperial decree without the consent of the chamber it had abolished. The act violated the letter of the Fundamental Laws, which had made the electoral law subject to the Duma's concurrence, and for that reason Witte — who had by then left the premiership — flatly called it a state coup. It was, he observed, deliberately prepared so that the public might "more easily digest" a change that struck at the representative principle: a bogus plot was manufactured to justify a dissolution that was really meant to remodel the parliament. The new law, framed by the reactionary Kryzhanovsky and approved under Stolypin's direction, heavily weighted the franchise toward the gentry and the propertied, cut the representation of the peasants, workers, and the national borderlands, and secured the government a chamber of landowners and moderates.

## The Third and Fourth Dumas: the constitutional limits

The Third Duma, elected under the new law in 1907, was a different body — conservative, submissive, and willing to work with Stolypin, who governed through it the last years of his life until his assassination in 1911. The Fourth Duma, elected in 1912, continued in the same spirit. This was the "constitutional" Russia that went to war in 1914: a parliament existed, elections were held, legislation passed through it, but the supreme power remained with the emperor and his ministers, responsible to him and not to the chamber. The constitutional experiment, in Witte's judgment, had been hollowed out: the empire had accepted the forms of a representative monarchy while keeping the substance of autocratic government. The Duma's weakness was the weakness of the whole edifice — an institution that could protest and obstruct but could not rule, and that found itself brushed aside when the war and the revolution came.

## Current state: the collapse of the experiment

In the frame of this corpus the Duma stands as the incomplete constitution that neither saved nor served the monarchy. It gave Russia the forms of parliamentary life and trained a generation of politicians, but it never acquired real power, and the tension between its existence and the survival of autocracy was one of the running complaints that fed the [[revolution-1905]]'s aftermath and the growing estrangement of educated society. When the war overwhelmed the empire, the Duma was powerless to arrest the collapse of authority that Denikin chronicles; the Provisional Government that followed the [[february-revolution-1917]] grew out of a Duma committee, and the [[october-revolution-1917]] swept both it and the whole constitutional experiment away. The concept stands between the [[autocracy-russia]] that resisted it and the revolutions that destroyed it, the proof that Russia could neither remain fully autocratic nor become truly constitutional. Related to it stand [[pyotr-stolypin]] and [[sergei-witte]], the [[october-manifesto-1905]] and [[revolution-1905]] that created it, and the [[white-movement]] that, after 1917, fought — ambiguously — for some part of the order the Duma had represented.
