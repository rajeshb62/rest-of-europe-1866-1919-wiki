# Trialism

Trialism (*Trialismus*) was the proposal to reorganise the Habsburg monarchy from a dual into a triple state by adding a third unit to the two of Austria-Hungary — in its common form, a South Slav unit embracing the South Slav lands of the monarchy together with Bosnia-Herzegovina. It was the alternative future most discussed on the eve of 1914, associated with the circle of Archduke Franz Ferdinand, and treated by Sosnosky in his survey of the possible futures of the monarchy as one of the four paths open to it. Its very name was a new, public-law-precise word for an old aspiration, the "Illyrism" of Ljudevit Gaj.

## The proposal

Sosnosky defines trialism as the conversion of the monarchy "from a double state into a triple state," in which the third in the alliance would be the South Slav lands of the monarchy — those belonging to Austria as much as those belonging to Hungary — together, naturally, with Bosnia-Herzegovina. As an idea it had been at first belittled as a fantasy of over-heated South Slav *exaltados*; but, Sosnosky observes, "an idea behind which seven million passionate adherents stand cannot be laughed away and cannot be talked away — it forces for itself a hearing and attention." The shots on Cuvaj, the Croatian ban, had already shown this, and the events of the Balkan wars made a longer "overhearing" of the question impossible: both Vienna and Budapest, whether they wished or not, would very soon be obliged to occupy themselves with this "supposed utopia."

## Its attractions and its dangers

For Austria, Sosnosky argues, the trialist idea had real merits. It rested on the natural ethnographic principle; the number three "fits a leading whole far better than the number two — *tres faciunt collegium*"; and the South Slavs would form a real counterweight to the preponderance of the Magyars. Above all, he writes, if the wishes of the South Slavs were not regarded, if Croatia remained longer exposed to the Magyar pressure, then among the South Slavs of the monarchy the empire-friendly great-Croatian movement might give way to the empire-hostile great-Serbian one, whose goal lay outside the monarchy — a possibility brought into dangerous proximity by the fresh victor's nimbus that the Serbs of the kingdom had recently won in the Balkans. The monarchy was thus confronted with the choice: either to transform dualism into trialism, or to bring about the erection of a hostile South Slav state on its borders. Yet trialism also carried grave dangers. The principal difficulty lay in Hungary, which would resist its realisation with all its force, and with right from the Magyar standpoint: the erection of a South Slav state would cost Hungary the loss of Croatia-Slavonia and thereby of its only access to the sea — "an amputation which no land and no people would allow itself to suffer willingly" — and probably also the Serb-inhabited districts of the Bácska and the Torontal, together with Hungary's claims on Bosnia-Herzegovina and Dalmatia. On the Austrian side the obstacles were scarcely smaller: Dalmatia, with its rich coast, was Austria's principal share of the Adriatic, and the loss of Triest was quite out of the question. Nor was it certain that a new Illyrian kingdom would remain with Austria rather than gravitate from Vienna toward Belgrade — that the great-Croatian realm would not become a great-Serbian one. Lacking any security against a South Slav–Magyar coalition, the leading authorities of the monarchy, Sosnosky judged, would hardly be inclined to risk so high a stake as the existence of the Habsburg realm on so doubtful a political experiment.

## Trialism and Franz Ferdinand

The trialist idea was most closely associated with the heir to the throne, Archduke Franz Ferdinand. His known hostility to the Magyar supremacy, his championship of the armed forces, and his personal concern with the South Slav question — Conrad records that the archduke asked his views on the South Slav problem and that Conrad urged a settlement favourable to the Croats — made him the natural patron of a reorganisation that would relieve the South Slavs of the Hungarian crown. Redlich, writing after the catastrophe, judged that it was the death of Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo that "removed, to all appearance forever," the possibility of breaking the Magyar supremacy from within the monarchy. The war that followed, however, dissolved the question in a different way: the South Slav lands passed not into a trialist third unit of the monarchy but into the new kingdom of the Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, and the monarchy itself ceased to exist.
