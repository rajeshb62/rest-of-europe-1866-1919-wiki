# The Stab-in-the-Back Legend

The claim, proclaimed aloud by Germany on the morrow of its defeat in 1918, that its army had suffered no military defeat in the field, that its soldiers had not been vanquished by enemies from without but had been stabbed in the back by the triumphant revolution from within. The German name for it is the *Dolchstoßlegende*, the dagger-stab legend. Recouly records and rebuts it in his closing chapter on war and peace, as the state of mind of Germany at the moment of the armistice and as the seed of the sombre prospect of a new war. It is the doctrine by which a defeated nation denied its defeat, transferred the responsibility for the catastrophe to its own internal enemies, and prepared the ground for the revisionist politics of the years that followed.

## The inscription on the tomb

The legend found its lapidary expression in the inscription that the celebrated scholar Wilamowitz-Moellendorff composed for the tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Berlin: *INVICTIS VICTI VICTURI* — "to the unconquered soldiers, today's conquered who will be tomorrow's conquerors." This phrase illustrated in a striking manner the state of mind of Germany immediately after its defeat. Germany proclaimed loudly, to the face of the world, that it had suffered, militarily speaking, no defeat at all. It affirmed — contrary to all truth, in Recouly's judgement — that its soldiers had not been conquered on the fields of battle by the enemies from without, but solely by the enemies from within: that they had been stabbed in the back by the triumphant revolution. Without that revolution they would have been victorious; their momentary failure was a simple accident, an anomaly that imminent and certain victories would cause to be forgotten.

## The assertion of the undefeated army

The whole legend rests on the assertion that the German army, in the field, was never beaten. Its soldiers, the claim ran, were undefeated — *invicti* — for the defeat that brought Germany down was the work not of foreign arms but of the internal revolution that rose in the rear. The revolution, that is, had struck the army a blow from behind, a *coup de poignard dans le dos*, a dagger-thrust in the back, at the very moment when its front was still holding. The [[armistice-11-november-1918]] came not because the army had been broken by the enemy but because the homeland had collapsed behind it. This was the sense in which the soldiers of the trenches were regarded as unconquered, their defeat in November 1918 as an accident that the victories of tomorrow would efface. The formula *INVICTIS VICTI VICTURI* united the three tenses of the legend: the soldiers unconquered in the past, the Germans conquered today, the victors of tomorrow.

The legend thus took on the colour of a betrayal — of the soldiers by the civilians, of the front by the home front, of the faithful by the revolutionary. It pointed to no foreign enemy as the author of the German collapse but to an enemy of another kind, hidden within: the revolution that had risen in the rear and cut the army down while it fought. In this it was the most complete expression of the refusal of the German people, or of those who spoke in its name, to accept that its armies had been vanquished in a fair and open contest of arms.

## The betrayal and the German pride

Recouly sets the legend against the larger moral of the war, which his whole history had been concerned to draw. Germany, for all its marvellous military preparation, was finally defeated because, too sure of its warlike superiority and committing its habitual sin of pride, it entered the conflict in bad diplomatic conditions and ranged too many enemies against itself. This was the background against which the legend had to be judged: a nation that had overreached itself, and that, rather than confess its miscalculation and its defeat, chose to throw the blame upon an internal betrayal. The claim of the undefeated army was in this light an act of the same pride that had entered the war believing victory certain — a pride that survived the catastrophe and refused to learn from it.

The legend was further sustained by the circumstances of the war's close. The armies of the Central Powers had stood, for long years, upon the soil of the enemy, in occupation of vast territories; the fighting in the west, after the failure of Ludendorff's offensives of the spring of 1918 and the counter-offensives that followed, had turned against them, but the retreat was a German retreat, and the illusion could be maintained that the soldiers had not been broken but merely abandoned. The revolution that rose at home in the closing weeks of the war gave the legend its ready-made author. It was easier, and more flattering, to say that the army had been stabbed in the back than to acknowledge that it had been beaten in the field.

## The decision was won in the field

Recouly's rebuttal rests on the true course of the war, which he had narrated from beginning to end. The decision of the war was won and lost upon the battlefield, by the merit or failure of the chiefs and the troops, and not delivered by any dagger from behind. At the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]] the skill and sang-froid of the French generalissimo, and the errors that excess of confidence made the German generalissimo commit, turned the situation, dismantled the German machine, and transformed a war that was to have been short into one of long duration. In 1918 the failure of Ludendorff's offensives of July, followed three days later by a still graver failure, marked the beginning of the descent that became ever more rapid and soon vertiginous; and it was Foch's counter-offensives, the [[hundred-days-offensive-1918]], that broke the German army.

Nor was the German collapse a sudden accident of the last weeks. The war had been lost step by step, on the field, from the very first battles. The grave blunder of the German military chiefs — ignorant of politics — in deciding on unrestricted submarine warfare had provoked American intervention and added one more enemy; and with the defection of Russia the German advantage had been won and lost again as the power of the West was concentrated against it. The army that the legend called undefeated had been defeated, over four years, by the weight of a war it had itself called down. The revolution at home was in truth the consequence, not the cause, of that defeat — the fruit of the exhaustion, the hunger and the disillusion that years of war had sown, not the sudden blow that felled a still-victorious army.

## The consequences of the legend

For Recouly the legend mattered above all for what it portended. It revealed Germany's true face on the morrow of its defeat, and it raised the question that tormented him: how those who had vanquished it with such difficulty, France first, could have committed the folly of not taking more solid precautions against it. A Germany that proclaimed its soldiers unconquered, that inscribed on its tomb the promise that today's conquered would be tomorrow's conquerors, was a Germany that had not accepted the verdict of the war and was preparing to reverse it. The treaty of peace, badly conceived and still worse executed, had done nothing to remove the danger; on the contrary, by the lightness and imprudence of a policy that rested on appearances and not on realities, it had left the legend to grow unchecked.

The sombre perspective of a new war, only sixteen years after a war that had nearly laid France low, was the direct consequence. The legend was not merely a false account of the past; it was the bridge by which the past defeat was converted into the expectation of future victory, the soil in which the next conflict was to take root. It is thus, in Recouly's telling, the central doctrine of the German refusal to accept the peace — a doctrine at once military, political and moral, by which a defeated and overreaching nation denied its defeat and armed itself, in spirit, for the day of its return.
