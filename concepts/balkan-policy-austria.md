# Austria-Hungary's Balkan Policy

Austria-Hungary's Balkan policy — *die Balkanpolitik* of the Habsburg Monarchy — is the specifically Habsburg programme within the wider [[eastern-question]]: the share the Dual Monarchy sought in the declining Ottoman inheritance from the catastrophe of 1866 down to the world war. Where the Eastern Question is the problem of Europe as a whole, the Balkan policy is the national, Habsburg answer to it — the policy that led from the occupation of Bosnia in 1878, through the annexation crisis of 1908, to the rivalry with Serbia and the road to [[sarajevo-assassination-1914]]. It is the concept under which the Monarchy's whole eastern course in this period is best understood, and Sosnosky devoted his two-volume work to recounting it.

## Definition: the Habsburg programme in the east

After Königgrätz (1866) drove the Monarchy out of Germany and Italy, the Balkan was, as Sosnosky writes, the only field left to its political activity; from that moment the Monarchy's future lay in the east. The policy it pursued was at first the conserving one of Metternich's inheritance — to keep the Turkish Empire alive as long as possible and to wait patiently for its death-hour, in order then to come forward and take a share of the inheritance. This was the programme of Gyula Andrássy, who after the disastrous *Beust* intermezzo took the helm and steered the new course to the east. Its decisive act was the occupation of Bosnia-Herzegovina, undertaken by virtue of the mandate of the [[congress-of-berlin-1878]], which planted the Monarchy among the South Slavs. The policy thereafter was a long sequence of accommodations and half-measures with the other Balkan and European powers — the alliance with Germany, the *Dreikaiserbund* entente with Russia, the watch over the Sandjak of Novi Pazar — all directed to the single end of keeping the Monarchy's position in the peninsula without risking a general war.

## The character of the policy: willing and power

Sosnosky's closing retrospect draws the character of the whole policy with a historian's severity. He discerns in it three abiding marks: a striking disproportion between the aims of the leading statesmen and the means of power at their disposal — between their *Wollen* and their *Können*; a frequent misreading of decisive political moments and the neglect of favourable opportunities; and a fatal inclination to half-measures. Every statesman of the period illustrates the pattern. Andrássy let the chance to annex Bosnia slip and contented himself with occupation, even secretly promising the Porte to hold the conquered lands only provisionally. His successors Haymerle, Kálnoky and Goluchowski carried the policy into a progressive paralysis, until under Goluchowski the will vanished altogether and the Monarchy knew only the desire to keep the peace at any price — the "good-natured universal uncle" who made way for everyone. Aehrenthal woke the Monarchy from its lethargy but used its recovered strength only passively: he bought Bosnia and Herzegovina from Turkey with money and, worse, surrendered the key to the Aegean, the Sandjak of Novi Pazar; and when the moment was ripe, with Russia powerless and the army waiting, he let the chance to settle accounts with Serbia pass and proclaimed that the Monarchy was satiated. Sosnosky calls that declaration the death-sentence of the Monarchy's future in the east.

## Berchtold and the crisis of the status quo

The policy reached its crisis under [[leopold-berchtold]] in the [[balkan-wars-1912-13]]. Berchtold had to pay for the sins of all his predecessors back to Andrássy: he was to make Balkan policy and dared take no step forward, bound as he was by the status-quo theory, by the alliance with Italy which claimed compensation should he advance, and by the reluctance of Berlin to be drawn into a Balkan war. When the Balkan League reduced the status quo to a bloody absurdity, he had to look on while the allies divided the Turkish inheritance, and let the opportunity to settle with Serbia slip past as Aehrenthal had done. His two positive acts — the creation of the truncated state of Albania, and the protest against the Peace of Bukarest that was not followed by deeds — were, in Sosnosky's phrase, half-measures in the old style. The [[july-crisis-1914]] was the last, forced application of the policy: the moment at which the Monarchy at last made serious demands upon Serbia, but, as Sosnosky lamented, again with half-measures — a punitive expedition that left Serbia in possession of its territory, which could only begin the old game anew.

## The Serbian question as the heart of the policy

In its last phase the Balkan policy was above all the Serbian question. The [[occupation-of-bosnia-1878]] and the annexation had fed the national movement among the South Slavs and their desire for territorial union; the large successes of Serbia in the Balkan Wars had given it a seductive glamour and drawn the eyes of the South Slavs from Vienna to Belgrade as the future capital of a South-Slav state. The great Serbian propaganda, conducted with the connivance of the Serbian government, ate into the Monarchy's southern lands. Sosnosky judged the Serbian question a *Lebensfrage* — a question of life — for the Monarchy, cutting as it did into its very organism through the South-Slav problem, and he concluded that only the disappearance of Serbia from the map could give the Monarchy rest. This was the conviction that underlay the policy of the general staff and of the war party, and it was the answer the Monarchy finally gave in the July crisis. The Balkan policy of Austria-Hungary thus runs, in one unbroken line, from the conserving conservatism of Andrássy to the ultimatum of Berchtold and the mobilisation of [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]].
