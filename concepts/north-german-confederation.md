# The North German Confederation

The North German Confederation (*Norddeutscher Bund*, "North German league") was the federal state created by Prussia after the [[austro-prussian-war-1866]] out of the German lands north of the river Main, and the constitutional form through which the [[kleindeutsch-grossdeutsch|kleindeutsch]] solution was first given institutional shape. Founded in 1867 (see the event [[north-german-confederation-1867]]), it united Prussia and the smaller north and central German states under a Prussian federal presidency, a Federal Council (*Bundesrat*), and a directly elected parliament (*Reichstag*). It was designed from the outset as the permanent shell of the future German nation-state: when the southern states joined after the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]], the Confederation's constitution was carried over almost unchanged into the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871|German Empire]]. The concept therefore describes not merely an interlude but the constitutional grammar of the Reich that followed.

## A Prussian construction

The North German Confederation was the work of [[otto-von-bismarck]] above all. Lenz's account of its founding is emphatic that, whatever the forms of consultation, the constitution was a personal creation. When the drafts of the privy counsellors Hepke, Lothar Bucher, and Max Duncker were laid before him, none found his approval; with their "centralizing, federal-state tendency" they reminded him too much of the constitutions of Frankfurt and Erfurt. He insisted instead on keeping to the ground of the memorandum of 1861: to hold to the form of a *Staatenbund* (a league of states) while giving it practically the nature of a *Bundesstaat* (a federal state), "with elastic, inconspicuous but far-reaching expressions." He had no scruple in applying the accustomed nomenclature — even the name *Bundestag* — to the new federal forms. The draft that became the foundation of both the North German Confederation and the German Empire itself he dictated in a few hours on the afternoon of 13 December 1866; Lothar Bucher worked out the sketch that same night, and on 15 December it was laid in print before the plenipotentiaries of the allied governments.

## The federal structure

The Confederation rested on a division of authority between the federal presidency, the Federal Council, and the Reichstag. The presidency, held by the Prussian king in his capacity as head of the union, was endowed with the strongest rights: the full representation of the union abroad, the power over war and peace, the supervision of the execution of federal decisions, the appointment of all federal officials, the executive power against recalcitrant members, and the supreme command in peace and war over a federal army organized on the Prussian model; the fleet was placed directly under Prussian command. Yet the constitution still granted the allied governments that measure of influence which corresponded to their power, and above all it guaranteed them the full security of their existence, both against the foreigner and against the national storms that had once threatened them with destruction.

Against the ministries and houses proposed by the privy counsellors — which would have fostered the particularist elements — Bismarck set "with a truly bold stroke" the *Bundesrat* directly in the place of a Reich ministry. This council, so named as the central authority, was to fill the individual offices of the federal service with expert commissions drawn from its own midst and to present its phalanx of forty-three seats to the Reichstag. It was the collective organ of the state governments, and Lenz observes that, despite repeated temptations that powerful governments such as Bavaria might lead a Catholic *Fronde*, the unity of the supreme council of the nation was never shaken, and the governments have ever since stood unanimous against the Reichstag.

## The popular element

The other pillar was the directly elected Reichstag, the expression of the national will, in which Bismarck deliberately placed the "counterweight" to dynastic ambition and particularist strivings. It was here that the statesman joined hands, against his own instincts, with the liberal and democratic forces he distrusted. He adopted the universal, equal, and direct suffrage on the model of the Reich constitution of 1849 — in part to outbid the Austrian and reactionary governments before the war, in part because he believed the lower classes more conservative and monarchical than the middle ones, and that the propertied employers would keep their social power over their workers even under universal suffrage. He rejected as "the most contemptible, the most senseless of all electoral systems" the Prussian system of indirect election by classes, wishing to drive liberalism from the stronghold it had built in Prussia through the class suffrage. As a counterweight he insisted that no pay (*Diäten*) ever be given to the deputies.

The liberal leaders, seeing in universal suffrage the strongest and most legitimate weapon against particularism, could not resist it. Miquel's argument carried the day: universal suffrage might have its doubts, but they must not reject it, for it was the summons to all classes and stations to join the national union and renounce the "dull separatism." The most acute liberal leader, Heinrich von Sybel — who had returned to parliament and joined the new National Liberal party under Bennigsen's leadership — warned of the inner dangers of unchaining the masses; but even he did not foresee that universal suffrage would one day subject the army of workers to socialist utopias and raise the clericals to power in the new Reich.

## A federal economy

The Confederation's parliament legislated the framework of a national economy. Unanimously the first Reichstag passed the law of freedom of movement (*Freizügigkeit*), which permitted every member of the union to reside and settle anywhere in the union and gave everyone the capacity to acquire landed property and to carry on a trade; the removal of restrictions on interest (*Zinsbeschränkung*) broke one of the strongest clamps on commerce and industry; and laws followed on the post, on passports, on federal debts, and on universal military service. These measures extended and deepened the economic unity already prepared in the [[zollverein|Zollverein]], whose reform in 1867 was handled through the new Federal Customs Council and its parliamentary counterpart, the [[zollverein|Zollparlament]], which met twice from 1868 onward.

## The frame of the Reich

The North German Confederation was explicitly a provisional form designed to admit the South. The Peace of Prague had given the southern states an international position that neither prescribed nor forbade their joining the northern power, and the question of their entry dominated the years 1867–70. Bismarck himself wished to postpone it, knowing that its coming would bring the international crisis — the great war happily avoided in 1866. When that war came, with France, the Confederation provided the ready-made instrument: after the victory, its constitution, "in its outlines already the constitution of the German Empire," was transferred almost entire to the new Reich, so that the Empire of 1871 was the North German Confederation enlarged and crowned. The concept thus stands at the hinge between the [[german-question]] and its final kleindeutsch answer.
