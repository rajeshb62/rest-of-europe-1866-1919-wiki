# Liberal Empire (Empire libéral, 1869–1870)

The Liberal Empire (*Empire libéral*) was the transformation of the Second Empire in its last two years — from the senatus-consult of September 1869 and the letter of 19 January 1869 through the Ollivier ministry of 2 January 1870 — whereby the authoritarian regime turned toward parliamentary government while preserving the imperial crown. It is the central subject of Émile Ollivier's great memoir *L'Empire libéral*, whose very title makes the claim that the liberal transformation was not a betrayal of the Empire but its fulfilment. The experiment died within months, engulfed in the Franco-Prussian War.

## The road to parliamentary government

The liberalisation began in measured steps. The letter of 19 January 1867 had conceded a regulated right of interpellation and promised press and meetings laws; these came fully into force in 1868. But the decisive turn came in 1869. After the elections of that year — which Ollivier reads as a defeat of the authoritarian Empire represented by Rouher and a victory for the liberal programme — the Emperor yielded the ground. The senatus-consult of 8 September 1869 replaced the irresponsible regime with a system of ministerial responsibility, giving the *Corps législatif* a genuine share in government.

Ollivier's *Le 19 janvier*, published in March 1869, supplied the doctrine: the Empire must either advance into responsible, parliamentary government or perish as an anachronism. In Ollivier's telling the Emperor, who had said he would not go back but would not go forward, finally accepted the necessity of moving forward. The negotiations that produced the ministry of 2 January 1870 are recorded by Delord in the *Préface de l'Empire libéral*: the approaches between Ollivier and the Emperor through Clément Duvernois, Ollivier's conditions and programme, his disguised journey to Compiègne, and the final formation of a ministry drawn from the liberal centre under his presidency.

## The ministry of 2 January 1870

The Ollivier ministry marked the inauguration of the Liberal Empire proper. It was a cabinet of the constitutional centres — Daru, Buffet, and their friends entering with conditions — and it presented itself as a government of the parliamentary majority, responsible before the Chambers. Ollivier, who had spent years as the advocate of liberal reform within the Empire, now became its president of the council, seeking to make the imperial crown compatible with a fully constitutional regime. The ministry's programme, in his telling, was to complete the edifice of liberty within the frame of the dynasty.

The task was formidable. Ollivier's own memoir emphasises the contradictions: the Empire was still a personal, plebiscitary power at its base, and the Emperor retained the reserve of his own authority; the liberal ministry had to govern with an assembly that contained both an intransigent republican left and a Bonapartist right hostile to parliamentary government. The prophecies of misfortune, Ollivier records, came from all sides — Thiers, the *irréconciliables*, and the opposition press alike foretold disaster for a hybrid regime that satisfied neither the partisans of absolute authority nor the partisans of liberty.

## The fate of the liberal experiment

The Liberal Empire governed for barely eight months. It had to confront the European crisis that broke over the Hohenzollern candidature and the Ems affair, and in July 1870 Ollivier, against his pacific instincts, led France into the war with Prussia. The catastrophe of Sedan, the capture of the Emperor, and the revolution of 4 September 1870 swept away both the Empire and its liberal ministry together. The experiment — an attempt to reconcile the Napoleonic tradition of authority with the nineteenth-century demand for parliamentary government — was never given the chance to mature.

For Ollivier the *Empire libéral* remained his great historical vindication, the subject of a multi-volume memoir composed in the decades after the fall. For the Republic that followed, the failure of the Liberal Empire demonstrated the impossibility of reconciling personal power with liberty — a lesson the Third Republic's founders took to heart. The term itself, however, continued to be used: Hanotaux, writing of the "liberal Empire" in the constitutional theory preceding 1875, treats it as one more episode in the nineteenth century's circuit between democracy and authority.
