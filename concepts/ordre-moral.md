# Ordre moral (1873–1874)

*L'ordre moral* ("moral order") was the policy of the conservative-moralist ministries under President Mac-Mahon after the fall of Thiers — the two first Broglie cabinets of May 1873 to May 1874 — which sought to govern France in the name of God, the family, and order, against the revolutionary and republican spirit. Hanotaux's second volume is organised around this policy and its failure: the attempt to restore a monarchical and clerical order, defeated by the divisions of the royalists and by the republican movement of the country.

## The politics of 24 May and the formula of order

After the fall of Thiers on 24 May 1873, Marshal Mac-Mahon was elected president — 390 votes, with the left abstaining — accepting with a soldier's word to continue the liberation of the territory and to restore *ordre moral*. Hanotaux notes the foreign alarm calmed by the Marshal's name, the reaction of a country weary of revolution and war, and the character of the new president: a soldier of honour, loyal, but no politician. The ministry of 25 May, under the presidency of the duc de Broglie, adopted the formula of "moral order" as its programme: the defence of religion, property, and social order against the democratic and revolutionary currents that had destroyed the Empire.

The religious character of the policy was explicit. Hanotaux's chapter "L'« ordre moral »" records the resumption of the Assembly session after 24 May, the interpellations, the religious question, the pilgrimages, the cult of the Sacré-Cœur, and the project of the votive church of Montmartre. The *ordre moral* presented itself as the reconciliation of France with its religious and monarchical traditions after the disasters of the Empire, the war, and the Commune — a moral restoration of the country under the authority of God and the head of state.

## The monarchical campaign and its failure

The *ordre moral* was bound up with the attempt to restore the monarchy. The monarchical parties, encouraged by their victory of 24 May, pursued the *fusion* of the two branches of the Bourbon house — the reconciliation of the Orléanists and legitimists around the *comte de Chambord*. Charles Chesnelong was delegated by the *commission des Neuf* to Chambord at Salzburg, where he received verbal declarations on the constitution and the flag; the campaign seemed on the point of success. But Chambord's letter of 27 October 1873, refusing to abandon his conditions and his flag, broke the enterprise. The monarchical past, Hanotaux concludes, was liquidated; the Republic's place was cleared.

After the failure of parliamentary restoration, the Assembly granted Mac-Mahon the Septennat (20 November 1873), and Broglie formed a second ministry, seeking to consolidate around the Marshal a "government of the middle classes." It failed in turn: the majority of 24 May disaggregated, the mayors' law and the constitutional projects divided it, and on 16 May 1874 the duc de Broglie fell. The *ordre moral* had lasted barely a year, destroyed both by the impossibility of restoring a king and by the divisions among its own supporters.

## The meaning of the failure

For Hanotaux, the failure of the *ordre moral* was the failure of the old France — of the privileged classes and the monarchy — to re-establish themselves after the catastrophe. The right had won the election of February 1871, but it could not turn its victory into a durable order; it was defeated less by the republicans than by its own divisions and by the refusal of the country to abandon the Republic that had made peace and begun reconstruction. The *ordre moral* stood for a conception of order imposed from above, religious and monarchical; its defeat prepared the way for the Constitution of 1875, for the republican conquest of the executive, and for the eventual passage from the clerical-conservative Republic to the laic, anticlerical Republic of the Ferry years. The cry that Gambetta had raised at Grenoble in 1872 — that clericalism was the remaining enemy — pointed toward the struggle that would define the Republic's later decades.
