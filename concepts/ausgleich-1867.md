# The Ausgleich of 1867

The *Ausgleich* ("compromise," in Hungarian *kiegyezés*) of 1867 was the constitutional settlement by which the Austrian Empire was transformed into the Dual Monarchy of Austria-Hungary. Concluded between the Crown and Hungary in the aftermath of the defeats of 1866, it restored Hungarian self-government, established the common affairs and the Delegations, and created the dualist structure that endured until 1918. Its central enactment in Hungarian law was Act XII of 1867, and its architect was Ferenc Deák. The settlement remains one of the most contested acts of the century: Beksics defends it as the restoration of Hungarian statehood, while Sosnosky condemns it as a work that carried the germ of strife within it.

## The constitutional arrangements

The Compromise rested on the historical Hungarian constitution and the Pragmatic Sanction. By Act XII of 1867 Hungary recognised the inseparable and indivisible possession of the kingdoms and the common defence, while receiving back its own responsible government, parliament and administration. The Austrian half, the kingdoms and lands represented in the Reichsrat (Cisleithania), was constituted in parallel by the December constitution of 1867, while the Hungarian half (Transleithania) comprised the lands of the crown of St. Stephen. The two states shared the person of the ruler and the common affairs: foreign policy, the army, and the finance for these, administered by three joint ministries and controlled by the Delegations drawn from the two parliaments. The customs and economic union, with a common tariff, currency and commercial policy, was a separate part of the settlement renewed periodically. Within Hungary, Croatia-Slavonia was bound to the kingdom by the *nagodba* ("settlement") of 1868, which gave it a measure of autonomy.

## Deák's work and the army question

The negotiations of 1866–1867, resumed after Königgrätz, were conducted for Hungary by Deák, who asked nothing less than the conversion of the old unitary empire into a double state. The Crown's first serious scruples concerned the unity of the army. In the drafting of § 11 of the Hungarian act, Deák refused to recognise a separate Hungarian army, declaring that such a course "would be personal union," and that a Hungarian army "has never existed, neither in law nor in practice." Andrássy supported him against the opposition, reminding it that with two legislatures and two governments a modern state could not also have two armies. Yet Deák was finally induced, to satisfy the demand of the subcommittee, to weave the "Hungarian army" into the wording of the paragraph — an inconsistency that Sosnosky calls fatal, since it kept the dispute over the common army alive for decades. The act was adopted by the Hungarian lower house on 30 March 1867 by 257 against 117 votes, and shortly after by the Magnates.

## The acquiescence of the Reichsrat

A notable feature of the settlement was that the Austrian parliament was never asked to consent to the state-legal transformation of the whole monarchy. The Hungarian parliament had decided the matter over the heads of the Austrian deputies. Sosnosky calls this a demand such as had hardly ever been made of a representative body — that the Reichsrat assent to a remodelling of the entire monarchy arranged without its participation. Yet the Reichsrat did not resist: it gave no formal consent, but tacitly acquiesced, sending a deputation to Ofen for the coronation of Franz Joseph as King of Hungary, which amounted to a formal recognition of the Hungarian decision, and refraining from asserting its parliamentary rights against Hungary. The German-liberal party then at the helm in Cisleithania, and the leading press, hailed the Ausgleich as the end of the "long pernicious quarrel" and the beginning of a prosperous development for the new double state.

## Two judgements

The verdicts of the two chief contemporary sources diverge sharply. Beksics, writing a generation after the event, defends the Compromise as the restoration and guarantee of Hungarian statehood: the dualism was no new creation but "gave form to a centuries-long historical development," and Deák's work could not be destroyed "because it answers the interests of both Hungary and Austria." Sosnosky, on the eve of 1914, rejects this: he writes that the Ausgleich was not a successful work, that "success was not granted to the work of Deák and Andrássy," and that it "already carried in itself the visible germ of strife." Yet even Sosnosky concedes the good faith of the makers, and both agree on the scale of the change: the Compromise of 1867 created the frame within which the monarchy's politics — the national struggles, the Magyarization policy, the Balkan policy, and at last the war — were all conducted down to the dissolution of 1918.
