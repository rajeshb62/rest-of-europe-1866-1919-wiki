# Republicanism in France

French republicanism was the ideology of the Republic — the doctrine that sovereignty resides in the people and that no man, no family, and no dynasty may stand above the law. In the decades after 1870 it passed from a persecuted opposition faith to the governing ideology of the state, and its internal tensions — between moderate and radical strands, between the mystique of the founders and the politique of their successors, between the promise of liberty and the demands of the laic school — defined the character of the Third Republic.

## The radical and moderate strands

Republicanism was not a single doctrine but a family of doctrines held together by a common hatred of monarchy and of personal power. Hanotaux's constitutional theory distinguishes the elements that survived into the Constitution of 1875: popular sovereignty, the law of majorities, universal suffrage, the rights of minorities, national unity, and the conciliation of unity and liberty. Against the memory of the Empire and of democratic Caesarism, the republicans set the representative regime and the parliamentary Republic. The moderates — the *centre gauche* around Thiers and then the "opportunists" — sought a conservative, reassuring Republic of order and liberty; the radicals — heirs of the old revolutionary tradition — demanded the full programme of social and political transformation, universal suffrage used boldly, and the destruction of clerical influence.

The radical tradition surfaced in the elections and agitations of the Second Empire's last years: Gambetta's Belleville programme of 1869, with its demands for civil liberties and its hostility to the Empire, and Jules Ferry's "necessary destructions" were the republican programme in opposition. Under the Republic, Gambetta — the great tribune of the movement — embodied the union of the two strands: the orator who spoke for the "new social strata" (*nouvelles couches*) at Grenoble in 1872 and who led the campaign against the *Seize Mai*, yet who could be charged, as a statesman, with moderating the radical impulse into the practical politics of consolidation.

## Gambetta and the Republic of the republicans

Gambetta was the central figure of republican consolidation. From his prodigious plea in the Baudin trial of 1868, through his provincial campaigns, his leadership of the union of the 363 against the *Seize Mai*, and his cry that Mac-Mahon must "submit or resign," he was the incarnation of republican energy. Hanotaux treats the twelve-year heroic age of the Republic's foundation as in large part Gambetta's work, and its close with his death in 1882 as a brutal conclusion after which "figures and the aspect of things change." Gambetta's republicanism was the republicanism of the nation and of the people — a faith in universal suffrage and in the regenerative power of the Republic to heal the wounds of defeat and civil war.

## The anticlerical and school-building programme

The distinctive achievement of republican consolidation — the programme that most sharply defined the regime — was the laic reform of education and the reduction of clerical influence. Jules Ferry's school laws made primary education free, obligatory, and secular; the expulsion of the congregations followed the decrees of 29–30 March 1880; the phrase that Gambetta had coined at Grenoble — "clericalism, that is the enemy" — became the watchword of the republican majority. Sabatier treats the laic school as "the new incarnation of a secular religious effort," a movement by which "our country has sought to conquer itself" — not a mere anticlerical negation but a constructive attempt to give the citizen a moral formation. The anticlerical and school-building programme thus belonged to the republicans' positive vision of a regenerated, unified nation, even as it divided France between clerical and laic camps for a generation.

## Mystique and politique

Beyond its institutions and its programme, republicanism carried a moral and spiritual content that Péguy articulated with unmatched force in *Notre jeunesse*. For Péguy, the republican faith of his fathers was a *mystique* — "when one died for the Republic" — a lived instinct borne by ordinary republican families, of which his own generation was the last to possess the tradition. The Dreyfus affair was "the last of the operations of the republican mystique." But by 1910 the Republic, he wrote, had become a *thèse*: the intermediate generation had lost the instinct of republican mystique, the Republic had passed from organic to logical, and the *mystique républicaine* had degenerated into *politique républicaine* — "everything begins in mystique and ends in politique." Péguy's lament testifies both to the depth of the republican faith and to the Republic's later loss of that faith, as the party politicians succeeded the founders. Republicanism was thus never merely a doctrine of state; it was a form of secular faith, and its decline into political calculation was itself a central theme of the generation before the war.
