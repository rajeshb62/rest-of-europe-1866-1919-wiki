# The Risorgimento

The *Risorgimento* — "the resurrection" — is the Italian name for the movement of national revival and unification that, across the first three-quarters of the nineteenth century, transformed a peninsula of separate and foreign-ruled states into the Kingdom of Italy. As the corpus's sources treat it, the Risorgimento was both an event and a narrative: a lived history of revolutions, wars and annexations running from the failed uprisings of 1821 and 1831 and Mazzini's *Giovine Italia*, through the revolutions of 1848–49, the war of 1859, the Expedition of the Thousand and the annexation of the South in 1860, the war of 1866 that brought the Veneto, and the [[capture-of-rome-1870]] that completed unification — and a national idea, a story the Italians told themselves of how a people that had been divided and ruled by foreigners came to be one nation. Tivaroni's three volumes, *L'Italia degli Italiani*, are explicitly a history of the movement as the culmination of the national idea; Garibaldi's *Memorie* tell the same story from within, as the autobiography of its most famous soldier; and Oriani's *La lotta politica in Italia* reads the whole of Italian history as the long preparation for the nation that the Risorgimento at last achieved.

## The idea and its makers

The Risorgimento began as an idea before it was a fact. In the restored absolutisms of the years after 1815, and against the legacy of the Napoleonic age, the national idea took form in secret societies and in the thought of its prophets. Tivaroni's retrospective on "lo svolgimento del pensiero nazionale" (the development of the national thought) traces the strands: the height of Mazzini's prestige and his doctrine of republican regeneration, the germs of monarchical unity in Vincenzo Gioberti's vision of an Italian federation under the Pope, the *murattismo* that dreamed of a liberal Napoleonic kingdom in Naples, and the *Società Nazionale Italiana* that eventually brought the monarchists and the revolutionaries together on the ground of unity under the House of Savoy. From the failed revolutions of 1821 and 1831 and the youth of *Giovine Italia* through the climacteric of 1848–49 — the Sicilian rising, the Milanese Five Days, the Roman Republic that [[giuseppe-garibaldi]] defended — the movement built its martyrology and its language. The Risorgimento, as Tivaroni presents it, was carried by the *partito d'azione*, the party of action, and by the moderates who finally supplied it a state.

## The wars and the conquests

The Risorgimento was accomplished by a combination of war, revolution and diplomacy. The war of 1859, prepared by [[camillo-cavour]] at Plombières and fought beside Napoleon III, drove Austria out of Lombardy; the revolutions of 1859–60 in Florence, Modena, Parma and Bologna, and the annexation of central Italy, extended the kingdom to the centre; and the [[expedition-of-the-thousand-1860]] of Garibaldi, carrying the revolution into Sicily and Naples, delivered the South to the national cause. Oriani divides the work between the "royal conquest" of the north and centre and the "revolutionary conquest" of the South, and Tivaroni's decisive year 1860 binds them together: the plebiscites of October 1860 and the fall of Gaeta made the Kingdom of Italy, proclaimed in 1861 under [[victor-emmanuel-ii]]. The two wars that followed completed the map — the [[italian-war-1866]] that added the Veneto (though by the diplomatic *cadeau* rather than by victory, with the defeats of [[battle-of-custoza-1866]] and [[battle-of-lissa-1866]]) and the [[capture-of-rome-1870]] of 20 September 1870, which breached Porta Pia, ended the temporal power of the Pope, and gave the kingdom its capital, followed by the [[law-of-guarantees-1871]].

## The unfinished nation

Even completed, the Risorgimento left the nation unfinished, and its aftermath belongs to this corpus's years. The annexation of the South brought the problem of the [[southern-question-italy]] — the brigandage reaction of the 1860s, the tributary burden, and the economic backwardness that Fortunato would spend his life exposing. The frontier fixed in 1866 left the Italians of the Trentino, Trieste and Istria under Austria, and from that incompleteness grew [[irredentism]], the claim of the *Italia irredenta* that would help carry Italy into the First World War in 1915 and be consummated in the victory of [[battle-of-vittorio-veneto-1918]]. And the politics of the new kingdom, with its [[trasformismo]] and its colonial misadventures, tested whether the national idea could survive the nation's existence. The Risorgimento thus frames the whole Italian story of this corpus: the movement that made the state supplies the lens through which every later Italian figure — the soldier Garibaldi, the statesmen Cavour and Crispi and Depretis, the meridionalist Fortunato, the generals of the world war — understood and justified his action.

## The interpretations

The sources of this corpus offer two great readings of the Risorgimento. Tivaroni, writing at the close of the century as the historian of the national movement, treats unification as the happy culmination of the Italian people's history, judging the actors by how far they advanced the national goals of Venice, Rome and the completion of the state. Oriani, the philosopher of the nation, reads the whole of Italian history backward to the Risorgimento as its consummation, and is more critical: the new monarchy, in his account, was historically insufficient, the parties were corrupted, and the national achievement was shadowed by the mediocrity of the men who followed Cavour. Garibaldi's *Memorie* stand between them, the testimony of the soldier who made the revolution and then lived to see the state he had served become the prison of his last aspirations. Between the celebrant and the critic, the Risorgimento emerges as the central event and the central idea of the Italian century — the act by which a people, in Tivaroni's closing image, made itself a nation.
