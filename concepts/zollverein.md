# The Zollverein

The Zollverein ("customs union") was the German customs union, founded in 1834 under Prussian leadership, by which the greater part of the German states abolished their internal tariff frontiers and adopted a common tariff toward the outside world. It was the great economic foundation of German unity, and it ran as a parallel and preparatory track to the political unification that this wiki follows: long before the [[north-german-confederation]] and the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]] gave Germany a political form, the Zollverein had already made it an economic unit. The concept covers the union's role in economic unification, its reorganization after 1866, and the federal organs — the Federal Customs Council (*Zollbundesrat*) and the *Zollparlament* ("customs parliament") — through which it was integrated into the new federal state.

## The economic foundation of unity

Lenz presents the Zollverein as one of the two institutions, along with the army reform, by which Prussia "developed institutions which Emperor Wilhelm and Bismarck could take up as constituent elements" in their work. It had been built up against Austria, and in the years of reaction it had been deliberately used by the Berlin government to bind the German states to Prussia. Prussia's possession of the Zollverein gave it, in the view of contemporaries, its one secure position in Germany: as Lenz notes the point that Prussia's constitution was "the only recruiting means Prussia had in Germany," while "in all other directions — Zollverein, material advantages, and so on — Austria would run Prussia a close race." The Zollverein thus stood as the economic counterweight to the Austrian political programme, and it was a chief field on which the [[kleindeutsch-grossdeutsch|kleindeutsch and grossdeutsch]] programmes contended long before the armies met.

## Austria's counter-policy

Because the Zollverein was the engine of Prussian influence, Austria repeatedly sought either to enter it or to break it. Lenz recounts how, in the 1850s, Austria had hoped to create a rival "seventy-million empire" that would "outshine the old Zollverein and drive Prussia from one of its surest positions," and how a treaty of 7 September 1851 bound the Habsburg government to join the old union on 1 January 1854. The Austrian ambassador Arnim was sent to negotiate a commercial treaty with Austria in place of the Zollverein, and the Austrians hoped to force their way into the union on the basis of the old tariff. Prussian policy resisted this, preferring the compact, Prussian-led union to a wider Austria-dominated one. The Bavarian chamber, in which the *Grossdeutschen* ruled, at one point passed resolutions on the Zollverein that came close to its dissolution in favour of a south German special league joining Austria. The union was thus a constant battlefield between the two great powers for the economic allegiance of Germany.

## Reform after 1866

The victories of 1866 transformed the Zollverein along with the German Question. With Austria expelled from German affairs, the way was open to reorganize the customs union as an instrument of the Prussian-led federal state. Sybel's sixth volume, covering the years 1866–67, treats this reorganization in detail under the heading of the "new Zollverein treaties" (*neuen Zollvereinsverträge*): the economic integration of the territories annexed to Prussia, the pressure put upon the southern German states to accept the new terms, and the deliberate linking of economic unity to political unification. A second set of chapters on the "consolidation of the Zollverein question" (*Befestigung der Zollvereinsfrage*) describes how the Customs Union was consolidated as an instrument of Prussian policy through the Federal Customs Council (*Zollbundesrat*), while particularist interests in the south continued to resist.

## The Zollparlament

The parliamentary arm of the reformed union was the Zollparlament, the customs parliament, which drew together elected deputies from across the union, including the southern states that remained outside the [[north-german-confederation]]. The idea of such a body was old in Bismarck's mind — Lenz records that, in the memorandum he drafted in 1861, he proposed a *Zollparlament* as a "surrogate" for the federal institutions that were lacking, since the existing order could only be completed through "voluntary and terminable associations beside the Confederation." After 1866 the Zollparlament gave institutional form to this notion, meeting twice from 1868 onward. Lenz observes that within it, and in the Reichstag, there were "heated debates, and the particularists of north and south clashed sharply," yet the interests themselves were "all by nature related and came to an accommodation through discussion and vote."

## The customs union and the Reich

The Zollparlament was a transitional device that bridged the gap between the economic unity of Germany and its incomplete political unity — for the southern states, inside the customs union but outside the northern federation, were bound to Prussia economically while still politically separate. When the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]] drew the South into the Reich, this economic seam was stitched shut, and the Zollverein was absorbed into the tariff and financial system of the unified Empire. The concept thus illustrates a wider theme of this corpus: the economic integration of Germany, prepared over decades by the Zollverein, ran ahead of and ultimately helped to compel its political integration. What the tariffs had bound together, the armies and constitutions of 1866–71 completed.
