# Conrad's Military Dictatorship and the Doctrine of Preventive War

The doctrine of preventive war as it was developed and pressed by the chief of the Austro-Hungarian general staff, [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]]: the conviction that the salvation of the Dual Monarchy lay in an early, decisive war against its enemies — above all Serbia, and behind Serbia Russia, and in due course Italy — fought before the odds grew irrecoverably worse. It is at once a military doctrine and a programme of political action, and it stood in chronic tension with the civilian leadership of the Monarchy. The name by which contemporaries and historians have known it, the *military dictatorship* of Conrad, captures the ambition of the general staff to bend the state's policy to the demands of the army and the clock of mobilisation.

## Definition: war as the continuation of policy

The doctrine rests on the maxim, which Sosnosky also quotes from the first page of the general staff's tradition, that war is nothing but the continuation of politics by other means. Conrad drew the conclusion that the root of all evil for the Monarchy was the relation to Serbia and to the Russia that stood behind Serbia, and that "at this root the evil had to be seized; everything else was secondary." He recognised two roads to the goal: first, the attainment of a lasting, confederal union of Serbia with the Habsburg Monarchy in a peaceful manner; but if Serbia declined the union and continued its hostile aspirations — and this, he held, was the case that existed — then a warlike settlement at the suitable moment. The suitable moment was, in his view, always passing. Already in 1906, on his appointment as chief of the general staff, he had pointed to this; in 1908–09 he had declared the moment come to make clear conditions with Serbia, foreseeing that the chances of this unavoidable settlement would only grow worse; and in 1913 he held the last moment to have come to carry it out at all with hope of success. If that moment too were neglected, he believed, the Monarchy would drift without a rudder to the doom that threatened it from Serbia and Russia.

## The preventive war against Serbia and Italy

The doctrine embraced, besides Serbia, the other enemies of the Monarchy. Conrad pressed the case for war not only against Serbia but against Italy, whose irredentist agitation and whose renunciation of the alliance, in his reading, made it a foe that must be settled before it could strike. His advocacy was grounded in the very character of the alliance system: the Monarchy was encircled, its opponents arming visibly, and only an early war fought on its own terms could prevent a later war fought at the enemy's convenience. The most famous formulation of the idea is the memorandum of the Monarchy's own minister at Bukarest, Count [[ottokar-czernin]], of 11 March 1914, preserved among Conrad's papers: that one must "have done with the present Serbia" if there were to be peace in the Balkan; and that a warlike preventive measure must not be judged only from the standpoint of consuming the prey — Serbia had been called the *europäischer Blinddarm*, the European appendix, and one operates on the appendix not to keep it but to have done with it. The analogy expressed exactly the preventive logic: war against Serbia was to be fought not for conquest but for relief, to end the perpetual source of infection at the Monarchy's flank.

## The tension with the civilian leadership

The doctrine brought Conrad into open conflict with the Monarchy's civilian statesmen. Against Baron [[alois-aehrenthal]] the divergence over the military requirements of the exposed position grew into a running feud; Conrad pressed for the reinforcement of the army and came into conflict with the minister of foreign affairs over the means he held the situation to demand, until on 30 November 1911 the Kaiser removed him from the post of chief of the general staff and appointed him army inspector. The conflict with the civilian Ballplatz was not one of mere procedure but of principle: where Aehrenthal and, after him, [[leopold-berchtold]] strove to keep the peace and to localise the Balkan crises, Conrad insisted that peace was the road to ruin and that the diplomatic half-measures of the state were wasting the last chances of a successful war. The rupture extended even to the heir, [[franz-ferdinand]], with whom Conrad quarrelled over policy in the manoeuvres of 1913, though the archduke, who had engineered Conrad's reinstatement, remained convinced that the man who had prepared all the plans of war should command the army should war come.

## The outcome: reinstatement and the coming of war

The vindication of the doctrine, as Conrad saw it, came with the war it had prophesied. Reinstated as chief of the general staff on 12 December 1912, in the midst of the Balkan crisis, Conrad watched the last moments pass unused while the policy of the Monarchy let itself be taken prisoner by side-issues — to the joy of its enemies, as he put it. When the [[sarajevo-assassination-1914]] came in June 1914, he greeted it as the war-declaration of Serbia, the hour that at last summoned every man to his place; and in the [[july-crisis-1914]] he threw the whole strength of the army into the war he had so long urged. Yet the sequel was the demonstration of the limits of the doctrine. In the [[battle-of-galicia-1914]] the war that was to save the Monarchy opened in disaster, and Conrad's own closing reflection held that the decision of the war lay in the failure of the agreed first action — the plan whose maker he had been. The military dictatorship of Conrad thus stands as the apotheosis and the bankruptcy of preventive war: the doctrine that brought the Monarchy to the sword it thought it must draw, and to the catastrophe that the sword could not avert.
