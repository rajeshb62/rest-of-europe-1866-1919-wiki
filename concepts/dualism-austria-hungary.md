# The Dual Monarchy

The Dual Monarchy (*Doppelmonarchie*, in Hungarian *dualizmus*, "dualism") was the constitutional system created by the Compromise of 1867 by which Austria and Hungary existed as two separate, self-governing states joined by the shared person of the Habsburg ruler and by a limited set of common affairs. It was the political frame within which the whole Austrian and Hungarian history of this wiki's period unfolded, from 1867 to its dissolution in 1918. The umbrella concept covers the Ausgleich itself, the common ministries, the Delegations, the customs and economic union, and the standing of Croatia and the nationalities within the Hungarian half.

## The dynastic foundation

The basis of the dualist system was the dynasty and the Pragmatic Sanction. Redlich explains that Austria was "the creation of a dynasty" which for more than six centuries had joined almost unmeasured authority to the satisfaction of the needs of many tangled peoples — the hereditary lands, the lands of the Bohemian crown, and the whole territory of the ancient Hungarian crown of St. Stephen. The dynastic idea was that all these, while keeping their historical-political individuality, formed from the standpoint of the head of the House of Habsburg one great condominium. Within this frame Hungary always stood apart: even after the Pragmatic Sanction of 1713 was adopted into Hungarian law in 1723, the kingdom's constitutional position remained fundamentally different. Beksics rests the whole subsequent relation of Hungary to Austria on that Sanction, by which the succession of the House of Habsburg and the inseparable and indivisible possession of the kingdoms was settled.

## Two states, one ruler

The essential structure of the dualism was the identity of the ruler. Beksics, in his chapter on the personal union (*personál-unió*), insists that the tie binding Hungary and Austria was the identity of the ruler's person, and that the union of the two mutually independent states existed for mutual defence and for that alone. As long as the ruler's person was common to Hungary and Austria this personal union and its consequences would stand; only in the single case of the cessation of the identity of the dynasty would new arrangements follow — an eventuality, he wrote in 1892, "entirely outside the range of political possibility." The common affairs (*közös ügyek*, "common matters") — foreign policy, war, and the finance for these — were managed jointly. The Austrian half was the *Reichsrat*-represented kingdoms and lands (Cisleithania); the Hungarian half was the lands of the crown of St. Stephen (Transleithania), including Croatia-Slavonia under the settlement of 1868.

## The common machinery

The common affairs were administered by three joint ministries — for foreign affairs, for war, and for the common finances — responsible to the two states through the Delegations (*delegációk*, "delegations"). The Delegations were the joint parliamentary bodies, drawn from the two parliaments, by which the common affairs were managed and controlled. Beksics devotes a chapter to them and to the common army. The army was the keystone of the Austrian position within the dualism: Sosnosky calls it "the last bulwark" of the crown's authority against the Magyar drive for independence. The customs and economic union bound the two states in a common commercial and monetary system, renewed periodically, while each retained its own finances, government and parliament for internal matters. Hungary kept, besides the common army, its own home-defence force, the *honvédség*, and the extraordinary levy.

## A contested settlement

The dualism was from the first a subject of bitter dispute. Beksics's whole treatise is a Hungarian defence of the Compromise against the claim, then beginning to be taught in the schools, that between Hungary and Austria there was a real union and that Hungary's statehood had been impaired by the dualist system. He argues that the Compromise preserved, and did not impair, the statehood of the Hungarian state, and that Deák's work answered the interests of both Hungary and Austria. Sosnosky, from the German-national side, condemns it: he compares the monarchy to "a wagon drawn by two horses that do not agree and pull apart," holds that the dualism in its present form had become untenable, and writes that "a higher and quite unprejudiced judge has given the verdict — history." Both writers, however, treat the dualism as the inescapable frame of the monarchy's politics: Sosnosky's four possible futures — revision of the dualism, a greater Hungary, trialism, and a federal "Greater Austria" — were all variations on or departures from it. The system endured until the war, when, as Redlich shows, the very structure of the two states — Hungary's refusal to accept the Austrian war-surveillance office, its economic isolation of Austria — became one of the forces of the monarchy's collapse, ending formally with the October Manifesto of 1918.
