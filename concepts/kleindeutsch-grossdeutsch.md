# Kleindeutsch and Grossdeutsch

The terms *kleindeutsch* ("lesser German") and *grossdeutsch* ("greater German") name the two rival solutions to the [[german-question]] — the two answers to the question of how, and over what territory, Germany should be united. The *grossdeutsch* ("greater German") solution would have gathered all the German-speaking lands, including the German provinces of the Habsburg monarchy, into a single national state, ideally under Austrian leadership. The *kleindeutsch* ("lesser German") solution would have united Germany under Prussian leadership, deliberately excluding Austria — because Austria, with its millions of Slavs, Magyars, and Italians, could never be absorbed into a German nation-state without dissolving its national character. The distinction was the central axis of German politics between the failure of 1848 and the wars of unification; the [[german-question]] was finally resolved in 1866–71 on kleindeutsch lines.

## The two programmes

The *grossdeutsch* programme rested on the ideal of a Germany co-extensive with the German language and culture, embracing Austria as its historical heart. It was the natural programme of the Austrian government, of the Catholic and clerical south, and of all those for whom German unity was inseparable from the greatness of the house of Habsburg. In its most ambitious form it would have transformed the Austrian Empire itself into the leading power of a great all-German confederation.

The *kleindeutsch* programme, by contrast, was the programme of Prussian hegemony. It accepted the sacrifice of Austria's German provinces — and therefore of millions of Germans — for the sake of a compact, powerful, Protestant-led state that the non-German peoples of the Habsburg lands could not corrupt. Its partisans were found above all among the liberal nationalists of Prussia and the north, the heirs of the failed Frankfurt Parliament of 1848, who had come to see that the German Question could only be solved by Prussian power.

## The nation against the Danube

Lenz repeatedly stresses how far the kleindeutsch sentiment ran through the whole national movement, even before it was forced to a decision. "However the German parties might conceive their goal," he writes of the 1850s, "they were all, even the Kleindeutschen, with their front toward the Rhine rather than toward the Danube" — that is, they all saw France as the enemy and Austria as at best a rival. This shared orientation was precisely what made Bismarck's policy exceptional: while the nation feared France, Bismarck alone identified Austria as the power that had to be broken. He dreaded nothing more than a Prussian war against France that would spare the Habsburg monarchy, and he aimed instead to let Austria fall in Italy while Prussia gathered strength at home. His difference from his own conservative friends, the *Kreuzzeitung* party who preached the defence of Austria's "legitimate rights," was total: for him the German Question could not be answered while Vienna remained within the German body politic.

## The pressure of the South

The division between the two programmes ran directly through the German states, and nowhere more sharply than south of the Main, where the *grossdeutsche* cause was strong. Lenz describes how, in the years before the decisive struggle, the Bavarian chamber, "in which the Grossdeutschen ruled," passed resolutions on the Zollverein that came close to dissolving it in favour of a south German special league joining Austria. The clerical and Catholic south gravitated to Vienna, while the Protestant and liberal north leaned to Berlin. "That in the nation itself the Grossdeutschen, whether stamped liberal or clerical, were one and all in Habsburg's camp needs no words," Lenz observes — "but even the ranks of the kleindeutsche party felt themselves shaken" by the pressure of the Austrian programme, fearing they might be left to face the darkest reaction on their own.

## The Austrian counter-programme

The grossdeutsch cause was most openly and formidably advanced by the Austrian government itself in the early 1860s. The reform plan of Schmerling, presented at the Frankfurt Congress of Princes in 1863, was in effect a grossdeutsch programme: a reorganized Confederation with an imperial presidency under the Habsburg emperor, a house of princes, and a delegated parliament. It was drawn up with the assistance of the old radical Julius Fröbel, and it aimed to reconcile legitimacy with liberty, the claims of Austria with the demands of the national state, and to found "the new Germany" peaceably at a congress of princes in the city of the old Bundestag under the presidency of the heir of the last imperial dynasty. For Prussia this was an attempt at "the violent coercion of Prussia" through the use of the majority in the Bundestag — and Bismarck met it with contempt and studied inaction, "effacing" Prussia and letting the scheme collapse of its own hollowness. The failure of the grossdeutsch programme at Frankfurt marked the last serious attempt to solve the German Question on the greater-German basis by agreement.

## The kleindeutsch victory

The decision was reached by war. In the [[austro-prussian-war-1866]] Prussia defeated Austria, and the kleindeutsch solution was imposed upon Germany by force: Austria was expelled from German affairs, the Confederation dissolved, and the [[north-german-confederation]] founded under Prussian leadership with no place left for Vienna. What remained was to draw the south German states — among whose populations the grossdeutsch sentiment still ran strong — into the Prussian system, which the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]] and the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]] accomplished. Lenz's account of the constitution Bismarck drafted in December 1866 makes the kleindeutsch character of the whole construction explicit: it was "in its outlines already the constitution which Bismarck gave the North German Confederation and, after the victory over France, transferred to the German Empire," and in it Austria had no place.

The kleindeutsch solution was thus a compromise that satisfied neither pure ideal — it excluded Austria but kept a directly elected Reichstag and a parliament of the nation, elements that had been demanded by the revolutionary and liberal movements of 1848. The *grossdeutsch* dream did not die, but it passed from the realm of the possible into that of nostalgia and grievance, surviving in the Catholic and particularist south and resurfacing, transformed, in the revanchist and pan-German politics of later decades. For the purposes of this corpus, the distinction matters as the frame within which [[otto-von-bismarck]] and [[wilhelm-i]] carried out the foundation of the Empire.
