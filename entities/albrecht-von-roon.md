# Albrecht von Roon (1803–1879)

Albrecht von Roon, Prussian minister of war, was the author of the army reorganization that made the Prussian victories of 1866 and 1870 possible and the central figure of the constitutional conflict that shook Prussia in the early 1860s. In this corpus's frame his story is inseparable from that of the crown he served: he was the man who gave [[wilhelm-i]] the instrument — a strong, professional standing army — that [[helmuth-von-moltke]] used to win Königgrätz and Sedan, and he was the friend and patron who, in the crisis of 1862, summoned [[otto-von-bismarck]] into the ministry to break the deadlock between crown and parliament. He is one of the three figures — the organizer, the strategist, and the statesman — whose combined arts carried Prussia through the wars of unification.

## The army reform

Roon rose with the accession to the regency in 1858. In July of that year he submitted to the Prince Regent a memorandum on the army constitution that, as Lenz records, "opened his way into the ministry." The core of his argument was a critique of the *Landwehr* — the territorial militia that was the second line of the Prussian army. The Landwehr, Roon contended, made one not master in one's own house. At every conflict of opinion between the government and the governed, one would have to calculate and estimate the effect that contested measures of external as well as internal policy might have on the armed part of the people, on the Landwehr; its existence therefore bound the government's hands, exposed it to the harassment of party scribblers, made it dependent on the wind of public opinion, and rendered any free political action impossible. The remedy was to reduce the militia element and build up the regular, professional army, bound to the crown.

Lenz presents Roon as "a man after Wilhelm's heart," obedient so long as the regent kept to a liberal course, but more capable and willing to hold him upright in conflict. He looked with contempt and anger on the "Democrats"; he called himself a reactionary at his first meeting with Gerlach; and from the first hour he was the wedge in the liberal ministry. The reform was carried through in the interest of the old military families and the Junker class: the Landwehr officers and the bourgeois officer candidates were pushed back, and the upper classes of the cadet houses were emptied to secure the new posts for the sons of military families. Crown and nobility, drawn together again by the shock of revolution, stood joined; the army became, in the famous phrase the sources echo, the *rocher de bronze*, the bronze rock, of the throne. Roon himself acknowledged that the king, if advised to give way, would do so — "but," the passionate minister wrote, "I hope to God that He will paralyse my tongue before it agrees." He set everything on spurring on his royal master against the liberal claim to limit the rights of the crown.

## The constitutional conflict

The reform required money, and the liberal Chamber of Deputies, the bearer of the constitutional movement, refused to vote it. There followed the constitutional conflict of the 1860s, in which the crown governed without a lawful budget. It was Roon who sought a way out. In the crisis of June 1861, when the question whether the new king should mark his accession by an homage in the old *ständische* forms set the cabinet against the crown, Roon acted. He was isolated in the ministry, and his object was first of all to strengthen his own position in it — above all to remove Graf Schwerin, the true bearer of liberal policy, from the king's council. On 26 June 1861 he wrested from the king, after long resistance, the commission to look about for other ministers; and on the same day he wrote to President von Möller and Herr von Selchow, and on the next day also to Bismarck at Petersburg. It is uncertain, Lenz notes, whether the letter to Bismarck went out directly on the king's order, but Wilhelm seems to have spoken of him and to have given permission to enquire of him.

Bismarck's reply set out the program that he would carry into office: to hold firm in the military question, to break with the Chamber over it, to dissolve it, and then to show the nation how the king stood with the people. But that first crisis ended in compromise — the homage was given up for a solemn coronation, and the liberal ministers kept their places. When Bismarck arrived in Berlin, he found Roon absent; and the plan came to nothing for the moment.

## The summons of Bismarck and the victory

The final break came in September 1862. In the extremity of the conflict over the military budget, when the crown could find no minister willing to govern against the parliament, it was Roon who at last prevailed on the king to take the step that decided Prussia's future: he called Bismarck from Petersburg to the ministry. Lenz's later reflection on the fall of 1890 marks the significance of that act: "King Wilhelm then seized Bismarck's hand, because he alone of all his servants promised him help in the matter most sacred to his life, and it was done for his salvation." The hand that the king seized had been reached to him by Roon, who had built the army and then brought in the statesman to defend it.

Roon's army reform, once freed from parliamentary obstruction by Bismarck's government, became the instrument of Prussian victory. The reorganized army, commanded on campaign by the General Staff of Moltke, overthrew Austria in 1866 and France in 1870–71. The victories of [[battle-of-koniggratz-1866]] and Sedan, which founded the [[north-german-confederation]] and then the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]], were won by the troops that Roon had organized and the system he had built. He stands, with Moltke and the King, among the founders of the Empire — the organizer whose work made possible what the strategist directed and the statesman guided.

Roon died in 1879, in the first decade of the German Empire his army had helped to create.
