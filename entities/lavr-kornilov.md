# Lavr Kornilov

General Lavr Georgievich Kornilov (1870–1918) was commander-in-chief of the Russian army in 1917, the central figure of the Kornilov movement and the [[kornilov-affair-1917]] of August, and the organizer and first commander of the Volunteer Army that opened the [[white-movement]] in the civil war. His death on 31 March 1918, struck down by a grenade during the assault on Ekaterinodar in the [[ice-march-1918]], made him the martyr of the White cause. Denikin, his chief of staff and successor, wrote the volume of his *Essays on the Russian Troubles* devoted to "the struggle of General Kornilov" — an account both apologetic and precise, treating the affair less as a "mutiny" than as the tragic turning of the revolution against itself.

## The divergence of paths

Denikin opens his second volume by insisting that the revolution had, by the summer of 1917, outgrown the simple picture of two forces — the Provisional Government and the Soviet — facing each other. By July and August it had become a many-sided struggle among government, the soviets, and the supreme command, and after the July days and the crisis of power the Third coalition government of [[alexander-kerensky]] held its authority only formally. Into this vacuum the figure of the commander-in-chief was thrust. Kornilov, in Denikin's account, had no definite political programme before the rising; the document later known as the "Kornilov programme" was the fruit of later collective work. What he had was the conviction that the army and the state were being destroyed, and the resolution to restore discipline. His "memorandum" on army reorganization, supported by Savinkov, demanded the restoration of the commanding authority that the revolution and the committees had broken.

## The Kornilov movement and the rising

The officer corps, after the failure of the June offensive, moved into opposition to the government, and around the commander-in-chief gathered the secret organizations and the public opinion of those who hoped for a strong hand. Denikin is careful with the words: "affair," "rising," "conspiracy," "mutiny" are all too narrow; he prefers the *Kornilov movement* for the whole, and for the days of 27–31 August the *Kornilov rising*. By late August Kerensky still wavered, weighing whether to be with Kornilov against the soviets or with the soviets against Kornilov; Savinkov balanced between them; Kornilov had decided on a change in the structure of power but not on the methods; and General Krymov's troops stood ready. The mission of V. N. Lvov, carrying between the premier and the commander-in-chief, issued in the declaration by Kerensky that the supreme commander was in "mutiny." Kornilov refused to yield; Stavka, the commanders, the Allied representatives, and the Russian public took sides; and when the rising collapsed without a battle, Krymov shot himself and Kornilov was arrested. Denikin's verdict is blunt: Kerensky's victory was the "prelude to Bolshevism" — a strong hand rejected, a disarmed command, and the road left open to the one organized force that would take power.

## Bykhov and the road to the Don

Imprisoned with his associates in the monastery-prison of Bykhov, Kornilov watched the autumn of the revolution from a cell. Denikin, himself among the prisoners, records the life of the Bykhov group, the relations of the prisoners with Stavka and Kerensky, and the elaboration of the plans and the "programme" that would govern the White cause. With the Bolshevik seizure of power in October, the fate of the prisoners hung in the balance; the murder of General Dukhonin at Stavka — who, as acting commander-in-chief, had released them — marked the end of the old army. In the chaos of the first days of Bolshevism the Bykhov prisoners escaped and made their way to the Don, where General Alekseev had already begun the organization of a volunteer force at Novocherkassk. Kornilov's arrival completed the "triumvirate" of Alekseev, Kornilov, and Ataman Kaledin, and the Volunteer Army — the nucleus of the [[white-movement]] — came into being in the winter of 1917–18.

## The First Kuban (Ice) March and death

The army's retreat from the Don under Bolshevik pressure became the First Kuban March, the great ordeal of the White movement. Denikin's narrative follows it from the military council at Olginskaya, through the crossing of the Kuban, the fights at Lezhanka and beyond the Laba, and the desperate passage of the "Ice March" of 15 March at Novo-Dmitrievskaya — rain, water, and mud turning to frost, the men frozen in their coats, Markov's Officer Regiment storming the stanitsa in the dark. Joined by the Kuban volunteers, the army turned on Ekaterinodar and assaulted it. On 31 March 1918, while the army was concentrated for the final assault, a Russian shell struck the headquarters. Denikin records the order: "At 7.30 on 31 March General Kornilov was killed by an enemy shell that hit the army staff. He fell a hero's death — a man who loved Russia more than himself and could not bear her shame." The loss was concealed from the army for a day; then [[anton-denikin]], his chief of staff, took command and lifted the siege. The First Kuban March — the "Ice March" — became the founding epic of the Volunteer Army, and Kornilov its eternal memory.
