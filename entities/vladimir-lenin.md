# Vladimir Lenin

Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, known as Lenin (1870–1924), was the leader of the Bolshevik party and the architect of the [[october-revolution-1917]]. From exile he watched the collapse of the [[autocracy-russia]] in the February revolution, returned to Petrograd in April 1917 with his famous theses calling for the transfer of power to the soviets and the end of the war, and in October led the seizure of power that established the first Soviet government, which he headed until his death in 1924. His part in this wiki's sources is that of the decisive antagonist: Denikin, writing from the staff and White side, treats him as the one leader with the will and the organized following to inherit the ruin that the Provisional Government and the failed command left behind.

## From exile to the February revolution

In 1917 Lenin was in exile abroad, isolated from the Russia whose revolution had broken out in February. Denikin notes the curious circumstance of the first months: the revolution that overthrew the tsar was made by men who did not expect Lenin's doctrines to govern it. Lenin had himself argued in 1905, as Denikin reminds the reader, that "of a socialist revolution by open class struggle against the whole bourgeoisie there could be no question" in Russia — an admission that the leader of 1917 had himself long insisted upon. Yet the war, the breakdown of the state, and the radicalization of the army and the workers created the conditions he had not foreseen as possible. The Provisional Government's prolongation of the war and the failure of the June offensive did the work of agitation for him, and by the autumn the ground was prepared.

## The April theses and the struggle for power

Returning to Petrograd in April 1917, Lenin at once set his course against the Provisional Government and in favor of "all power to the soviets" — a break, Denikin notes, with the patriotic line of the other socialist parties and with the very notion of continuing the war. Denikin quotes the Bolshevik leader's appeal against the government and the commanders and traces the open, almost public process by which the Bolsheviks prepared the seizure of power: the Northern Regional Congress of Soviets, the Petrograd Soviet, the whole Bolshevik press — in which the hidden Lenin wrote under his own name — calling for insurrection; the organization by Trotsky, on 16 October, of the Military-Revolutionary Committee, which claimed the exclusive right to dispose of the Petrograd garrison. Denikin's judgment is that by October there was in all Russia no effective organization besides the Bolsheviks able to claim the heavy inheritance, and that this fact pronounced sentence on the country, the people, and the revolution.

## October and Brest-Litovsk

The October revolution was, in Denikin's account, less a secret conspiracy than a demonstration of strength — power "was to be taken not by conspiracy but by a friendly demonstration of forces," as Trotsky declared in the Soviet a week before the rising. The Provisional Government, isolated and unresisted, was overthrown, and [[alexander-kerensky]]'s attempt to rally troops at Gatchina failed. Lenin's government at once sued for peace, and in March 1918 concluded the [[treaty-of-brest-litovsk-1918]], by which Russia left the war and surrendered vast territories to Germany — a peace that Denikin, with the White cause in view, describes as a "bargain" and a "burning shame," and that stands in the broader frame of the [[total-war-economy]] and the disintegration of the Eastern front. The new regime's first measures — the decree on land, on workers' control, on self-determination of peoples, the break-up of the old army — Denikin records as the beginning of the world the Whites would fight against. Lenin ruled the Soviet state until his death in 1924, having carried his party from exile to the mastery of Russia in the space of seven years.
