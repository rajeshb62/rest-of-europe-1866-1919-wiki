# Hubert Lagardelle

Hubert Lagardelle (1875–1958) was a French socialist publicist and the leading theorist of revolutionary syndicalism in the decade before the First World War. A collaborator of the *Mouvement socialiste*, the review of the syndicalist Left, he devoted his writing to a single question: whether socialism would be absorbed by democracy, or whether, while using democracy for its own ends, it would remain itself as the movement of the working class. His book *Le socialisme ouvrier* (1911), a collection of essays written from 1898 to 1908, marked the stages of his thought through the Dreyfus affair, the ministerialist experiment of Waldeck-Rousseau and Millerand, and the rise of the syndicalist movement. He is the corpus's principal spokesman for the doctrine that the working class must act through its syndicates and direct action rather than through the parliamentary party.

## The stages of a thought

*Le socialisme ouvrier* was not, Lagardelle warned, a literary work. It brought together fragments written "au jour le jour, sous le coup des événements," day by day, under the pressure of events, following in anxiety the transformations of contemporary socialism. He did not conceal the book's vices — its discontinuities, repetitions, disproportions — nor that many parts had grown old, nor that there were affirmations he would no longer present in so absolute a manner. His conceptions, he admitted, had modified themselves as they underwent the test of life; he meant only to mark the stages of a thought. That honesty was itself characteristic of a man who was always re-examining his own doctrine as events outran it.

## Socialism or democracy

The first part of the book, written at the height of the Millerand crisis, posed the founding question of the age: *Socialisme ou Démocratie?* Would socialism be absorbed by democracy, or, while using democracy for its ends, would it remain itself? Lagardelle examined the democratic confusion, the programme adopted at the Congress of Tours (1902), and the conditions of a minimum programme, and set the claims of democracy and class struggle against the practical work of democratic politics and economic organisation. He treated at length the career of Millerand — his *Plateforme électorale* of February 1898, his entry into the ministry of Waldeck-Rousseau alongside the Marquis de Galliffet, the general of the Commune's suppression — as the great test of whether socialism could survive ministerialism. The Dreyfus affair, the amnesty, and the work of the Waldeck-Rousseau ministry (which he called "Dreyfusism in power") gave the question a concrete urgency: could socialists participate in a bourgeois government without ceasing to be socialists?

## The function of the party

Lagardelle's answer turned on the distinction between the action of the party and the action of the class. He analysed corporatism, syndicalism and politics as competing forms, defended socialist unity, and weighed "electoral revolutionaryism" against the patient organisation of the working class. The party, in his syndicalist scheme, could not be the true organ of the workers' emancipation; that role belonged to the syndicate. Democracy, though it might be used, threatened to dissolve the class struggle into a purely political contest, and the parliamentary party risked becoming the means by which the workers were absorbed into the bourgeois state.

## The ideas of syndicalism

The second part of the book set out the syndicalist doctrine that Lagardelle had made his own. He noted the unequal import of the congresses of Amsterdam and Bourges in 1904, and traced the formation of syndicalism as "une formation de l'histoire," a product of history rather than of theory. In his essay on "le révisionnisme de gauche" (November 1904) he observed that there was unanimous agreement that the socialist crisis was due to the divorce of theory and practice. The syndicalist ideas he defended were anti-statism and revolutionary evolution; the syndicate as the organ of the class struggle; direct action and the general strike as the true means of emancipation; and the subordination of the party to the public services the workers controlled. Against the parliamentary path he set the autonomous, economic action of the working class.

## The syndicalist policy toward the peasants

Lagardelle extended his doctrine beyond the industrial workers to the countryside. At the socialist congress of Toulouse (15–20 October 1908) he urged the party to carry its case to the small rural proprietors and the non-working popular masses. If it came to them, he argued, with a programme of the reduction of the State and of financial morality, it would penetrate the rural milieux; if it came with dogmatic programmes and abstract formulas, it would not touch them; if it offered a conservative programme, it would not seize them either. Carried by the idealism that issued from the syndicalist working masses, the party must appear as the only party of political cleanliness and of anti-statist action, making its own the essential claims and fundamental ideas of syndicalism.

## Significance

Lagardelle stands in this corpus as the theorist who carried the syndicalist current of French socialism to its clearest formulation in the years between the Dreyfus affair and the war. His work marks the moment when the syndicalism of the *Bourses du travail* and the general strike confronted the parliamentary socialism of Jaurès and the ministerialism of Millerand, and refused to let the class movement be absorbed into democratic politics. Read alongside Péguy's lament for the republican mystique and Sabatier's survey of the religious orientation of the age, *Le socialisme ouvrier* belongs to the ferment of French ideas on the eve of 1914.
