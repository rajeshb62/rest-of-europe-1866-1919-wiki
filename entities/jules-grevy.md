# Jules Grévy (1807–1891)

The third President of the Republic, from January 1879 to 1887, and the man who consolidated the parliamentary regime that Mac-Mahon had tried to resist. Jules Grévy, the austere lawyer of the Jura, was the president who made the office subordinate to the Chamber — the living proof that the Republic could govern without a strong head of state. His presidency marked the passage from the founding "heroic age" to the settled parliamentary routine, and Hanotaux treats his election with a certain irony.

## The republican of 1848

Grévy belonged to the older republicanism, born before the Second Republic of 1848. He had been associated with the republican movement since his youth; Hanotaux's portrait recalls his origins, his role in 1848, his relations with Lamartine, and his long career as a parliamentary republican. As president of the National Assembly he had shown the reserve and legalism that marked his whole career. He was, above all, a man of the constitution and of the parliamentary majority — the antithesis, in temperament, of the soldier-president Mac-Mahon he succeeded.

## Chief of the republican party

When Thiers died in 1877, in the middle of the [[seize-mai-1877]] crisis, Grévy became the leading figure of the republican party — the steady hand that could hold the union of the 363 together against the Marshal's dissolution. He had already given his name to a celebrated constitutional amendment during the debates of the National Assembly, a motion for a purely parliamentary Republic without a strong presidency. His victory in the senatorial elections of January 1879 and Mac-Mahon's consequent resignation opened the presidency to him.

## President of the parliamentary Republic

Elected president in January 1879, Grévy at once set the tone of his presidency: he would not govern, he would let the Chamber govern. Where Mac-Mahon had asserted the independent authority of the head of state, Grévy accepted that the president stood aside, that ministries rose and fell with parliamentary majorities, and that the Republic was above all the rule of the Assembly. Hanotaux's judgment is that Grévy's election was in itself a fault — for the Republic, Hanotaux argues, needed a president of some authority rather than a pure spectator — yet it was precisely this self-effacement that consolidated the parliamentary regime. Under Grévy the ministries of Waddington, Freycinet, and Ferry carried through the amnesty, the school laws, and the Tunisian campaign while the president looked on.

## The consolidator forced from office

Grévy's very conception of the office made him the personification of the parliamentary Republic's stability through the early 1880s. But the same low-key presidency could not resist the scandals and crises that the maturing regime threw up, and in 1887 he was forced to resign — the victim of the corruption affair that touched his entourage and of the political storms of the Boulanger era. His departure, coming after the death of Gambetta and the fall of Ferry, closed the generation of the founders. Grévy had shown that the Republic could survive without a powerful president; the price was the weakening of the presidency itself.
