# Giuseppe Garibaldi (1807–1882)

Giuseppe Garibaldi, born at Nice in 1807 of a Genoese sailor family, was the soldier of the Italian Risorgimento — the one man of the movement whose name crossed every frontier and outlived every party. In this corpus's frame he is the veteran who, having already conquered the Two Sicilies for the national cause in 1860, fought on through the defeats of the following decade — wounded and captured in the Calabrian hills at Aspromonte, broken at [[battle-of-mentana-1867]], and in his last campaign leading an improvised volunteer army for the French Republic in the Vosges during the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]] — before returning to die quietly on the rocky island of Caprera in 1882. His own *Memorie*, dictated and signed at Civitavecchia in 1875 and published in their diplomatic edition by Ernesto Nathan in 1907, tell the whole story in the first person: a "stormy life, made up of good and evil," written by a man who called himself a hater of tyranny and falsehood, a republican who yet believed a temporary and honest dictatorship necessary at the head of nations "victims of the most pernicious Byzantinism." Tivaroni, surveying the movement in the closing volume of his history, gives him a gallery of portraits — the adventurer, the hero, the general — and judges him before history's bar as the emblem of the armed revolution that made the kingdom.

## The years in South America

The opening of the *Memorie* carries Garibaldi from his Genoese childhood and early voyages and service in the Sardinian navy, through his conversion to the Italian national idea and his flight into exile after the failed rising of Genoa in 1834, to a decade of warfare across the Atlantic. In South America he served the Rio-Grandense republic in its war of independence against the Brazilian Empire — the corsair voyages, the building and arming of a little fleet, the defence of the Rio Grande, and the campaigns with the gaucho republicans that brought him his wife Anita. He knew disaster on the shores of Santa Catarina, shipwreck, and flight across the Sierra. Then came the passage to Uruguay and the defence of Montevideo against the siege of Oribe and Rosas, where he formed the Italian Legion and won the soldier's fame that carried his name home. It was from the Plata that he resolved, in 1848, to return and offer his sword to Italy, sailing with sixty-three volunteers on 15 April 1848.

## The Roman Republic

The second period of the *Memorie* is the return and the revolutionary year. Garibaldi offered his and the Legion's services to the Italian cause in the Lombard war against Austria, entered Rome, and took part in the defence of the Roman Republic of 1849 against the French army of Oudinot. The siege of Rome, the fighting at the gates, his wounding, and the heroic retreat with the remnants of his command across central Italy — pursued through the Romagna until the pursuit died out and the survivors dispersed — made him, as Tivaroni's retrospective holds, the symbol of the Italian revolution. From that episode his fame ceased to be merely that of a capable commander of volunteers and became legendary; the fugitive who had defied the French before Rome was henceforward the living standard of the *partito d'azione*, the party of action that stood apart from the constitutional monarchists.

## The Expedition of the Thousand (1860)

Garibaldi's greatest feat, and the centre of the whole Risorgimento as his memoirists tell it, was the [[expedition-of-the-thousand-1860]] of May–October 1860. The third period of the *Memorie* opens with a hymn to Sicily — "genitrice degli Archimedi," the land of the Sicilian Vespers and of the two lads of Syracuse — the island that was to "wake the sleepers." The account runs from the embarkation at Quarto and the landing at Marsala, through the astonishing victory at Calatafimi (15 May 1860) and the taking of Palermo (27 May 1860) and the reduction of the island, to the crossing of the Straits of Messina, the landing on the Neapolitan mainland, the assault of Reggio, and the advance on Naples; it closes with the decisive victory of the Volturno (1–2 October 1860, with Bronzetti at Castel Morrone and the fighting at Caserta Vecchia). By that campaign the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies was delivered to the national cause, and the Thousand and the southern volunteers became the heroes of a whole generation. Oriani, writing from the other political pole, nevertheless treats this as the revolutionary conquest that supplied the monarchy its second half — the royal annexation of the North and centre balanced by Garibaldi's popular conquest of the South.

## Aspromonte and the Trentino

The fourth period of the *Memorie* (1860–1870) is one of disillusion and defeat. In 1862 Garibaldi marched again, this time against Rome, in a movement Victor Emmanuel's government could not sanction; at Aspromonte the royal troops met him in Calabria, wounded him, and took him prisoner, and he was held at the Varignano before returning to Caprera. In the war of 1866 he led the advance into the Trentino — the only bright spot of an otherwise disastrous [[italian-war-1866]] — threatening Riva and Trento while the main armies stumbled toward [[battle-of-custoza-1866]]. His own *Memorie* and Tivaroni's history agree that the Trentino campaign showed him at his best, pressing the Alpine passes with his volunteers as far as prudence and the faintheartedness of his superiors allowed. And then came the catastrophe of [[battle-of-mentana-1867]] (3 November 1867), where the attempt to seize Rome for the Republic was crushed at the gates of the Papal city by the Papal troops and the French reinforcements. Garibaldi ended the period a prisoner and exile of his own island.

## The campaign of France and the return to Caprera

The fifth and last period is the campaign of 1870–71. Called to the service of the French Republic after Sedan, Garibaldi commanded the improvised Army of the Vosges against the Prussians: the combats of Lentenay and Autun (21–23 January 1871), the three days' holding of Dijon, and the final retreat ordered when the enemy masses, freed by the fall of Paris and the armistice, gathered to envelop the small army. Elected a deputy to the National Assembly at Bordeaux, he took his seat "with the sole intent of carrying my vote to the unhappy Republic," and, seeing nothing more could be done, returned to Caprera, where he arrived on 16 February 1871. His Army of the Vosges, composed of too republican elements, was dissolved by the government of Thiers. The *Memorie* close with his death at Caprera in 1882, and Tivaroni's portrait of "Garibaldi at Caprera" fixes the final image of the exile-general who, having given the nation its kingdom, retired to a barren rock and the memory of a thousand battles.
