# Benedetto Cairoli

Benedetto Cairoli (1825–1889) was an Italian statesman of the Risorgimento, prime minister of the Kingdom of Italy in the years 1878–79, and a leading figure of the parliamentary Left that took power in 1876. In the corpus's frame he appears as a survivor of the heroic age of the national movement carried into the prosaic work of governing — a veteran of the revolutionary wars, brother of the Cairoli brothers who fell as volunteers, and the head of the Left whose irredentist leanings and whose conduct of foreign policy toward Tunisia and the colonial question defined the second period of the monarchy. His premiership marked the moment when the generation of the unification, having risen on revolutionary enthusiasm, had to confront the realities of a poor, newly united state.

## A revolutionary family

The name Cairoli belonged, in the memory of the Risorgimento, to a family of heroes. Oriani, evoking the volunteers of the Thousand and the national wars, recalls "intere famiglie di fratelli come i Cairoli" — whole families of brothers like the Cairolis — and numbers among the legionaries the Cairoli brothers who were to die in the ventures of the movement. The family was one of those "ultimi" — the last of the line — who went to their deaths in the republican expeditions; at the tragic enterprises of the later 'sixties the last Cairoli brothers "dovevano morirvi," were to die there. It was out of this world of the *partito d'azione*, of Mazzinian volunteers and Garibaldian legionaries, that Benedetto Cairoli came, as one of those political men, long unknown, who were to become ministers. In 1867 he was president of the central committee of the party of action at Turin, which, at the moment of the attempted insurrections, "titubò malgrado tutte le istanze di Mazzini" — hesitated despite all Mazzini's urgings.

## The advent of the Left

After the taking of Rome in 1870 and the law of the Guarantees, the older generation of the national movement passed from revolution into the state. The advent of the Left in 1876, which closed the long supremacy of the Right under Cavour's successors, brought to power the men of the *partito d'azione* — Cairoli, Depretis, Crispi, the radicals of the historical Left. Oriani treats this as the opening of "il secondo periodo monarchico," the second monarchical period, in which the Left, the party of the men who had once been Garibaldians and conspirators, took the direction of the kingdom. Cairoli stood among the principal leaders of this new ruling group, the survivors of the heroic generation now charged with the government of a state that had emerged from the Risorgimento exhausted and poor.

## The premiership

Cairoli became prime minister in March 1878, at the head of a Left ministry. The succession of ministries in these years was rapid and confused — Fortunato's list of the Thirteenth Legislature records the alternation of Depretis and Cairoli ministries: the first ministry of Depretis (1876–77), a second of Depretis (1877–78), then "1° Min. Cairoli, dal marzo al dicem. '78," the first Cairoli ministry from March to December 1878; then a third Depretis ministry, a second Cairoli ministry from July to November 1879, and a third Cairoli ministry from November 1879. This was the era of unstable parliamentary majorities that Fortunato, as a young deputy of the dissidence, criticised — the era in which the Left, having broken the Depretis majorities, could not itself hold power steadily. Cairoli's own tenure was short and embattled, caught between the factions of his own party.

## Irredentist leanings

Cairoli's politics carried the mark of his revolutionary past. As a leader of the Left he was associated with the irredentist tendency — the current of opinion, strong among the men of the *partito d'azione*, that looked to the redemption of the "unredeemed" Italian lands still under Austrian rule, Trentino and Trieste. Fortunato, addressing the electors of Melfi, invoked Cairoli and Zanardelli by name in the context of the national cause and the Italian "fratelli" beyond the frontier, praying that the state would not "togliere con una mano ciò che diamo con l'altra" — take away with one hand what it gives with the other. The irredentist sympathy was a source of difficulty for the monarchy, straining its relations with Austria-Hungary and complicating the very alliances on which Italy's security rested.

## The colonial reverses

The great failure of Cairoli's years in office lay in the colonial and foreign field. The "conquista africana" — the African conquest — which Oriani treats as the theme of the second monarchical period, led Italy into the Red Sea and toward the colonial adventures that ended in the disaster of Dogali (24 January 1887). The reverses in Tunisia — where France established her protectorate in 1881, to the chagrin of the Italian colonial party — and the subsequent African reverses fell upon the governments that followed the Left's advent, and the irredentist-cum-colonial policy with which Cairoli's name was linked contributed to the mood of disappointment that Fortunato chronicled. The defeat at Dogali, closing Oriani's narrative, marked the bitter end of the African phase begun in the years of Cairoli and the Left.

## Legacy

Cairoli died in 1889, at the end of the decade that had seen the Left in power and the beginnings of the African and colonial disappointments. He stands in this corpus as the representative of the revolutionary generation turned statesman — the Garibaldian and party-of-action veteran who took his place among the leaders of the Left, whose irredentist sympathies and whose ministries of 1878–79 marked the passage from the Risorgimento to the mundane politics of the new kingdom, and whose name was linked to the colonial reverses that Oriani recorded. He embodies the tension between the heroic ideal of the national movement and the difficult realities of the Italian state that Fortunato, from the South, so persistently lamented.
