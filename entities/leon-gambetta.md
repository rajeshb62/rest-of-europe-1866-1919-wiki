# Léon Gambetta (1838–1882)

The orator who founded the Third Republic. Léon Gambetta, son of a Genoese grocer, was the tribune of the republican opposition under the Second Empire, the proclaimer of the Republic on 4 September 1870, the animator of national defence in the provinces during the war, and for a decade the leading voice — and at the last the minister-president — of the parliamentary Republic. His death in 1882 closed the "heroic age" of the Republic's foundation, the period Hanotaux himself called by Gambetta's name.

## The tribune of the opposition

Gambetta's rise was the rise of democratic oratory. Hanotaux's portrait traces his origins and intellectual formation: a provincial of modest means who came to the bar and to politics, made his name in the great trials of the Empire's last years, notably the Baudin subscription of 1869, and was elected to the Corps législatif. He embodied the republican Left that fought the liberal Empire of [[napoleon-iii]] as an inadequate concession. When the Empire fell in the disaster of Sedan, it was Gambetta who, on 4 September 1870, proclaimed the Republic from the balcony of the Hôtel de Ville and demanded the *guerre à outrance* that the more prudent republicans — and above all Thiers — refused.

## The war and the Assembly

During the war of 1870–71 Gambetta left Paris by balloon to direct national defence in the provinces, the incarnation of resistance to the Prussian advance. Yet the elections of 8 February 1871, held under the shadow of defeat, returned a monarchical Assembly committed to peace, and Gambetta was forced to bend to the country's verdict. As deputy he stood for the Republic within an Assembly that had none, and by 1872 he had become, in Hanotaux's account, the leader around whom the republican party organized itself.

## The Grenoble speech and the 'nouvelles couches'

It was at Grenoble on 26 September 1872, in the second oratorical campaign that carried him through Savoie and the Dauphiné, that Gambetta proclaimed the advent of democracy in politics — the celebrated speech on the "new social stratum" (*nouvelles couches sociales*), the rising middle and popular classes who were henceforth to govern France. The same campaign named clericalism the remaining enemy, and Hanotaux records the watchword that would follow the Republic for a generation: *« Le cléricalisme, voilà l'ennemi ! »* ("Clericalism — there is the enemy!"). The speech alarmed the right and electrified the republican Left; it fixed Gambetta as the dangerous man of the new France that the monarchists had to stop.

## Leader of the republican party

After the fall of Thiers in 1873 and the failure of the monarchical campaign, Gambetta led the republican opposition through the *ordre moral* and the septennat. His provincial tours, his interventions in the Assembly, and his leadership of the Left made him the presumptive chief of the coming parliamentary Republic. He pressed Mac-Mahon's ministries, demanded that the president submit to the parliamentary majority, and was the soul of the union of the 363 that resisted the [[seize-mai-1877]] dissolution. His challenge — that the Marshal must *se soumettre ou se démettre* ("submit or resign") — became the programme of the republican victory of 14 October 1877.

## President of the Chamber and the ministry

With the parliamentary Republic triumphant after 1879, Gambetta became president of the Chamber of Deputies, holding the tribune he had so often dominated. Only in 1881–82 did he at last form the ministry that bore his name, the "grand ministry" of his friends and admirers. It was a disappointment: his project of constitutional revision and *scrutin de liste* was beaten, and the government fell after a short tenure. Hanotaux treats the failure of the Gambetta cabinet as cutting the wings of hope and disconcerting the logic of events. Gambetta died in 1882 at the height of his legend, and Hanotaux suspends his narrative at that death — a brutal conclusion after which "figures and the aspect of things change." For Hanotaux, Gambetta was the founder of the Republic and the democratic tribune whose voice had called it into being.
