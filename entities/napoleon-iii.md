# Napoleon III (1808–1873)

**Charles-Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte**, nephew of Napoleon I, President of the Second Republic and, from the *coup d'état* of 2 December 1851 and the proclamation of December 1852, Emperor of the French as Napoleon III. In the frame of this wiki he is the sovereign of the [[second-empire-france]] through its transformation into the [[liberal-empire]] and into the catastrophe of 1870, in which he lost his throne, his capital and his liberty. The corpus gives him a double portrait: the republican historian Taxile Delord measures him as a dynastic adventurer watched by a hostile Opposition, while Émile Ollivier, the liberal statesman who served him to the last, defends his intentions and the sincerity of his constitutional turn. Both agree that the Empire ended not merely at the hands of the Prussians but by what Ollivier calls its own "suicide."

## The Emperor at the close of 1866

Coming out of the war of 1866, in which France had stood aside while Prussia overwhelmed Austria, Napoleon III faced the immediate problem of prestige. Delord records the universal sense that Sadowa had shifted the centre of gravity of Europe and that the French Empire, founded on military glory and the "idea of nationality," needed a success of its own. The opening of 1867 saw the Emperor announce, in his letter of **19 January**, a regulated right of interpellation and the promise of press and meetings laws — the first steps of a liberal turn that would occupy the remaining years of the reign. His ministers and his Court, the *parti de l'action* gathered around the Empress, were not uniformly agreed on the pace of reform; Delord notes that the Emperor's concessions were extracted under the pressure of opinion and of a parliamentary Opposition newly emboldened after 1866.

## The Luxembourg crisis and the compensations question

In 1867 Napoleon sought a compensation for the Prussian aggrandisement that could be shown to the nation. Delord recounts how France, refused any cession on the left bank of the Rhine, was left by Bismarck to seek compensations in Belgium and Switzerland, and how the affair settled on Luxembourg — a Dutch possession whose fortress and garrison were under the German Confederation. A secret Franco-Dutch understanding on cession was broken, and the crisis brought France and Prussia to the edge of war before the powers agreed to a conference in London that neutralised the duchy in May 1867. The Luxembourg affair, in Delord's telling, was a humiliation for Napoleon: he had claimed compensation and obtained only a neutralised fortress, while the European concert, with Prussia at its centre, policed the settlement. The episode set the tone of the late Empire — every French move watched, every concession parried by Berlin. See [[luxembourg-crisis-1867]].

## The Mexican adventure and Mentana

The same years darkened the imperial record abroad. The Mexican expedition, undertaken to plant an empire under the Archduke Maximilian, ended in 1867 with the French evacuation and Maximilian's execution at Querétaro. Delord gives the episode as a study in responsibility: Napoleon III left to Washington the choice of the Mexican president and withdrew, while Maximilian's clerical experiment collapsed before the Juarists; the French public read the death of the archduke as a moral disaster. On the other hand, in the Roman question the Emperor was drawn the opposite way. When Garibaldi invaded the Papal States in 1867, the Empress pressed for intervention and Napoleon, after hesitation, sent troops who fought the Garibaldians at **Mentana** in November 1867 — a success that Delord treats as a checkmate of the Empire's own principles, since France now sustained the Pope against the very principle of nationality on which the Empire claimed to stand. See [[battle-of-mentana-1867]] and [[capture-of-rome-1870]].

## The liberal turn and the ministry of 2 January 1870

Under the pressure of the elections of 1869 and of a resurgent Opposition, Napoleon III moved decisively toward a parliamentary regime. Delord's last chapters describe the "Préface de l'Empire libéral": the negotiations with Émile Ollivier, conducted through Clément Duvernois; Ollivier's conditions and programme; the disguised journey of the Emperor to Compiègne to meet him; the hesitations; and finally the Emperor's letter of **27 December 1869** and the formation of the **ministry of 2 January 1870**, in which Ollivier became the effective head of government. The senatus-consult that followed gave the Corps législatif a share in power and made the ministers responsible to the Chambers. To Delord, a republican, this was the authoritarian Empire conceding what it had suppressed; to Ollivier, it was the honest attempt to reconcile the dynasty with liberty. Sybel, writing from the German side, treats the same evolution as a weakening of France — an empire hesitating between the *parti de l'action* and the liberal ministries, unable to pursue a coherent foreign policy. This transformation is the subject of the concept page [[liberal-empire]].

## The foreign-policy missteps and the Hohenzollern affair

The liberal Empire inherited the Emperor's foreign difficulties. The Spanish revolution of 1868, which toppled Isabella II, left a vacant throne that the French government feared might pass to a Prussian prince; Sybel narrates at length how Marshal Prim, at the head of the Spanish regency, sought a candidate and eventually turned to Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen. When Leopold's acceptance was revealed in July 1870, the crisis that followed — the [[hohenzollern-candidature-1870]], the negotiation at Ems, the [[ems-dispatch-1870]] — swept away the diplomacy Ollivier and the Emperor had been conducting. The Emperor, whose health Delord and Ollivier both record as failing and whose personal command of the army was to prove disastrous, was pushed by the war party and by his own Court toward a war for which, in Ollivier's later judgment, the responsibility rested with Bismarck's provocation.

## The war, Sedan and captivity

In July 1870 France declared war on Prussia. Ollivier, who carried the declaration, later defended the decision as forced upon the ministry by the Prussian outrage. The opening campaigns — [[battle-of-woerth-1870]], Forbach — went against the French; by mid-August the Emperor, ill and stripped of effective command, had resigned the direction of the army to Marshal Bazaine and retired toward Châlons. His presence with Mac-Mahon's army of relief drew that army into the trap of the Belgian frontier, and on **1 September 1870** the whole force was encircled at **Sedan** ([[battle-of-sedan-1870]]). Napoleon III, seeing further resistance hopeless, ordered the white flag raised and surrendered in person to the King of Prussia. Hanotaux records that at Sedan "Napoleon III did not only make himself prisoner": the capitulation of the emperor and of an entire army opened a governmental void — the "vacance du pouvoir" in Thiers's phrase — that the proclamation of the Republic on 4 September filled by revolution. The dynasty had fallen with its sovereign.

## Exile and death

Carried into captivity at Wilhelmshöhe, the deposed emperor was released after the armistice and took refuge in England at Chislehurst, where he lived out the defeat in company with the Empress and their son. Hanotaux notes his death on **9 January 1873**, at the approach of his sixty-fifth year, a sudden event that moved French politics on the eve of the monarchical restoration crisis. He died never having reigned again, the founder of [[bonapartism]] leaving his name and cause to a son, the Prince Imperial, who would die young in Africa.
