# Alexander II

Alexander II (1818–1881), Emperor of All the Russias from 1855, enters this wiki's frame already the *Tsar-Liberator* (*Царь-Освободитель*) — the author of the emancipation of the serfs (1861) and of the whole complex of [[zemstvo-great-reforms]] that reshaped Russian life in the 1860s. In the years 1866–1881, which Kornilov's course treats as one long reactionary period, his story is that of a reformer caught between the consequences of his own work and a revolutionary movement that answered reform with terror. The arc runs from the Karakozov attempt of 4 April 1866, through the Russo-Turkish war of 1877–78 and the rise of the [[narodnaya-volya]] (\"People's Will\"), to the catastrophe of 1 March 1881, when the emperor was assassinated on the very eve of the constitutional experiment he seemed at last ready to grant. His reforms before 1866 belong to the preceding wiki of 1800–1865; here the emphasis falls on the reaction that followed the first shot and on his death.

## The Karakozov attempt and the onset of reaction

Kornilov dates the reaction precisely: it opened with the attempt on the emperor's life by Dmitry Karakozov on 4 April 1866, and thereafter coloured the whole remaining fifteen years of the reign. The shot did not halt the gradual development of Russian internal life after the reforms of the 1860s — the peasant reform was extended to the appanage and state peasants, the Municipal Statute of 1870 and Miliutin's military reforms with universal military service (1874) were carried through, the new judicial institutions opened and the zemstvo spread. But it turned the temper of the highest governing circles. Two men came to stand for the two opposite sides of Alexander II's reign: Count D. A. Tolstoy, minister of public instruction, who after 1866 pressed the reactionary line of classicism in the schools, the restriction of the universities, and the struggle against the zemstvo school; and D. A. Miliutin, the reforming war minister, who in Kornilov's reading represented the reforming side of the same emperor. Tolstoy's allies were the publicist Mikhail Katkov and, behind both, the conviction that the programme of the previous decade had gone too far.

## Zemstvo, court, and press under reaction

The reaction touched every institution the Great Reforms had created. The zemstvo, which had been granted a sphere of local activity and its own budgets in the 1860s, found itself fighting the government over the right to tax trade and industry, and watched its field of activity steadily narrowed as the ministry struggled with it. The new courts, opened with such promise, were slowly hemmed in: Count Pahlen's procuracy reasserted control, special procedures were devised for state crimes, and cases were gradually withdrawn from the jury. The press, which the 1860s had loosened, was subjected again to the censor's hand, and the principal organs of opinion hardened into their camps — the reactionary papers of Katkov on one side, the populist journals on the other. The mood of society soured with the reaction, and the gap between the reforming promise of the early years and the closing bureaucracy of the later ones became the underlying complaint of educated Russia.

## The popular masses and the populist movement

Beneath this struggle of government and society lay the unresolved peasant question. Kornilov's account of the economic condition of the peasantry in the 1860s–1870s is sombre: pauper allotments, the *otrezki* (\"cut-offs\") of land taken from the peasants at the emancipation, rising lease prices, arrears of taxes and redemption payments, and the famines of 1868 and of Samara in 1872–1873. The peasants' insufficiency of allotments and the burden of payments became the standing subject of zemstvo investigations and of the populist literature. From this soil grew the movement of the intelligentsia "to the people" — the student disturbances of 1869, Nechaev and his conspiratorial methods, the Chaikovtsy circle, the theories of Bakunin and Lavrov, and ultimately the revolutionary society *Zemlya i Volya* (\"Land and Freedom\"), which by the end of the 1870s had given way to the terrorist party [[narodnaya-volya]] that set its sights on the person of the emperor himself.

## The Eastern Question and the war of 1877–78

Foreign affairs meanwhile carried Russia into the [[russo-turkish-war-1877-78]]. The Eastern Question, the rivalry of Russian and English interests in Asia, the conquest of the Caucasus and the Central Asian khanates, and above all the troubles of the Balkan Slavs under Turkish rule — the Bulgarian massacres, the Serbian war — drove the government to war with Turkey. Russia's victory brought the Peace of San Stefano, only to be revised at the Congress of Berlin (1878) before the other great powers, and the economic and financial cost of the war, with the resignation of Finance Minister Reutern, added to the discontent at home. The war stirred the Slavophiles and revived the zemstvo movement; the government, appealing for society's support, met instead with zemstvo addresses demanding reform. The revolutionary movement, far from quieting, organized into the party that would make the assassination of the emperor its central aim.

## Loris-Melikov and the catastrophe of 1 March 1881

After a series of attempts on his life — including the explosion in the Winter Palace itself — the emperor established the Supreme Administrative Commission headed by Count Loris-Melikov, who sought to combine the repression of the terrorists with concessions to the liberals: senatorial inspections, attention to the peasant question, the beginnings of a rapprochement with the constitutional movement, and finally a report proposing a special preparatory commission of elected representatives. Revolutionaries and liberals alike watched him warily. Kornilov records that Loris-Melikov, confident of his system's success, misjudged the revolutionaries' strength. On 1 March 1881, while the emperor, who had twice been kept from Sunday outings by Loris-Melikov's warnings, drove back through the capital to the Winter Palace, the *Narodnaya Volya* struck. The conspiracy of Zheliabov and Perovskaya, the bomb-throwers stationed along the route, the mines laid under the streets — all came together as the first bomb wounded the emperor and the second, thrown as he lingered over the wounded, killed him. The assassination of Alexander II not only cut short the possible reform of Loris-Melikov but handed the throne to a son resolved on autocracy, and so closed the reign of the Tsar-Liberator with the reaction that would mark the age of [[alexander-iii]].
