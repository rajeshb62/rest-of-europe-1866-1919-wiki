# Luigi Cadorna (1850–1928)

Luigi Cadorna, born in 1850, was Chief of Staff of the Italian army from July 1914 to November 1917 — the general who commanded the Italian front through the first three years of the [[italy-ww1-entry-1915|Italian war]], conducted the endless offensive battles of the Isonzo, and was dismissed on 8 November 1917 in the midst of the retreat that followed [[battle-of-caporetto-1917]]. His two volumes, *La guerra alla fronte italiana fino all'arresto sulla linea della Piave e del Grappa* (1921–23), are the official account of his own command, carried down to the moment of his removal; and Attilio Frescura's *Diario di un imboscato* supplies the soldier's view of the same years, including a chapter on the dismissal itself. Cadorna was a soldier of the old school — rigid, exacting, and unforgiving — and his name became, for the war generation, the symbol both of the terrible grinding of the Isonzo and of the disaster of Caporetto.

## Italy's war and the plan of operations

When the European war broke out in 1914, Cadorna was made Chief of Staff and charged with preparing the army that the neutrality of 1914–15 allowed to mobilise. His first volume opens with the condition of Italy at the outbreak — the army, the country, the government and parliament — and the military preparation during the period of neutrality in 1915, before the kingdom declared war on Austria on 24 May 1915. The plan of operations was the offensive on the Isonzo and the Julian front, where the boundary with Austria ran through the mountains toward the plains of the Veneto: Cadorna committed Italy to an offensive strategy that sought to break through the Austro-Hungarian lines and to drive toward Trieste, and to defend on the Alpine and Trentino fronts. It was a plan of frontal attack, and it set the pattern of the war on the Italian front for three years.

## The Isonzo offensives and the Strafexpedition

Cadorna's command was a series of offensives on the Isonzo — the First through Fourth Battles of 1915, the Battle of Gorizia (August 1916, the Sixth), and the great autumn struggles on the Carso — each a grinding assault against the Austrian entrenchments with heavy losses and limited gains. He also had to meet the Austrian counter-stroke: the *Strafexpedition*, the "punitive expedition" launched from the Trentino in the spring of 1916, which Cadorna's first volume describes at length — the Austrian offensive from the Trentino against the First Army, and the Italian counter-offensive that checked it. Through 1917 he pressed on, with the offensive on the Giulia front in May (the Tenth Battle) and the battle between the Idria and the Timavo in August–September (the Eleventh Battle), in the *[[isonzo-battles-1915-17]]* that were the central experience of the Italian soldier. Frescura, who served through these years, records the "infinite gradations of the shirker" and the rearguard life of a war that spent its strength in the trenches and the Carso.

## Caporetto and the dismissal

The end came in October 1917. On 24 October 1917 the Austro-German attack fell on the Second Army at Caporetto, broke the front, and dissolved the line in a retreat that Cadorna's second volume narrates in detail — the retreat to the Tagliamento, the retreat from the Tagliamento to the Piave, and the defensive line of the Piave, where the retreat was finally arrested in November 1917. The disaster cost the army hundreds of thousands of prisoners and guns, and it cost Cadorna his command: on 8 November 1917 he was dismissed and replaced by [[armando-diaz]]. Frescura's chapter "Cadorna" describes the reorganisation of the Supreme Command that followed, and the official account of the war would henceforward be Diaz's. Cadorna spent the rest of his life defending his record — the two volumes are in large part that defence — and he died in 1928, remembered above all as the general of the Isonzo and the casualty of Caporetto. His own narrative of the retreat insists that the line was held on the Piave, and rebuts the view that Marshal Foch came to Italy as adviser and inspirer of the retreat, arguing rather that Foch opposed it.
