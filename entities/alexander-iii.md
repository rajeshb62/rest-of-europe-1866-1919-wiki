# Alexander III

Alexander III (1845–1894), Emperor of All the Russias from the assassination of his father on 1 March 1881 to his own death in 1894, was the autocrat of the counter-reforms. Where his father had been the Tsar-Liberator, he was, in the language of the age, the *mírotvorets* (\"peacemaker\") — a title his contemporaries bestowed on the emperor who kept Russia at peace for thirteen years and won the French alliance. Yet at home his reign meant the deliberate undoing of much of the reforming impetus of the 1860s: the tightening of autocracy under the influence of [[konstantin-pobedonostsev]], the zemstvo captains and the counter-reform of local institutions, the reaction in education and the press, and the elevation of finance — under Vyshnegradsky and then the young [[sergei-witte]] — into the instrument of a rearmed and protected state. Witte, who served him, calls him a great emperor undervalued by contemporaries; Kornilov, a liberal historian, treats him as the embodiment of the reaction that closed the century.

## The unready heir and the first steps

Witte stresses that Alexander III, as the second son of Alexander II, was never prepared for the throne: his elder brother Nicholas was the heir, and when that brother died the future emperor found himself upon it with little of the education reserved for a crown prince. He came to power on 1 March 1881 convinced of the justice of autocracy. In the highest governing circles the first weeks of the reign saw a struggle between the constitutional tendencies of Loris-Melikov and the reactionaries gathered around Pobedonostsev. At the conference of 8 March 1881, called to consider Loris-Melikov's project of a representative commission, the emperor heard his old tutor [[konstantin-pobedonostsev]] denounce the plan as the culmination of the "talking-shops" — the zemstvos, the new courts, the licentious press — and Pobedonostsev had his way. On 29 April 1881, to the surprise of the ministers, the manifesto appeared declaring the firm resolve to uphold and defend the *samoderzhavie* (\"autocracy\") against all encroachments. Loris-Melikov resigned, and the reaction had found its direction.

## The counter-reforms

The reaction unfolded through the 1880s. Under the ministry of N. P. Ignatiev and then, after his fall, under the decisive hand of Count D. A. Tolstoy, the government set itself against the institutions of the preceding reign. The guiding idea, as Kornilov records the phrase of the ruling circles, was to create in the localities a "strong authority close to the people" — an administrative tutelage that would also restore the nobility to an honored and influential place. This found its clearest expression in the law of 12 July 1889 on the land captains (*zemskie nachalniki*): men drawn from the landed nobility, armed with wide powers of tutelage over the peasants and their self-government and courts. The Statute on zemstvo institutions of 12 June 1890 followed, drawing the zemstvo into the system of state institutions and cutting back the independence it had enjoyed since 1864; the justices of the peace were abolished, their functions divided between the circuit courts and the new land captains. In the schools the reaction reigned under Delianov and Tolstoy, in the press under the new law of 1882, and against the non-Orthodox and the Jews the government moved to persecution. The nobility's jubilee of 1885 and the programme of Pazukhin gave the reaction its social programme: the defence of the nobility as the chief support of the throne.

## Finance: Vyshnegradsky and the beginning of the Witte system

On the financial side the reign belongs first to I. A. Vyshnegradsky. After the humane and democratic-minded Bunge had been hounded from the finance ministry at the start of 1887, Vyshnegradsky — a technologist, professor, and speculator rather than a theorist — took the direction sharply rightward. Kornilov describes his system: the accumulation of large cash reserves in the treasury, intervention in foreign exchange markets to support the credit ruble, and the extreme development of protectionism, carried to its height in the new tariff of 1891. The economic policy strained the taxable capacity of the people and subordinated the factory legislation of Bunge's years to the wishes of large industry. At the same time the groundwork was laid for the monetary reform that [[sergei-witte]], who followed Vyshnegradsky in the ministry, would carry through under the next reign — the gold standard toward which the finance ministers of the century had all in some measure striven. Witte, brought by Alexander III from the South-Western railways to the department of railway affairs and then, after the catastrophe at Borki (which his own warnings had nearly prevented), to the ministry of ways of communication and the finance ministry, was the emperor's own discovery; the two men's relationship, as Witte tells it, was one of mutual regard, and the emperor's death in 1894 cut short a minister's service he had come to value.

## Foreign policy: the Franco-Russian alliance and the Far East

In foreign affairs the reign was marked by the pursuit of peace and the reorientation of Russia's alliances. The conquest of Turkmenia and the check on English rivalry in Central Asia were accomplished, and the Balkan complications with Bulgaria managed. Most importantly, when the new German Emperor Wilhelm II proclaimed the existence of the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria, and Italy directed in effect against Russia, Alexander III answered by announcing his own alliance with France. The French fleet's visit to Kronstadt and the Russian reply at Toulon, the French crowds' enthusiasm — Kornilov records these as the outward signs of a new European system. The Franco-Russian rapprochement, in his judgment, put an end to Bismarck's design of a final blow against France and for a comparatively long time secured the European peace; it was for this that some called Alexander III the peacemaker. In the last years of the reign the same emperor's diplomacy turned increasingly to the Far East, and the building of the Siberian railway — begun on his initiative and pushed forward — prepared the ground for the new policy that would draw Russia into collision with Japan under his successor, [[nicholas-ii]]. Kornilov ends his course at this point, on the threshold of the Far Eastern crisis and the great events of 1904–1906.
