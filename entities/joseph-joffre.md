# Joseph Joffre (1852–1931)

Joseph Joffre, French commander-in-chief from the outbreak of the war until December 1916, was the victor of the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]] and the architect of the French war in its first and hardest years. Recouly's synthesis makes him the central figure of the French high command: the generalissimo whose skill and *sang-froid* turned the situation at the Marne, whose robust common sense weighed the strategic problem at Chantilly in 1916, and whose "disgrace" — the removal of 1916 — Recouly bluntly numbers among the events that saved Germany. In this corpus's frame Joffre's story is that of the offensive-minded commander who opened the war, held the frontiers against the German wheel, won the decisive defensive battle of the war, and was then superseded in the long attrition of Verdun and the Somme.

## The man of the offensive

Recouly presents Joffre as "entirely won over to the idea of the offensive," on moral, political and military grounds alike. The offensive answered, in this conception, to the French character and temperament; and an army that had not fought in Europe for half a century could only regain confidence in itself through advance and attack. His staff work had bound France and Russia in formal military agreements obliging both armies to attack the German forces as soon as they were ready — the framework of the [[war-plans-1914]] that governed the opening of the war. Joffre was sixty-two years old in 1914, a year younger than [[marshal-foch]], and it was he, as generalissimo of the French armies (*généralissime des armées françaises*), who received the directives of the concentration and issued the orders of the opening battles.

## The Marne

The great act of Joffre's command was the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]] of September 1914. Recouly stresses that nothing is more complicated or confused than the battle if one tries to follow it in detail, nothing simpler or clearer if one holds to its great lines. It was the skill and sang-froid of the French generalissimo, and the mistakes that excess of confidence made the German generalissimo commit, that turned the situation. After the defeat of the frontier battles and the long retreat, Joffre prepared the counterstroke. Recouly recounts how, on the eve of the decision, he drove in his limousine through crowded roads to the château of Vaux-le-Pénil near Melun for an moving interview with the British commander-in-chief, who finally promised his cooperation; how Franchet d'Espérey, "a magnificent soldier," declared himself ready to march; and how, the last hesitations lifted, Joffre — fully conscious of the enormity of the risk — ordered the great battle engaged for 6 September, warned the government, which had left Paris for Bordeaux, and signed for the troops his immortal order of the day. The Marne was lost for Germany, and at a stroke the German machine was half-dismantled: the short war became a long one. Yet Recouly also records that the exploitation of the victory gave not the results Joffre expected, and that his attempt to free the territory in the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914|course to the sea]] collided with an analogous German manœuvre.

## Chantilly and the strategy of 1916

In 1916, the third year of the war, Recouly presents Joffre examining the situation at his headquarters at Chantilly "with robust and clear common sense." The year that had ended had marked a setback for the Allied cause, yet the failures suffered, especially in the East, were not catastrophic. It fell to Joffre to frame the Allied plan of 1916 — the coordination of the Somme offensive in the West with the pressure of Russia and the new allies in the East. But the double strain of the [[battle-of-verdun-1916]] at Verdun and of the Somme, and the political impatience of the Third Republic, were wearing down his command.

## The disgrace

The chapter of Recouly's book that closes Joffre's command bears a striking title: *La disgrâce de Joffre et la révolution russe sauvent l'Allemagne* — "the disgrace of Joffre and the Russian revolution save Germany." For Recouly the removal of Joffre in December 1916 was one of three hard blows that the German staff, already shaken by the failure before Verdun, the attrition of the Somme, and the entry of Romania, might not otherwise have survived. Joffre's own finest instrument, [[marshal-foch]], was likewise set aside; Recouly notes that even Weygand, who was to become Foch's chief of staff, "was still a choice of Joffre." His dismissal, whatever its political motives, deprived the French command of the generalissimo who had won the Marne at the very moment the war was entering its most trying phase — and Recouly regards the succession as one of the gravest errors of the Allied war.
