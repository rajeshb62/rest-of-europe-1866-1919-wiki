Franz Graf Conrad von Hötzendorf (1852–1925)

Franz Graf Conrad von Hötzendorf, chief of the Austro-Hungarian general staff in 1906–1911 and again from December 1912, was the leading soldier of the monarchy in the decade before the First World War and the man who more than any other shaped its war plans against Serbia, Russia and Italy. His story in this corpus’s frame runs from his appointment in 1906, through the annexation crisis and the Balkan wars when he pressed unceasingly for a preventive war, to his reinstatement in 1912 and his command in the opening campaigns of 1914, and ends with his relief from office in 1917. The great instrument of his memory is his five-volume memoir Aus meiner Dienstzeit 1906–1918, written in the collapse after 1918 to set down — as he said, not as a general history but as purely personal records — the political and military career that the memory of the old army had come to need.

The calling to the general staff

Conrad came to the highest military post late and by reluctant steps. In the spring of 1899 he was colonel and regimental commander in Austrian Silesia; on 9 April 1899 he was appointed brigadier at Trieste and promoted major-general three weeks later, and as the senior general there soon took over the military station command when the senior admiral left the post of sea-district commander. The position imposed on him political as well as purely military duties, and from Trieste he gained an early picture of the situation whose essential conclusion, as he recorded it, was that it was a utopia to count on Italy as an ally — one had rather to be prepared to be deserted by Italy, and in all likelihood attacked, as soon as a favourable opportunity offered. His summons to the office of chief of the general staff in 1906 came against his own pleading to be excused, and he set down in his memoir the stages through which a man in an exposed official position passes — first congratulated, then observed, then criticised, then abused, and finally pelted with stones — through all of which he said he had since passed.

The governing conviction of his whole peacetime activity, he declared, was that the monarchy was threatened by dangers to its very existence that his opponents in opinion either did not estimate at their full magnitude or believed they could escape by temporising, whereas he was convinced they could be met only by preventive action. The dangers were the disintegrating national movements within, the aggressive tendencies of Serbia, Russia and Italy, and the indirect dangers flowing from Austria-Hungary’s alliance with Germany. In these he saw the determining factors for the conduct of internal and external policy, and the necessity of reckoning with war as their means. From the first he sought an intimate connection between external policy and the work of war preparation, and on entering office wrote a courteous letter to the minister of foreign affairs, Baron Ährenthal, asking his support in common working; the reply was a formally correct and obliging letter, but in time many divergences of opinion led to it that the accord he held so indispensable was not reached on all questions.

The annexation crisis and the war against Serbia

In the years of the bosnian-annexation-crisis-1908 Conrad’s view of the South-Slav question hardened into the conviction that would govern him for the rest of his career. He described the process by which the annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina came to be proclaimed, and the friction the crisis produced between the general staff and the foreign ministry. When the extra sheet of the Belgrade Politika announced on 6 October 1908 that the annexation was to be proclaimed, the Serbian foreign ministry demanded immediate mobilisation and war to the death against the monarchy — a mood, he insisted, that was not first called forth by the annexation question but was the permanent keynote, breaking out spontaneously at the least occasion. Already in 1906, on his appointment, he had pointed to the settlement with Serbia as the root of all evil; in 1908–09 he described the moment as come to make clear conditions with the monarchy’s southern neighbour, foreseeing that the chances of this unavoidable settlement must from then on only grow worse.

Against Italy his mistrust was equally fixed. He had from Trieste concluded that Italy must be reckoned a potential enemy, and he laboured in the years of office for measures against a possible Italian war, urging that the monarchy, with its manifold frontiers and its exposed position, could not restrict its war preparations to a few limited war-cases as Spain and Italy could. His proposal of an action against Italy in 1908 foundered on the resistance of the foreign ministry, and the conviction grew on him that the moment when one might act with the back safely covered against Italy was always slipping away. It was on this ground that his conflict with Count Ährenthal deepened year by year.

Dismissal and reinstatement (1911–1912)

The divergences between the military requirements Conrad pressed and the conduct of the foreign ministry came at last to open rupture. He demanded the creation of six army inspectors as future commanders-in-chief and the reinforcement of the army, and clashed with the minister of foreign affairs and the authorities over the means the exposed position of the monarchy demanded. On the afternoon of 29 November 1911 he was told on the archduke-heir’s behalf that on the following day he was to appear in audience before the Kaiser, who would announce his dismissal from the post of chief of the general staff with simultaneous appointment as army inspector. Conrad replied that he had no thought of sulking, that he had done his service as chief as well as he could, and was glad to be rid of a post that had in any case been forced upon him and to come again nearer to the troops. On 30 November 1911 the Kaiser at Schönbrunn told him that he was obliged to remove him from his present post and appoint him army inspector; and the personal relation, the Kaiser was graciously pleased to say, had become a friendly one.

The dismissal lasted barely a year. With the outbreak of the first balkan-wars-1912-13 in October 1912 the whole situation of the monarchy was changed, and the archduke-heir franz-ferdinand charged Conrad with a mission to Bukarest to secure Rumania for the case of war against Russia, under the outward pretext of conveying the Kaiser’s condolences to King Carol on the death of the Countess of Flanders. On 7 December 1912 the archduke received him at the Belvedere, where the maps of the various theatres of war lay on the tables, and turned to him suddenly: “So, now I must tell you something more; you must become chief of the general staff again.” Conrad hesitated and objected that it would not be right to remove his successor who had been appointed barely a year before; but the archduke answered that this removal would take place with all honour for the person concerned, and that he, as presumptive supreme commander of the army, must lay weight on having at the head of the general staff, should it come to war, the man from whom all the preparations for war proceeded. On 12 December 1912 Conrad received the hand-written warrant of the Kaiser reinstating him, and took up again his activity in that office.

1913 and the last moment

Reinstated, Conrad pressed with redoubled force for what he called the last moment. In 1913 he set down his conviction with the greatest plainness: the root of all evil for the monarchy was still, as always, the relation to Serbia and to the Russia standing behind Serbia, and at this root the evil had to be seized; everything else was secondary. The ways that had always floated before him were, in the first place, the attainment of a lasting confederal union of Serbia with the Habsburg monarchy in a peaceful manner; but if Serbia declined the union and continued its hostile aspirations directed against the monarchy — and this case, he held, was present — then a warlike settlement at the suitable moment. In 1913 he held the last moment to have come to carry out this settlement at all with hope of success; if this moment too were neglected, the monarchy seemed to him to drift without a rudder toward the unavoidable doom that threatened it from Serbia and Russia. It remained unused. The policy of the monarchy, he lamented, let itself be taken prisoner by side-issues and distracted from the great goal — to the joy of its enemies.

The account of 1913 and the first half of 1914, as he rendered it month by month, carries the reader through the second Balkan war, the Skutari affair, the peace of Bucharest and of Constantinople, and his own rupture with the archduke-heir after the manoeuvres in Bohemia, down to the last audiences and the departure for Sarajevo on 23 June 1914, four days before the assassination. The war he had so long foretold came with the sarajevo-assassination-1914, and Conrad entered it as the commander whose plans he had spent his whole tenure preparing, an episode that Czernin, a critic of his conduct, recorded from the proximity of the court.

The world war and the relief

In the war Conrad commanded through the opening campaigns of 1914 — the terrible fighting of the east, the engagements that belong to the story of the battle-of-galicia-1914 — and continued at the head of the army through the exhausting years that followed, drawing ever closer to the front of the military-dictatorship that his opponents accused him of seeking. His memoir carried the account through the war years to his relief from office in 1918, when he closed his military career and withdrew into private life, at first, as he said, with no thought of writing memoirs. It was the collapse of the old fatherland and its glorious army, and the many publications and requests that reached him, that moved him at last to set down his experiences. He professed the philosophical conviction that not individual men make their time, but that the time makes its men — that the men who stand by chance at the head of great epochs act by obeying the impulses dictated by the great drift of the age, and are commonly identified with successes and failures that are in truth caused only by the great course of historical development. States and peoples, like trees in a forest, germinate, strike root, grow and spread, and when their lifetime runs out they break down to make room for new formations.
