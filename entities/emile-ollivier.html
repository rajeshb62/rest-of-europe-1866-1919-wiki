Émile Ollivier (1825–1913)

Émile Ollivier was a French lawyer, orator and statesman of the liberal Opposition who became the head of the government of the liberal-empire — the “ministry of 2 January 1870” — and who, by his own account, bore the decision for the war of 1870. He is at once a protagonist of the Second Empire’s last years and the author of the great memoir-history L’Empire libéral that this wiki draws on for the crisis of July and August 1870. No other figure of the corpus is so entangled in the central question of the war’s responsibility, and no other defends himself so explicitly against the charge of having gone to war “au cœur léger” (with a light heart).

The liberal deputy who rallied to the Empire

Ollivier began as a republican and a young opponent of the Empire, but in the 1860s he moved toward the position that the dynasty could be transformed from within — that a “liberal Empire” was possible and preferable to sterile opposition. Delord, the republican historian, watches this evolution with suspicion. At the opening of the session of 1867, in the wake of the Emperor’s letter of 19 January promising interpellation and press and meetings laws, Ollivier astonished his friends by rallying to the majority. In the same session he turned on the ministry and called Rouher, the authoritarian vice-emperor, the “vice-empereur.” Delord presents the rallying of Ollivier as the emblem of a regime that bought reformers by conceding liberty piecemeal. Through the elections of 1869 and the extraordinary session, Ollivier’s star rose as the natural leader of the tiers-parti, the centre that would reconcile the dynasty and the Chambers.

The ministry of 2 January 1870

The formation of the government of 2 January 1870 was the work of long negotiation, conducted between the Emperor and Ollivier through Clément Duvernois. Delord’s chapter “Préface de l’Empire libéral” recounts Ollivier’s conditions and programme, his disguised journey to Compiègne, and the Emperor’s final acceptance; the ministry was completed only when Daru and Buffet agreed to enter, and it was Magne who was sacrificed to bring them in. With this ministry the senatus-consult of 1870 made the ministers responsible to the Corps législatif, and Ollivier, though without the formal title, stood at the head of the government. The liberal Empire, in his own conception, was the honest attempt to give the Second Empire the parliamentary institutions it had always lacked. Sybel, from the German side, treats Ollivier’s ministry as a sign of French weakness and indecision rather than of renewal — a cabinet caught between the Court, the war party and the parliamentary Left.

The July 1870 crisis and the decision for war

In July 1870 Ollivier was at the centre of the hohenzollern-candidature-1870 crisis. When Leopold of Hohenzollern’s acceptance of the Spanish crown was revealed, the ministry resolved to turn aside the candidacy by diplomacy, and Ollivier read from the tribune the ministerial declaration of 6 July. He records his despair at the trap, his efforts to confine the quarrel to the candidacy and to set aside older grievances, and his repeated attempts to keep the peace even after the demand of guarantees was forced upon the government by the Right. In his account it was Bismarck’s falsification of the ems-dispatch-1870 — the soufflet, the “slap in the face” — that made war unavoidable, and it was to this outrage that France was bound to reply. On 15 July 1870 Ollivier read the declaration of war to the Chamber of Deputies; replying to Thiers, who had argued that this was the wrong occasion to avenge Sadowa, he pronounced the words for which he was afterwards condemned: that he accepted the war “le cœur léger,” certain in his conscience that France had not wanted it. His own volume L’Empire libéral is framed from its opening as a defence of this decision and of the ministry’s conduct against the postwar verdict of a “mediocre talker whose presumptuous ineptitude lost two provinces.”

The fall of the ministry, 9 August 1870

The war opened with disaster. At Wissembourg on 4 August and at battle-of-woerth-1870 and Forbach on 6 August the French armies were beaten, and the news broke over Paris on the night of the 6th and the days that followed. Ollivier’s own memoir of these days — Le Suicide — is among the most intimate in the corpus: the false news of a victory on the balconies of the Ministry of Justice, the rising agitation in the capital, the cries of “Ollivier à la lanterne! Vive la République!” in the streets. He worked for national defence, attempted to arrest the revolutionary chiefs, and pressed for the Emperor’s return to Paris, while the Court and the Empress moved against him. On 9 August 1870 the Chamber, turned against the ministry by a coalition of the Right and Left united on the Duvernois motion, forced his resignation. Ollivier was dismissed by the Empress; the new government was formed by General Cousin-Montauban, comte de Palikao. He calls the overthrow of his cabinet a “national catastrophe,” the first act of the Empire’s suicide.

The memoirist and his verdicts

After 1870 Ollivier lived as a private citizen, and devoted his later decades to writing L’Empire libéral (published from 1895), a massive apologia-and-history in which the July crisis, the battles and the fall of his ministry are recounted in exhaustive detail with a block of documentary éclaircissements. His two volumes in this corpus — the fourteenth, La Guerre, on July 1870, and the sixteenth, Le Suicide, on the opening disasters — carry his double purpose: to vindicate the liberal Empire and its decision for war, and to fix responsibility. He is generous to Mac-Mahon, who “accused no one,” and severe on the charge against Marshal Bazaine, whose conduct at Metz he analyses at length; he attacks the German thesis that Bismarck was innocent of the war, arguing that Bismarck alone organised the candidacy and falsified the dispatch, and that serious historians of all nations place the responsibility on him. As a source he is a participant whose honesty and self-interest must be weighed together — the very debate the corpus preserves.
