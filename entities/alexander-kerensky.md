# Alexander Kerensky

Alexander Fyodorovich Kerensky (1881–1970) was the head of the Provisional Government of Russia in 1917, the man who presided over the empire's brief, fateful passage from the fall of the monarchy to the fall of the republic itself. A lawyer and a leader of the Trudovik and then the Socialist-Revolutionary movement, he rose in the [[february-revolution-1917]] as minister of justice, then of war, and finally, in July 1917, as prime minister. His dilemma was the war: the obligation to keep Russia in the [[total-war-economy]] and honor the Allied cause against the rising demand of the army and the people for peace. Denikin, who watched him from Stavka and then from Bykhov, treats him as the man whose "victory" over the Kornilov movement cleared the road for Bolshevism, and whose final overthrow in the [[october-revolution-1917]] ended the only alternative to both the old autocracy and the new dictatorship.

## The minister of the revolution

From the first days of the revolution Kerensky was its most prominent figure — the spellbinding tribune who, as Denikin notes, drew his name, his position, and his power from "revolutionary democracy," the socialists of the soviets and the parties of the left. He held the war portfolio in the first Provisional Government, and it fell to him to carry the army through the collapse of discipline that followed Order No. 1 of the Soviet. Denikin's first volume records the corrosion of the army under the committee system and the impossible position of a minister who had to hold the loyalty of the revolutionary soldiers while the officers, whom he could not fully trust, watched the ruin of their command. In the war, Kerensky's dilemma was the central question of the summer: the Provisional Government's declaration of its determination to defend the country and the Allied cause, which Denikin dismisses as "phrases" masking the reality of an army that would no longer fight. The failed June offensive, which the revolution had been expected to revive, broke the last hope of a military solution.

## The struggle with Kornilov

The crisis of the summer brought Kerensky and the commander-in-chief, [[lavr-kornilov]], into open collision. Denikin analyses the mutual suspicion: Kornilov distrusted the premier and feared arrest; Kerensky, for his part, saw in Kornilov's demand for the restoration of discipline a threat to the revolution and to his own supremacy, and he feared that the "strong hand" would displace the socialists. Savinkov, the war minister's agent, moved between them. When the Kornilov movement came to its head in late August, Kerensky declared the supreme commander a mutineer and crushed the rising — with the aid, Denikin points out, of the very forces, the soviets and the revolutionaries, that would next turn against him. Denikin's verdict is the volume's title theme: Kerensky's "victory" over Kornilov was the "prelude to Bolshevism." By destroying the only organized force that could have restored order, the premier left the state defenseless before the one party, the Bolsheviks, that had a will to power.

## The finale: October

After August, Denikin shows Kerensky ruling in growing isolation. The command was disarmed, the administration was "leaders without an army," and the soviets advanced steadily upon the government. The prime minister's own position became a personal dictatorship without force behind it — a solitude that the Bolshevik seizure of power in October exposed. The capture of Petrograd found the Provisional Government almost unresisted, and Kerensky's attempt to rally troops at Gatchina came to nothing. He fled Russia and spent the rest of his long life in emigration, his name fixed in history as the man who, by refusing to choose between the revolution and the state, lost both. Zhevakhov's hostile sketch of the "hysterical Kerensky" of the February days — the premier "casting lightning glances about him" at the release of the arrested dignitaries — and Denikin's cooler analysis together frame the paradox of a tribune of the revolution who ended by serving neither the revolution nor the state.
