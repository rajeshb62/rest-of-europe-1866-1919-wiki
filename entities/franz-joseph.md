# Franz Joseph

Franz Joseph I (1830–1916), of the House of Habsburg-Lorraine, came to the throne as Emperor of Austria in 1848 at the height of the Revolution and died as Emperor of Austria and King of Hungary in the autumn of 1916, in the third year of the Great War. His reign spans the whole frame of this wiki's period: it was he who, defeated at Königgrätz and driven out of Germany, accepted the transformation of the old unitary empire into the Dual Monarchy through the *Ausgleich* ("compromise") of 1867; he who for nearly half a century thereafter held together the two halves of that monarchy by the sheer force of his person; and he who carried the realm into the war of 1914. If the dynasty was the foundation of the Habsburg state, Franz Joseph was in his long age its most constant embodiment — a ruler whom Redlich would describe as the bearer of a conception of the Austrian state, and whom Sosnosky, with all his criticism of the dualist system, never ceased to treat as the living keystone of the whole.

## The crown as burden

The opening essay of the *Österreichische Rundschau* Sonderheft on the heir to the throne sets out the peculiar condition of the Habsburg ruler that Franz Joseph for sixty-eight years endured. In this many-shaped realm, the essay argues, the crown is a heavier burden than anywhere else, "for here the dynasty itself is the foundation of a structure that is not always logically and organically articulated — elsewhere the state carries the dynasty, here the dynasty carries the state." Redlich, writing in the first chapter of his Carnegie study, explains this in constitutional terms: Austria was "the creation of a dynasty" which for more than six centuries had combined an almost unmeasured idea of its own authority with the satisfaction of the needs of many tangled peoples — the old hereditary lands, the lands of the Bohemian crown, and the whole territory of the ancient Hungarian crown of St. Stephen. The dynastic, the family idea, was that all these, though each kept its historical-political individuality, formed from the standpoint of the head of the House of Habsburg one great condominium, a Reich unity whose political and military force flowed from the will of the hereditary lord. Within that frame, however, Hungary always stood apart: even after the *Pragmatica Sanctio* of 1713 was adopted into Hungarian law in 1723, the old constitutional position of the kingdom remained fundamentally different from that of the other lands.

## The Compromise and the king of Hungary

It was the defeats of 1866 — Königgrätz against Prussia, and the loss of Venice to Italy — that forced on Franz Joseph the settlement which the Hungarians, led by Ferenc Deák, had for years been demanding. Deák asked nothing less than the conversion of the old unitary empire into a novel double state, and the Crown's first "serious scruples" concerned the unity of the army. Beksics, in his defence of the dualism, insists that Deák's work preserved rather than impaired Hungarian statehood, resting as it did on the Pragmatic Sanction and the centuries-old tie of the shared dynasty: Hungary and Austria were to remain two mutually independent states, joined "for mutual defence and for that alone." The Hungarian parliament adopted the Compromise act on 30 March 1867 by 257 against 117 votes, and the Magnates followed; the Austrian Reichsrat, which was never even asked to consent to a state-legal transformation of the whole monarchy, tacitly acquiesced — indeed it sent a deputation to Ofen for the coronation of Franz Joseph as King of Hungary, which the Croatian diet refused to do. Sosnosky, sharply critical of the whole Ausgleich, nevertheless records what the dualism meant for the Emperor: the crown lost no part of its reality in Hungary, for the tie of the shared person of the ruler remained the one firm and immovable thing binding the two states.

## The long reign amid the nationalities

For the four decades that followed, Franz Joseph presided over a monarchy beset by the national principle that Sosnosky calls the governing force of the age. Within Austria (Cisleithania) the German, Czech, Polish, Ruthenian and Slovene populations — roughly ten nations, or by some reckonings twenty nationalities counting Hungary — contended ceaselessly; within Hungary the Magyar national state pressed upon its German, Romanian, Slovak, Ruthenian, Serb and Croat subject peoples. The Emperor's part was to hold the balance and to reconcile the divergent elements through the person of the crown. Sosnosky dwells on the proofs of "self-overcoming and of forgetting" that Franz Joseph gave toward Hungary: in 1906 he received in audience Ferenc (Franz) Kossuth, son of the very Lajos Kossuth who had declared the House of Habsburg forever deprived of the Hungarian throne, and conversed with him in Hungarian; in the same year he ordered the repatriation of the remains of Ferenc Rákóczy from Turkey, the same Rákóczy who had once conspired with the Turks and Louis XIV against the house. These gestures, Sosnosky argues, were among the strongest proofs of a self-command rare in any monarch.

## The army and the Crown

The common army was the keystone of the Austrian position within the dualism — "the last bulwark," as Sosnosky calls it, of the crown's authority against the Magyar drive for independence, and the point on which the Hungarian opposition concentrated its attacks. It was Deák who, in the negotiations of the Ausgleich, denied the historical right of the Hungarians to a separate army, declaring that such a course would be "personal union," and who warned: "Let us say that word, and the curse will fall upon us." Franz Joseph, as *Kriegsherr* ("war-lord"), remained the head of this common force, and the archduke who was his heir took up the same cause, labouring for the reform and strengthening of the army and navy. The struggle over the language of command and the Hungarian military demands — reaching its crisis under Count István Tisza in the parliament of 1903–1904, when obstruction over the recruits' quota pushed the realm to the brink of an *ex-lex* state — was in effect a struggle over how much of the crown's last fortress the Magyars could breach.

## The war and the death of the Emperor

When the war broke out in 1914 Franz Joseph was in his eighty-fourth year. The Reichsrat had been sent home in the spring of 1914 on the renewal of obstructionist tactics among the Slav parties, and the government had resolved to govern through the emergency-notification provision of Article 14 of the Austrian constitution — a system, Redlich notes, that the ministry in Vienna had long in practice exercised anyway. The war government that followed was shaped by the supremacy of the Hungarian government and of Count Tisza over a subordinated Austria, under the tenacious anti-parliamentary policy of the Austrian minister-president Count Stürgkh. Franz Joseph did not live to see the catastrophe. His death and the accession of Emperor Charles form the opening of Redlich's "second period of the war government": the death of the Emperor Francis Joseph, as Redlich writes, at once weakened the influence of the Army High Command on the Austrian government and on the shaping of home policy, and made way for the eventual convocation of parliament and the attempts at reconciliation that came too late. On the Emperor's death the tie of the person common to Hungary and Austria — the very foundation of the personal union that Beksics had declared "entirely outside the range of political possibility" to dissolve — passed to his grand-nephew, and within two years the Dual Monarchy itself was gone.
