# Patrice de Mac-Mahon (1808–1893)

Marshal of France, victor of Magenta, commander of the armies that suppressed the [[paris-commune-1871]], and second President of the Third Republic from 24 May 1873 to January 1879. Mac-Mahon was the soldier-president the monarchical majority set against Thiers, the figurehead of the *ordre moral* and of the attempted royal restoration, and finally the defeated champion of a presidential interpretation of the constitution who resigned rather than submit to a republican Chamber. His presidency spans the monarchical failure, the [[septennat-1873]], the Constitution of 1875, and the [[seize-mai-1877]] crisis.

## The soldier

Mac-Mahon was the man of the army before the man of politics. A soldier of Algeria and the Crimea, he had won the day at Magenta in 1859 — from which his title of duc de Magenta — and commanded with honour in the opening disasters of 1870, wounded at Sedan. His name, Hanotaux records, calmed foreign alarm when Thiers fell: here was a marshal, a man of word and discipline, not an adventurer. His whole career had been obedience to established authority, and it was as a soldier that he entered the Élysée — a soldier's word to continue the liberation of the territory and to restore the *ordre moral*.

## The election of 24 May 1873

Chosen by the conciliabules of the monarchical parties as the candidate who could unite the right, Mac-Mahon was elected president on 24 May 1873 with 390 votes, the left abstaining (Laurier alone voting for Grévy). He accepted after hesitation, and after a call on the fallen Thiers. He was the instrument of the right's design — a president of transition, under whom the monarchy might be restored. But Mac-Mahon was an honest soldier, and Hanotaux emphasizes that he entered the presidency as a neutral head of state rather than a partisan of a dynasty.

## The ordre moral

Under Mac-Mahon the first two ministries of the duc de Broglie governed in the spirit of the *ordre moral*: the formula of moral and religious restoration, the cult of the Sacré-Cœur, the pilgrimages, the measures against republican propaganda. The Marshal lent his name to this conservative order while the monarchical campaign ran its course. When the comte de Chambord's letter of 27 October 1873 refused the tricolour and ruined the restoration, Mac-Mahon's seven-year presidency was fixed by the [[septennat-1873]] law of 20 November 1873. He declined to receive Chambord at Versailles — the Marshal, having sworn to the flag, would not bargain with the pretender over it.

## President under the Constitution of 1875

The [[french-constitution-1875]] made Mac-Mahon president under a republican constitution he had not sought and did not entirely accept. He watched the elections of 1876 deliver a republican Chamber, Dufaure and then Jules Simon govern under him, and the parliamentary regime establish itself. The Marshal, faithful to a conception of the presidency as an independent authority rather than a ceremonial office, came to resent a Chamber that pushed him aside. Hanotaux treats his eventual act of 16 May 1877 as the constitutional corollary of the septennat compromise — a foreseen, deliberated assertion of the president's power rather than a mere boutade.

## Seize Mai and the defeat of the presidential interpretation

On 16 May 1877 Mac-Mahon dismissed Jules Simon and called the duc de Broglie, dissolving the Chamber in the hope of winning a conservative majority — the [[seize-mai-1877]] crisis. The elections of 14 October 1877 returned a republican Chamber; the Marshal's "submit or resign" challenge was answered by the country. After the second Dufaure ministry and the republican victories in the senatorial elections of January 1879, Mac-Mahon's position was untenable: the Republic he had been set to contain had mastered him. He resigned in January 1879, the last president who seriously believed he could stand apart from the parliamentary majority. His departure left the field to [[jules-grevy]] and the consolidators of the parliamentary regime, sealing the victory of the interpretation of the constitution that [[leon-gambetta]] and the republicans had demanded.
