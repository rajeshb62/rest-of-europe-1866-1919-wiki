# Francesco Crispi (1818–1901)

Francesco Crispi, born in Sicily in 1818, was the most formidable of the Italian statesmen of the second half of the century — a republican revolutionary of the Risorgimento who became, late in life, the strong-armed prime minister of the monarchy, the author of its colonial policy, and the man who fell with the disaster of Adua in 1896. His story is the arc of the revolutionary made minister: the organizer of the Sicilian rising of 1860, the prophet of the unitary monarchy in the South, the republican republican who declared that "the monarchy unites us and the republic would divide us," and finally the autocratic premier of the 1880s and 1890s whose African adventure ended in catastrophe. Oriani's *La lotta politica in Italia* follows him from the Sicilian preparations of 1859–60 through the making of the kingdom, and [[giustino-fortunato]]'s parliamentary speeches record the ruin of his policy after [[battle-of-adua-1896]].

## The Sicilian revolutionary

Crispi began as a conspirator of the Mazzinian left. Oriani's account shows him, in the years before 1860, repeatedly entering Sicily to introduce arms and to organize the risings, in contact with the agents of the *partito d'azione* and encouraged by Mazzini himself. His part in the preparation of the [[expedition-of-the-thousand-1860]] was that of the political organizer who prepared the ground for Garibaldi's landing; Oriani describes him as the most obstinate persuader of the enterprise, and notes that the ascendancy he had gained in the island made him the natural intermediary between the Garibaldian movement and the cautious Piedmontese government. It was to the Sicilian revolutionary tradition that Crispi belonged, and from it he carried into his later ministry the instincts of the strong state and the distrust of party.

## The turn to the monarchy

The decisive turn of Crispi's early career was his acceptance of the monarchy as the necessary form of Italian unity. In the annexed South, Oriani records, the aristocracy had been drawn into the national movement by the monarchical element, and a republic would have loosed an indomitable reaction in the Mezzogiorno. The phrase Oriani attributes to Crispi in 1864 — "la monarchia ci unisce e la republica ci dividerebbe," "the monarchy unites us and the republic would divide us" — crystallized the policy by which the republican left reconciled itself to the Savoyard crown, and it marks the point at which the old conspirator became a pillar of the state. The remainder of the 1860s and 1870s saw him in and out of office, one of the leading men of the parliamentary Left, before the death of [[agostino-depretis]] in 1887 opened the premiership to him.

## Prime minister and the strong state

Crispi was prime minister from 1887 to 1891 and again from 1893 to 1896, and he governed as a strong man in a parliamentary age — the opposite pole of the [[trasformismo]] of his predecessor, and a figure feared and hated in proportion to his energy. He carried the colonial policy beyond the Eritrean foothold toward the interior of Ethiopia, committing the kingdom to the African expansion that Oriani's ninth book treats under the heading of "la conquista africana." Domestically his years were marked by the repression of the socialists and of the Sicilian *Fasci* in 1893–94, which made him the symbol of authoritarian order against the democratic and socialist Left. His ambition was to make Italy a great power — a navy, an empire, a place in the first rank of Europe — and his methods were those of a man who believed the state could be governed only by a firm hand.

## Adua and the fall

Crispi's empire foundered in the highlands of Ethiopia. The drive to subdue the Negus, Menelik, led the Italian army deep into Tigré, and on 1 March 1896 the column was annihilated at [[battle-of-adua-1896]] — the disaster that Fortunato calls the "immane disastro di Adua" which suddenly made the country, and the parliament born of the passions of that year, a prisoner of the consequences. The defeat brought down Crispi's government and his political career; the colonial programme collapsed into the defence of the Eritrean foothold. In the elections that followed, Fortunato, speaking to the electors of Melfi in March 1897, urged a policy of "absolute recollection" — to restrict all military ambition to the Eritrean colony, to withdraw from the African adventure, and to repair at home the damage done abroad. The catastrophe, in Fortunato's reading, was not the work of a single hand but the outcome of the "sciagurata" colonial policy that Crispi embodied, and it left the kingdom humiliated and its institutions shaken. Crispi died in 1901, out of office and out of power, remembered as the strongest and most dangerous of the makers of the new Italy.
