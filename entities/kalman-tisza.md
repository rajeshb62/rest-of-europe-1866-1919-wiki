# Kálmán Tisza

Kálmán Tisza (1830–1902) was the Hungarian minister-president from 1875 to 1890, the longest tenure in the history of the kingdom, and the principal consolidator of the Dualist settlement of 1867. In the corpus's frame he appears through the eyes of the German publicist Theodor von Sosnosky as the builder of the "magyarische Nationalstaat" — the statesman who, coming out of the old opposition to the Compromise, made himself its most effective defender and who welded the Hungarian Liberals into the governing party that carried Magyar supremacy over the subject peoples for a generation. He was the father of Count Stephen Tisza, who would carry the same defence of dualism and of Magyar supremacy into the years of the Great War.

## From opposition to the Compromise

Tisza's political journey mirrored that of the Magyar gentry as a whole. He had sat as a member of the oppositional minority in the fifteen-man committee that drafted the Compromise act of 1867 — the committee whose deliberations turned on the interpretation of the Pragmatic Sanction and of the word "common" in the common institutions. In that committee, Sosnosky records, Tisza was among those who would allow certain "Agenden des Heerwesens" — the business of the army — to be regulated "im gemeinsamen Einverständnis" with Austria, but not "gemeinsam," not as a truly common affair. The independence of Hungary, Sosnosky remarks, was the political ideal of all Magyars without distinction of party, and the future minister-president, as a former member of that oppositional minority, could not have stood far from that view. Yet, once in power, he felt himself obliged to uphold the settlement that bound Hungary to the Crown of Austria.

## The long premiership

Tisza became minister-president in 1875, at the head of the Liberal party he had formed by fusing his own following with the Deákist supporters of the Compromise. He remained in office for fifteen years, until 1890 — a stability that gave the Dualist system in Hungary its settled parliamentary form. Sosnosky presents him as the object of the fiercest hatred of the oppositional parties, which branded him a traitor to the national cause, accusing him of connivance with Austria. But the truth, in Sosnosky's judgement, was the reverse of the accusation: the very independence party that so passionately hated and pursued him, and that set him up as a betrayer, was in reality served by his policy better than by its own. The difficulties he made for Austria, the obstacles he placed in the way of Viennese centralising designs, worked for the cause those parties claimed to serve far more effectually than their own agitation did.

## The builder of the Magyar national state

The premiership of Tisza was the period in which the "magyarische Nationalstaat" — the Magyar national state — took its definitive shape. Against the background of the passionate, domineering temper of the Magyar political nation that Sosnosky describes in his introduction to the Hungarian half of his work, Tisza's government carried through the policies of Magyarisation that were to define the treatment of the subject nationalities down to 1918: the school, the courts, and the electoral system were all turned to the creation of a Magyar political nation, and even those who were half-Magyarised — the "Talmi-Magyaren," the sham Magyars — were the product and the tool of this project. It was under the aegis of Tisza's long ministry, and of the Liberal party he commanded, that the German, Romanian, Slovak, Ruthenian and Serb populations of the kingdom found themselves pressed within a state conceived as the state of the Magyars alone.

## The defence of dualism

Tisza's achievement was to reconcile the two poles of Magyar politics — the ideal of independence and the necessity of the tie to Austria — in a working system. He defended the dualist settlement against the pan-Magyar extremists who dreamed of a Hungary sharing nothing with Austria but the ruler, even as he resisted the Austrian claims that would have reduced the kingdom to a province. His long tenure gave the Compromise of 1867 the force of an established order, so that when his son Stephen came to defend the same settlement a generation later, against the renewed assault of the independence party and the cry of *Los von Österreich*, it was Kálmán Tisza's consolidation that formed the ground on which the son stood. The old Tisza, Sosnosky suggests, had by his patient defence of Hungarian interests done more for the cause the extremists professed than their own intransigence ever could.

## Legacy

Kálmán Tisza died in 1902, twelve years after laying down the premiership. He left behind him the Liberal party he had created, the long peace of the Dualist system, and a tradition of Magyar state-building that his son inherited and carried into the fatal confrontation with the nationalities in the years before the First World War. To Sosnosky, writing in 1913, he was the exemplar of the settled Magyar politics of the age of consolidation — the man who had made the Dual Monarchy work for Hungary, and in doing so had shaped the national state whose unresolved nationality question stood at the centre of the monarchy's troubles on the eve of 1914.
