# Camillo Cavour (1810–1861)

Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, was the Piedmontese statesman who engineered the unification of Italy, and he stands at the very edge of this corpus's frame: born in 1810, he died on 6 June 1861, weeks after the kingdom he had created had come into being, and therefore just before the 1866 starting-point of this wiki's survey. He is included here as the indispensable predecessor — the architect whose death left the new Kingdom of Italy to be governed by his successors, and whose methods, in Oriani's judgment, were never again equalled. Tivaroni, in the gallery of the men of the Risorgimento with which he closes his history, devotes to Cavour the fullest of his portraits — the youth, the journalist, the deputy and minister, the judgements upon him, his mind, and the judgement of posterity — and Oriani treats him as the mind of the "royal conquest" that complemented Garibaldi's popular one.

## From journalist to minister

Cavour came late to politics, as a journalist and economic reformer of Piedmont before he entered the ministry. Tivaroni's portrait traces his youth in the conservative Piedmontese world, his turn to English-style liberalism, and his establishment as the leading parliamentary figure of the constitutional monarchy. After the defeat of 1849 and the accession of [[victor-emmanuel-ii]], Cavour rose through the ministries to become prime minister, and from 1852 governed Piedmont in the interest of a national policy that the other Italian states could not pursue. His instruments were the *Statuto*, a free parliament, a modernized administration, and a diplomacy that lifted the little subalpine kingdom into the councils of the great powers — most strikingly by joining the Crimean war in 1855 to secure a seat at the peace table.

## The war of 1859 and the annexations

Cavour's decisive stroke was the alliance with France. At the secret meeting of Plombières he and Napoleon III agreed on a war against Austria, and in 1859 the Franco-Sardinian armies won the battles of Magenta and Solferino and drove the Austrians out of Lombardy. The peace of Villafranca, which Napoleon III concluded behind Cavour's back and which left the Veneto still Austrian, provoked his furious resignation; but the revolutions of central Italy in 1859–60, which overthrew the old regimes in Florence, Modena, Parma and Bologna, and the cession of Nice and Savoy to France as the price of the annexation of the centre, restored him to office to complete the work. The year 1860 saw the [[expedition-of-the-thousand-1860]] conquer the South under Garibaldi, and Cavour — fearing a revolution he could not control — sent the royal army through the Marches to meet it, so that the plebiscites of October 1860 delivered the whole peninsula to Victor Emmanuel's crown.

## The last months and the death of 6 June 1861

In the spring of 1861 the first Italian Parliament assembled, and the Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed with Victor Emmanuel II as its king and Rome as its nominal capital. Cavour's last months were occupied with the consolidation of the new state — the finances, the unsettled South where brigandage was already rising, and the vain negotiations with the Holy See over Rome. He died on 6 June 1861, before any of these tasks was resolved. Tivaroni's account of his death — "La morte del conte di Cavour" — frames it as the moment at which the state lost the mind that had made it; the ministries that followed, those of Ricasoli, Rattazzi, Farini-Minghetti and Lamarmora, would govern the peninsula until 1866 without his commanding intelligence. Oriani's verdict is that none of his successors ever showed his greatness: the mediocre show of the first ministries is for Oriani the proof of how much the new kingdom owed to a single man, and how uncertainly it could steer itself without him. Cavour thus belongs to this corpus less as an actor within its years than as the foundation with which every subsequent Italian politician — and above all the parliamentary men of the post-unification decades — had to reckon.
