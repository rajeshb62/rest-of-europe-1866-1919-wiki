# Sergei Witte

Count Sergei Yulievich Witte (1849–1915) was the architect of modern Russian state finance and the first constitutional premier of the empire. Rising from the railway service of the south-west to the finance ministry under [[alexander-iii]], he carried through the monetary reform and the gold standard, the spirits monopoly, the railway programme and the Trans-Siberian line, and the protectionist fiscal system that financed the [[industrialization-witte]] of the 1890s; under [[nicholas-ii]] he negotiated the peace of Portsmouth that ended the [[russo-japanese-war-1904-05]] and presided over the granting of the [[october-manifesto-1905]], becoming the first chairman of the Council of Ministers. He is also the author of the three-volume memoirs that form one of the principal sources of this wiki — an extraordinary, opinionated testimony to the Russia of Alexander III and Nicholas II.

## The railway man

Witte was born in Tiflis in 1849, the son of a director of state domains in the Caucasus and a mother of the Fadeev family; his uncle was the publicist and soldier Rostislav Fadeev. After a university training in pure mathematics he was turned from a professorship by his family's sense of what was fitting for a nobleman, and entered railway service in Odessa. He made his career on the railway "kings" of the age, as the South-Western Railway company was formed from the unprofitable Odessa, Kiev-Brest, and Brest-Grajewo lines, and rose to be its manager. His memoirs abound in the portraits of this world — the concessionaires, the engineers, the governors-general. It was his practical command of the railways that brought him to the emperor's attention: on the imperial journeys over the South-Western line he urged that the train be run at safe speeds, and when, against his warnings, the imperial train was driven at reckless pace, the catastrophe at Borki followed — a disaster that discredited the ministry of ways of communication and opened Witte's own road to power.

## Minister of finance

After the death of Alexander III Witte was already minister of finance, having succeeded Vyshnegradsky; he had served as director of the department of railway affairs and minister of ways of communication, and had even been sent by the emperor to the cholera districts in 1892. The financial system he built is the subject of the Granat volume's opening essay, which records both its brilliance and its cost. He introduced the spirits monopoly — the state sale of drink reorganized from 1896 — which became one of the largest and steadiest revenues of the treasury. He carried through the monetary reform that had been prepared under Bunge and Vyshnegradsky: by the ukases of 3 January, 29 August, and 14 November 1897 the credit ruble was stabilized on a gold basis, a new gold ruble was established by a kind of devaluation fixed at the most stable existing rate of the paper ruble, and the state bank was charged with the unfailing conversion of notes into gold. Foreign loans, above all the great French loans, supplied the gold reserve. Bogolepov's essay in the Granat volume is critical: the "brilliant period" of Russian state economy was purchased at the price of extreme strain on the taxable capacity of the people, a loss-making state economy, and the ruin of the propertyless. Yet the system worked — Russia's currency became convertible, foreign capital poured in, and the railway network, including the [[industrialization-witte]]'s greatest monument the Trans-Siberian Railway, was pushed forward at a pace unknown before.

## The Far East and the Japanese war

Witte's finance and his diplomacy ran together in the Far East. He negotiated with Li Hung-chang the treaty with China of 1896, with the Russo-Chinese Bank and the Chinese Eastern Railway across Manchuria, and watched the court's adventure take the matter out of his hands. The "Bezobrazov clique" — the adventurers around the throne — pressed the reckless policy in Korea, and in 1903 Witte was driven from the finance ministry to the empty chairmanship of the Committee of Ministers. When the [[russo-japanese-war-1904-05]] ended in disaster, the emperor had to turn to him again. Witte was sent to America, where with President Roosevelt's mediation he negotiated the peace of Portsmouth in 1905 — a peace that secured Russia from the worst terms of the military defeat and made Witte a popular hero for his deft bargaining. From America he returned through Paris and Berlin, and entered Petersburg amid the revolution.

## The October Manifesto and the premiership

In the autumn of 1905, with the revolution at its height, Witte pressed on the emperor the necessity of concession. On 17 October the manifesto was issued granting civil liberties and a legislative Duma, and Witte became the first chairman of the Council of Ministers. His account of the half-year premiership is frank: the repression of disorders and the punitive expeditions, the amnesty, the electoral law, the great foreign loan of 1906 that liquidated the war's financial legacy, the framing of the Fundamental Laws. He records the pressure of Trepov and the great duke on the cabinet and the intrigues of the right, and he records, with particular bitterness, how the court later spread the story that he had wrung the manifesto from the unwilling sovereign. Dismissed in April 1906, he spent the rest of his life watching the regime from outside — an ousted and embittered observer of the Stolypin years, whose memoirs, written in retirement and completed around 1911, are a running indictment of the men who undid his work. He died in 1915, in the second year of the war he had always feared.
