# Jules Ferry (1832–1893)

The minister of the school laws — the laws that made primary education free, compulsory, and secular — and the republican statesman who carried the laicization of the French school into law. Jules Ferry, the austere and combative Lorrain of the parliamentary Republic, was twice president of the council in the 1880s, the author of the *école laïque* and of the Tunisian protectorate, and the man whose fall over Tonkin opened a new era in colonial politics. Hanotaux presents him as the representative, in power, of the doctrine of republican anticlericalism.

## The republican of the centre

Ferry belonged to the generation of republicans who came to office with the triumph of the parliamentary regime after 1879. Hanotaux records that he brought to the realization of the republican programme "vigour, rudeness, realistic logic — whence his strength and the hatreds he raised." He was a republican of the moderate centre, prepared to build the secular Republic by law rather than by revolution, and the natural colleague of Gambetta and the leaders of the Chamber. He entered the Waddington cabinet as minister of public instruction, and it was there that he prepared the great school legislation.

## The school laws and article 7

Ferry's school policy rested on a plan conceived in advance: in less than three months he deposited the series of projects that struck, in Hanotaux's phrase, at the three teachings at once — "the famous school laws, 'the wicked laws'" (*les lois scélérates*). The primary-school law introduced *gratuité, obligation, laïcité* — free, compulsory, secular schooling — taking up the older Barodet proposal and making it the law of the Republic. Against the teaching congregations Ferry pressed the notorious article 7, which would have forbidden the unauthorized religious orders from teaching; when the Senate rejected it, the [[french-constitution-1875]]-era ministry fell back on the decrees of 29–30 March 1880, which led to the expulsion of the Jesuits. The school, Ferry insisted, was the instrument by which the Republic would form its citizens and detach the rising generation from clerical influence — the institutional expression of the anticlericalism that Gambetta had proclaimed the enemy.

## The Tunisian campaign

Ferry's foreign policy carried the Republic into empire. At the Congress of Berlin and its aftermath the powers had tacitly offered Tunisia to France as a compensation for the acquisitions of others; Ferry, with the support of the Chamber, resolved to take it. The Kroumir incursions furnished the pretext, the expedition was launched, and the [[third-republic-france]] established its protectorate over Tunisia by the Treaty of Bardo. The venture satisfied French expansion and the need for colonial prestige after the loss of Alsace-Lorraine, though it alarmed Italy and embroiled France in the Mediterranean rivalries that would shape the coming decades.

## Fall over Tonkin

Ferry's second great colonial undertaking, the advance into Tonkin and Annam, proved his undoing. The distant war, the reverses, and the mounting cost turned the Chamber against him; defeated on the Tonkin question, he fell from power. Hanotaux's narrative, which closes on the death of Gambetta in 1882, treats Ferry within the larger arc of the Republic's founding ministries — the age in which the parliamentary Republic, having conquered the domestic institutions, turned to the world. Ferry, like Gambetta, embodied the confident republicanism of the "heroic age" that was passing.
