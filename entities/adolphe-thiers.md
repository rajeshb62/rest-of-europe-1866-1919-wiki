# Adolphe Thiers (1797–1877)

The little man with the spectacles who governed France through its deepest humiliation since the First Empire — and who alone had the authority to make peace, crush the Commune, pay the indemnity, and free the territory before the monarchical majority toppled him. Thiers, called by the elections of February 1871 "administrator of the national misfortune," was head of the executive power from February 1871, then President of the Republic under the Rivet law of 31 August 1871, until his overthrow on 24 May 1873. His triple task — peace, repair, and a durable political form — defined the opening years of the Third Republic.

## The statesman of 1830

Thiers was not a man of the new France but of the old: a Marseillais of modest birth, author of the *Histoire de la Révolution française*, journalist of the *National* who had helped make the July revolution of 1830. Hanotaux's portrait sets him directly against Napoleon III — prudence, experience, foresight against adventure; short stature, spectacles, imperturbable aplomb. Where the Empire had gambled on glory, Thiers offered the continuity of a cautious administrator. It was this reputation that made him, in the disasters of 1870–71, the natural rallying point of a country that wanted peace and order rather than either *guerre à outrance* or a king it could not agree upon.

## Chief of the executive power

Elected in twenty-six departments with nearly two million votes, Thiers met the National Assembly at Bordeaux and was named chief of the executive power. His cabinet of 19 February 1871 organized the new authority and won recognition from the foreign powers. Against the republicans of the Left who demanded war to the last, Thiers carried the peace: he negotiated the preliminaries at Versailles, sat with Bismarck (21 February), and defended the claims of the country even as the German chancellor held the armed hand. The question of Metz and Belfort divided the two; Thiers saved Belfort for France and struck a milliard from the original demand. He bound himself to the country's desire for peace, and made that peace the foundation of his authority.

## Peace and the Commune

The Assembly's shift to Versailles and its measures against the armed population of Paris detonated the insurrection of 18 March 1871. For two months Thiers and the Versailles government fought the [[paris-commune-1871]] — the siege of the capital by the army under Marshal Mac-Mahon, the provincial risings put down, and finally the Bloody Week. Thiers, like the Assembly, regarded the suppression as the indispensable condition of national recovery. At the same time he completed the peace: the definitive [[treaty-of-frankfurt-1871]] of 10 May 1871 ceded Alsace and part of Lorraine and fixed the five-milliard indemnity. When Bismarck offered German financiers to carry the debt, Thiers refused and staked the payment on France itself — the loan of two milliards of June 1871, subscribed 381,906 times, proved his confidence. The conventions of 12 October 1871 began the evacuation of the German troops.

## The apogee and the 'libérateur du territoire'

The session of April–August 1872 was the apogee of Thiers's government: peace signed, the Commune defeated, reconstruction under way. Under the Rivet law he had become President of the Republic, yet remained responsible before a hostile Assembly. His message of 13 November 1872 finally pronounced the word "Republic," and the right recoiled. Negotiating for the anticipated evacuation of the remaining German soldiers — Gontaut-Biron, Saint-Vallier, Arnim, Bismarck — Thiers secured the convention of liberation of 15 March 1873, and the Assembly declared that he had deserved well of the country. He became the *libérateur du territoire*, the man who paid the milliards and sent the occupying armies home.

## The fall of 24 May

The very success undid him. Having secured liberation, Thiers had spent his usefulness in the eyes of the monarchical majority, which now wanted a president it could control and a king it still hoped to crown. The pact of the three monarchical parties, the resignation of Grévy, the election of Barodet at Paris, the resignations of Jules Simon and de Goulard — all preceded the decisive sittings. On 24 May 1873 Thiers made his great speech: peace signed, Commune defeated, indemnity paid, evacuation decided. "I have taken my party on the Republic," he told the Chamber. Put in the minority, he resigned. Mac-Mahon was elected president in his place. Hanotaux's judgment is that the right toppled Thiers before he could found the Republic, and that both conservatives and republicans misread the act — the Republic was not killed, only postponed. Thiers, the *libérateur du territoire*, lived to see the Republic he had prepared emerge from the ruins of the monarchical attempt, dying in 1877 just as the parliamentary Republic was being born.
