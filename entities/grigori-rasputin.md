# Grigori Rasputin

Grigori Efimovich Rasputin (1869–1916), the *starets* (\"elder\") from the Siberian village of Pokrovskoe, was the peasant holy man who gained an extraordinary hold over the court of [[nicholas-ii]] and the Empress [[alexandra-feodorovna]] in the last years before the revolution. To the public of the capital he was the scandal of the age — the debauched monk whose favor was believed to decide appointments, whose name was whispered behind every ministerial change, and whose murder in December 1916 became one of the great episodes of the monarchy's collapse. His treatment in this wiki's sources comes almost entirely from the official and courtly side: [[nikolai-zhevakhov]], the deputy ober-procurator, writes as a defender of the imperial family and of eldership itself, insisting that the legend of Rasputin was manufactured to destroy the dynasty; Witte, writing earlier and from outside the court, records the beginnings of the Rasputin business in the last years of his life.

## The holy man and the court

Zhevakhov, who knew the religious atmosphere of Petersburg at first hand, places Rasputin in the long line of Russian "God-seekers" and holy fools — predecessors such as Archimandrite Mikhail and the "tongue-tied Mitya" had prepared the capital's appetite for just such a figure. Rasputin appeared as the peasant who could pray for the sick, above all for the hemophiliac heir Alexei, whose illness dominated the private life of the imperial family. The Empress, in Zhevakhov's account, believed in him with a religious conviction — when a lady of the court protested against the scandal Rasputin caused, the Empress answered that "the official Church has always crucified its prophets." The Sovereign, too, trusted him: "if not for the prayers of Grigory Efimovich," he is reported to have said, "I should long ago have been killed." Around this private faith grew the public legend, and with it the machinery of the dynasty's ruin.

## The scandal and its uses

For the hostile public, every evil of the reign was laid at Rasputin's door. Zhevakhov's central contention is that the danger was not the man but the weapon his name became: that "talk about Rasputin was more harmful than Rasputin himself," and that a small circle working to destroy the state used the name of the starets as one of its instruments. He records the psychosis of the capital, in which every appointment — every high position — was ascribed to Rasputin's influence, and in which men nearest the throne were most eager to disown any connection with him, shouting his condemnation the louder to prove their loyalty. Witte, from the cooler vantage of his retirement, records the same thing with contempt: the rise of Rasputin, and the crowd of Grigori's, of Germogen, of Iliodor, of the "dark forces" who trafficked in the favor of the court. Whether the starets truly directed the government or was merely the screen behind which others governed, the effect was the same — the monarchy, identified in the public mind with Rasputin, lost the last remnant of its prestige.

## The murder

The catastrophe came in December 1916. In Zhevakhov's account, which he sets within his scheme of the "periods" of the collapse, the murder of Rasputin marked the passage of the revolution from agitation to act. He records the killing as an act of treachery against a man he insists was not what his enemies said — "Rasputin was killed not when he was a power, but when he was already..." — and he is careful to note the strange alliance of those who struck: the aristocrat Yusupov and the monarchist Purishkevich, men who thought to save the dynasty by removing its disgrace. The murder did not save the throne; it removed the one figure whose favor had seemed to bind the Empress, and left the court, the Church, and the government more isolated than before. Two months later the February revolution swept away the monarchy that had been so fatally entangled with the name of the starets.
