# Pyotr Stolypin

Pyotr Arkadievich Stolypin (1862–1911) was prime minister of Russia from 1906 to his assassination in 1911 — the strong man who carried the empire through the aftermath of the [[revolution-1905]] and imposed upon it a hybrid regime of [[duma-constitutionalism]] and repression. In his person the two faces of the post-October state met: the reformer who broke up the peasant commune in the celebrated land reform, and the hangman of the punitive courts. He was the architect of the 3 June 1907 coup that changed the electoral law to secure a submissive Duma, the promoter of the Western zemstvo that brought him down, and — in the furious judgment of [[sergei-witte]], whose memoirs supply this wiki's fullest account of him — a "most honest phrasemonger" whose liberal speeches clothed an unprecedented arbitrariness.

## The appearance of Stolypin

Stolypin came to the premiership through the fire of the revolution. He had been governor of Saratov province, then minister of internal affairs, and his resolution in the disorders brought him forward as the successor to Witte, who had resigned in April 1906. The First Duma — which Witte called the Duma "of popular revenge," meeting in the summer of 1906 — was dissolved, and Stolypin governed through the summer of the punitive expeditions, the courts-martial, and the hangings. Witte, writing from retirement, draws the portrait that has stuck: Stolypin in the Duma spoke the language of liberalism, and his collected speeches read, as Witte says, like those of a liberal statesman — while no one, Witte insists, "killed so many and in the most atrocious manner, no one acted so arbitrarily, no one so spat upon the law" as Stolypin did. The gulf between the words and the deeds, in Witte's telling, was the essence of the man.

## The land reform

Against the agrarian discontent that fed the revolution, Stolypin advanced his great constructive measure: the break-up of the peasant commune and the establishment of the peasant as an individual proprietor. The measure, prepared in the years of reaction against the insufficiency of allotments that the zemstvo investigations had exposed, was designed to create a class of sturdy independent farmers as a conservative support of the state. Witte's account of the peasant question and of the resignation of the finance minister Kutler in 1906 records the resistance the reform encountered in the councils. The land reform was carried through the Duma and into the villages in the years before the war, and its success or failure became the great question of Russian agrarian policy. In Witte's telling the whole was vitiated by the same arbitrariness that marked the man: the means by which it was imposed on the country were those of a government that would not be bound by its own law.

## The 3 June coup

The climax of the repression was the dissolution of the Second Duma and the change of the electoral law by the manifesto of 3 June 1907 — the *gosudarstvenny perevorot* (\"state coup\"), as Witte unhesitatingly calls it. By it the emperor, at Stolypin's urging, broke the constitution that had been granted on 17 October 1905 and issued a new electoral law in violation of the Fundamental Laws. Witte's verdict is precise: the law was artificial, founded not on firm principles but on the shakiest calculations, and its effect was to produce a Duma not "chosen" but "picked" (*podobrannaya*) by the government, a Duma made for the privileged and the strong. The Third Duma that followed, though more tractable, was never in Witte's eyes a true representative chamber. The 3 June system — a loyal Duma under a strong minister — was Stolypin's working constitution, and it held until his death.

## The Western zemstvo and the assassination

The same energy that made enemies on the left made them on the right. Stolypin's plan to introduce the zemstvo into the Western provinces, where the Polish nobility dominated, arrayed the conservative interests of the State Council against him. Witte, who had his own score to settle, records the frictions at the top that accumulated through 1909–1911. In September 1911, at Kiev, during the festivities of the unveiling of the monument to Alexander II, Stolypin was shot in the theatre in the presence of the emperor by an agent of the security police, and died a few days later of his wound. Witte's reflection on the murder is characteristic: the killing was "revolting and cannot be justified," yet "it can be understood," for the thousands hanged under Stolypin's premiership had made of state killing a commonplace — the prime minister who held the police and the whole apparatus of repression in his own hands had, in Witte's view, the first responsibility for his own fate. With Stolypin's death the strong-man regime of the Third Duma was over, and the succession fell to the placid Kokovtsov.
