# Ferdinand Foch (1851–1929)

Ferdinand Foch, Marshal of France, was the French soldier who embodied the Allied recovery of the Great War — the victor of the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]] in its immediate aftermath, the great captain of the Second Battle of the Marne, and from the spring of 1918 the generalissimo of the Allied forces whose [[hundred-days-offensive-1918]] broke the German army and forced the [[armistice-11-november-1918]]. In Recouly's synthesis, written twenty years after the war, Foch is "a soldier of genius" to whom only the threat of catastrophe compelled the Allies to confide a single command; his story in this corpus's frame is the story of how the French and Allied supreme command was created, exercised, and crowned with victory. His name recurs from the Marne to the armistice train at Rethondes, and — as Cadorna's appendix testifies — as the adviser sent to Italy in the darkest hour of the Italian front.

## The teacher of the École de Guerre

Foch came to the war as a thinker and teacher of war as much as a field commander. Recouly recalls his conferences at the *École de Guerre*, where he demonstrated with luminous force the errors and dangers of the German command method — the method, as Moltke the elder had conceived it in 1870, of giving the executants general directives drawn from an overall plan and then leaving them to fend for themselves. Such a method, Foch argued, could only end in a disordered, incoherent battle not directed by its chief. He compressed the judgment into a lapidary phrase that Recouly quotes: *« La stratégie allemande fait faillite: elle n'est sauvée que par la tactique »* — "German strategy fails; it is only saved by tactics." He was sixty-three years old in 1914, a year older than [[joseph-joffre]], and he brought to the war the will and the philosophy of command that the defensive crisis of 1918 would finally make the possession of the entire Alliance.

## The Marne and the war of movement

Foch was one of the principal executants of the victory of the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]] in September 1914. Recouly records his celebrated maxim, *« Une bataille perdue est une bataille qu'on croit perdue »* — "a lost battle is a battle one believes lost" — and judges that the psychological factor almost always has a preponderant weight in war as in life. The maxim was put to the test in those days of extreme strain on the French left, where Foch's command stood in the very line of the German wheel toward Paris. He survived the test; and the Marne, which turned the short war into a long one, began his rise as the master of the battle of attrition that the war of movement had become.

## The creation of the supreme command

The central theme of Foch's later career, as Recouly frames it, is the command unique — the single, unified direction of the Allied war. From the first winter of the war Recouly marks the capital question as the *commandement unique*, the supreme direction of the war: a generalissimo, master of combining all operations, disposing of reserves whether French, English, Belgian, or Italian. But this ideal solution, Recouly notes, was long out of the question; to realise the single command, only three years later, required the direct menace of a defeat and even of a disaster — the German march on Paris in the spring of 1918 separating the French armies. When Foch discussed the subject he remarked, with his habitual brusqueness, that a study on unified command should begin: *« Article premier: Il n'y a pas de commandement unique »* — "Article one: there is no unified command." The irony was that he himself would become its living embodiment. Only the threat of catastrophe, Recouly writes, compelled the Allies to accept the single command, confided to "a soldier of genius, Foch."

## Generalissimo of the Allied armies

Appointed generalissimo of the Allied forces, Foch conducted the defensive against [[unrestricted-submarine-warfare-1917|the German offensives of 1918]] and then turned to the counter-offensive. His method, Recouly explains, consisted in multiple attacks that harried the fragile German facade (*la façade fragile*) — attacks designed to shake the German army of occupation and to overwork the German army of shock, in striking contrast with the single, concentrated blows of Ludendorff. When Ludendorff's 15 July offensive failed and was followed three days later by a graver failure, there began for the German leader the descent that would become ever more rapid and soon vertiginous. Foch's counter-offensives broke the German army in 1918; the offensive of [[hundred-days-offensive-1918]] that began on 8 August — which Ludendorff himself called the black day of the German army — carried the Allies to the verge of final victory. It was Foch who received the German plenipotentiaries at the little station of Rethondes in the forest of Compiègne on the night of 7 November 1918 and imposed the [[armistice-11-november-1918]] of 11 November that ended the fighting.

## Foch in Italy, 1917

Foch's name also connects the French and Italian commands. Cadorna's appendix, added to the second edition of his war account, treats the mission of Marshal Foch in Italy in October–November 1917, immediately after the catastrophe of [[battle-of-caporetto-1917]]. Cadorna destroys two legends at once: on one side the French legend that Foch came to Italy as a counsellor and inspirer of energy to the Italian Supreme Command, and on the other a legend, current in Italy, that Foch, arriving at the Italian headquarters at Treviso on 30 October, advised the retreat from the Piave to the Adige and the Mincio. Against both, Cadorna insists that Foch raised no objection to the sending of the first four French divisions of the 10th Army to the Montello, and that the offensive of 24 October had been foreseen, announced and awaited by the French command rather than a surprise. The exchange over Foch's mission is thus part of the larger argument over responsibility for the Italian disaster and over the creation of the Allied command that Foch came to personify.
