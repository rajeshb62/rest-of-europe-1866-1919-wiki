# Victor Emmanuel II (1820–1878)

Victor Emmanuel II, King of Piedmont-Sardinia and from 1861 the first King of Italy, stands in this corpus's frame as the constitutional monarch who bound the revolutionary conquest of the peninsula to an old dynasty — the man the people nicknamed the *Re Galantuomo*, "the Gentleman King." Born in 1820, he succeeded to the Piedmontese throne in 1849 on the abdication of his father Charles Albert, kept the constitutional *Statuto* that the revolutions of 1848 had wrung from the house, and reigned through the war of 1859, the annexation of central Italy, the Expedition of the Thousand and the conquest of the South, the disastrous [[italian-war-1866]] that yet brought the Veneto, and finally the [[capture-of-rome-1870]] that made him master of the Eternal City. He died in 1878, having outlived nearly every figure of the movement that made his kingdom. Tivaroni, closing his history with a gallery of the men of the Risorgimento, ends on "Vittorio Emanuele — *Re Galantuomo*; the good and intelligent King; the first King of Italy," and Oriani treats him as the fixed pole of the monarchical solution to which the whole country, against the republicans, finally adhered.

## The Gentleman King and the making of the Kingdom

Victor Emmanuel came to power in the year of the defeat of Novara, when his father's abdication left the young king to salvage what he could from the collapse of the war of 1849. The saving act, in the account of both Tivaroni and Oriani, was his fidelity to the *Statuto*: he kept the constitution and the parliament that the revolution had won, and so furnished the conservative monarchy of Piedmont with the liberal instrument it needed. Through the ministry of [[camillo-cavour]] after 1852 he set Piedmont on the course of a national policy, entering the Crimean war to win a seat at the councils of Europe, and arranging the secret meeting of Plombières with Napoleon III in 1858 that prepared the war of 1859. The battles of Magenta and Solferino and the annexations of central Italy in 1859–60 extended his rule from the Alps to the Adriatic, and on the death of Cavour the young monarch was left to consolidate a state far larger than the little subalpine kingdom from which he had started.

## The Expedition of the Thousand and the annexation of the South

The decisive year was 1860. While [[giuseppe-garibaldi]] carried the revolutionary flag through Sicily and Naples in the [[expedition-of-the-thousand-1860]], Victor Emmanuel and his government struggled to keep control of a movement they could neither bless nor suppress. The royal army marched through the Marches and Umbria to meet the Garibaldian conquest, and the king received the South by the plebiscite of 21 October 1860. Oriani's account stresses the necessity of the monarchy for the annexed Mezzogiorno: the aristocracy of the southern countryside was "transcinated into the movement by the monarchical element," and had the country constituted itself a republic, "a perhaps indomitable reaction would have burst forth in the South." The phrase that summed the policy for a generation — attributed by Oriani to [[francesco-crispi]] in 1864, that "the monarchy unites us and the republic would divide us" — was the ground on which the royal solution rested. Cavour's death on 6 June 1861 left Victor Emmanuel to preside over the first Italian Parliament and the unquiet consolidation of the new kingdom, shadowed by the brigandage of the South and the unsolved question of Rome.

## The war of 1866 and the Veneto

In 1866 the king led Italy into the [[italian-war-1866]] in alliance with Prussia against Austria, in return for the promised cession of the Veneto. The war was a humiliation for the new kingdom: at [[battle-of-custoza-1866]] on 24 June the army, commanded through Lamarmora, was thrown back, and at [[battle-of-lissa-1866]] on 20 July Admiral Persano's fleet was defeated by Tegethoff. Yet diplomacy recovered what arms had lost. Tivaroni records the irony by which Italy received the Veneto as the *cadeau*, the "gift" of the Emperor Napoleon III, who transmitted it after Prussia's victory at Königgrätz had settled the continental war — so that the province was handed to the kingdom without being won by it, and the Trentino, for which Garibaldi had fought, was excluded from the treaty. The rising of Palermo in September 1866 and the continuing unrest showed how little the annexation had satisfied the country.

## Rome: the Law of Guarantees and the last years

The last great act of the reign was Rome. After the failure of Garibaldi at [[battle-of-mentana-1867]], the Roman question remained frozen between the kingdom and the Holy See while the French garrisoned the Papal city. The crisis of 1870 dissolved the obstacle: the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]] drew the French troops home, and on 20 September 1870 the Italian army breached the walls at Porta Pia and entered Rome. Victor Emmanuel took up residence in the Quirinal, and in 1871 the [[law-of-guarantees-1871]] unilaterally defined the relations of the State and the Papacy, ending the temporal power while the Pope remained in the Vatican. The annexation of Rome was the completion of unification. The remaining years of the reign, as Tivaroni's retrospective presents them, saw the kingdom settle into its constitutional routine under the successors of Cavour, until the king's death in 1878. Tivaroni's closing judgment — the good and intelligent King, the first King of Italy — is the verdict of the monarchical Risorgimento that he embodied, and that Oriani, looking back across the whole struggle, concedes was the only solution under which the newly annexed South and the reluctant Papacy could be held together.
