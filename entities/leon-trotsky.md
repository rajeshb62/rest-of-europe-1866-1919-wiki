# Leon Trotsky

Leon Trotsky (1879–1940), born Lev Davidovich Bronstein, was a leader of the Russian Bolshevik party and, in the frame of this corpus, the man who faced the Central Powers at Brest-Litovsk in the winter of 1917–18 and there negotiated the peace that took Russia out of the war. He appears in these sources as seen from the opposite camps of the Great War: the Austrian foreign minister Ottokar Czernin met him at the negotiating table and left a vivid portrait of the Bolshevik orator reckoning on world revolution, while the White general Anton Denikin, surveying the collapse of Russia, counted him among the destroyers who had laid the Russian state low. He stands beside Lenin as one of the two architects of the Bolshevik seizure of power and of the revolutionary peace.

## The revolutionary and the return from America

Trotsky's path to the revolution of 1917 ran through exile. Czernin, describing the factions of the Russian revolution for his readers, notes the group of "Internationalisten" — those who wished to restore the International, called also Zimmerwaldists or Kienthalists — under the leadership of "Trotzki (recte Bronstein)," Trotsky (rightly Bronstein), the one returned from America, along with Larin, Martow and Martynow returned from Switzerland. The years of emigration, spent in Vienna, Paris and New York, had made him a figure of the international socialist movement; the revolution of February 1917 brought him home, and by the autumn he had thrown in his lot with Lenin's party. Denikin, in his survey of the two extremes of the Russian social order in which alone, he thought, there was a real strong will — "die leider dem Zerstören und nicht dem Aufbauen dienstbar," unhappily a will to destruction and not to construction — names the one extreme as having already given "Lenin, Bronstein, Apfelbaum, Uritsky, Dzerzhinsky, Peters": Lenin, Bronstein, Zinoviev (Apfelbaum), Uritsky, Dzerzhinsky, Peters. Trotsky stood in that catalogue as one of the principal names of the revolutionary camp.

## The seizure of power

The Bolsheviks took power in Petrograd in October 1917, in the coup that overthrew the Provisional Government of Kerensky. Trotsky was, after Lenin, the most prominent figure of the new regime, and it was he who became the people's commissar for foreign affairs. Denikin, from the side of the White command, saw in the advent of the Bolsheviks the culmination of the process by which the strong will of the extremes had triumphed over the weak will of the middle — the will to destruction prevailing over the will to construction. The revolutionary government's first acts — the decree on peace, the decree on land, the appeal to the peoples to end the war — were the work of the men who had seized the state, and Trotsky, as foreign commissar, was their spokesman toward the world.

## At Brest-Litovsk

The new regime's most pressing task was peace. Russia, exhausted by three years of war and now in revolutionary disintegration, confronted the Central Powers across a negotiating table at Brest-Litovsk in the winter of 1917–18. Czernin, who had travelled there as the representative of Austria-Hungary, records the Russian delegation that first appeared under Joffe — "ein geborener Jude namens Joffe," a born Jew named Joffe, with Kamenev, the brother-in-law of the Russian minister of foreign affairs Trotsky, and Madame Bizenko, all released from prison by the revolution. Then, at the decisive moment, Trotsky himself arrived. Czernin describes the arrival of Trotzki, the tension of the negotiations with Kühlmann, General Hoffmann and himself, the furious telegrams of Hindenburg over any "renunciation," Ludendorff's hourly telephone calls, and the demand of the Austrians for a free hand should the Germans break with the Russians. In the end Trotsky accepted the ultimatum, and the peace was signed.

## The reckoning on world revolution

What distinguished Trotsky at Brest was his reading of the situation as a revolutionary opportunity rather than a mere surrender. While Austria starved — Czernin records the "Hungerkatastrophe in Österreich," the famine catastrophe in Austria — and the Central Powers pressed their territorial demands, Trotsky "rechnet auf Weltrevolution," reckoned on world revolution. He did not treat the peace as a defeat but as an episode in a larger struggle; the collapse of the old order in Russia was, for him, the beginning of a European and world upheaval that would overtake the Central Powers in turn. Czernin, who had debated with the Bolsheviks the nature of power and of rule, was struck by this conviction and by the confidence of a man who could calmly accept an ultimatum in the certainty that history was on his side.

## The judgement of his adversaries

Czernin's portrait of Trotsky is that of an enemy who commanded a reluctant respect. In his account of the peace efforts he reflects on the coming of a "world of Bolshevism" — he wrote, in his closing reflections on the war, of the danger that anarchy and Bolshevism posed to Europe, and he recalled the remark of Charlotte Corday — "nicht einen Menschen, sondern eine wilde Bestie habe ich getötet" — as a warning of what might again be done to rid the world of such men, hoping that "diese Bolschewiken" would vanish and that a Corday might be found for Trotzki. Yet even in that hostility he granted the Bolsheviks the reality of their will and their doctrine. Denikin, for his part, treated the Bolshevik triumph as the working of the destructive will that had taken hold of one extreme of Russian society, and Trotsky, under his true name Bronstein, as one of the leaders who had brought Russia to ruin. Both accounts agree on the magnitude of the man's role: it was Trotsky who carried the peace of Brest-Litovsk through, and in doing so withdrew Russia from the Great War.

## Significance

Trotsky stands in this corpus as the Bolshevik statesman who concluded the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk and thereby ended Russia's part in the First World War — the indispensable lieutenant of Lenin in the seizure of power, and the negotiator whose confident reckoning on world revolution marked the revolutionary internationalism of the new regime. Seen through the hostile eyes of Czernin and Denikin, he is a measure of how the European order perceived the Bolshevik danger in the very year it was born. He would go on to found and command the Red Army in the civil war that followed the peace, but it is as the man of Brest-Litovsk that he enters this corpus's story of the years 1866–1919.
