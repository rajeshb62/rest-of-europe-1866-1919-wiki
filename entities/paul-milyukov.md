# Paul Milyukov

**Paul Nikolayevich Milyukov** (1859–1943) was the historian and statesman who led the Russian Constitutional Democrats (the Kadets) and became foreign minister of the first **Provisional Government** in 1917, the parliamentary voice of liberal Russia between the **Duma** and the revolution. In **Denikin's** account of the Russian Troubles he embodies the war-to-victory liberalism that the February Revolution could not sustain.

## The Kadet leader and the Duma

A professional historian of Russia, Milyukov turned in the revolutionary decade of 1905–17 to politics, founding and leading the Kadets, the principal liberal party of the Duma era. In the State Duma he was the spokesman of constitutional opposition to the autocracy, one of the figures **Zhevakhov's** loyalist memoirs single out in the assembly that confronted the Imperial government in the war years. His programme — a constitutional monarchy on the British model, civil liberty, and, after 1914, the faithful pursuit of the war against Germany — made him the leading representative of the liberal centre.

## Foreign minister and the fall

With the February Revolution of 1917 Milyukov became foreign minister in the first Provisional Government. Denikin records the defining act and its consequence: Milyukov's note to the Allies, insisting that Russia would fight to a victorious finish, unleashed the April crisis and forced his resignation. The demand for peace, coming from the streets and the soviets, overwhelmed the liberal minister who had staked everything on the war. He remained through 1917 a voice of the constitutional idea, and after the Bolshevik revolution emigrated, spending his later years as a historian of the Russian revolution abroad.

## Place in the corpus

For this knowledge base Milyukov is the emblem of liberal-constitutional Russia — the man who stood for the war and for the parliament against both the old autocracy and the coming soviet power, and who was crushed between them. His trajectory from Duma oppositionist to wartime minister to émigré historian traces the fate of Russian liberalism in a single life.
