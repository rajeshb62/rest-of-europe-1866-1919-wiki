# Wilhelm II (1859–1941)

Wilhelm II, German Emperor and King of Prussia from 1888 to 1918, was the sovereign of the Reich through the years of its greatest power and its overthrow. Grandson of [[wilhelm-i]], he dismissed [[otto-von-bismarck]] in 1890 and thereafter personified an assertive, restless German policy; in 1914 he led Germany into the war that destroyed his throne, and in November 1918 he fled to exile in the Netherlands as his empire collapsed. In this corpus's frame his story is that of the Kaiser who was "a prisoner of his generals" — the ruler whose divine-right conception of kingship, whose oscillations in war aims, and whose final helplessness before the Supreme Command frame both the causes and the defeat of the Great War. Czernin's memoir, written at the moment of the catastrophe, is the most intimate study of his character; Recouly and Lenz set him against the Germany that made and unmade him.

## The heir of Bismarck's empire

Wilhelm II came to the throne in 1888, the year of the three Kaisers, after the brief reign of his father Friedrich III. Lenz's biography of Bismarck frames his accession as the rupture that ended the chancellor's career: the old king [[wilhelm-i]], who in his deepest need had taken Bismarck's hand and kept faith with him to the last, was succeeded by a young emperor who feared no conflict, hoped rather through a policy of conciliation and social reform to win back the masses hostile to the state, and in 1890 dismissed the man who had made his empire. Lenz sets the grandfather's salvation against the grandson's rupture: the new Kaiser felt himself strong enough to govern without the Iron Chancellor. The dismissal marked the beginning of the *personal regime* of Wilhelm II, in which the monarch's temperament — restless, theatrical, eloquent and impetuous — came to stand for the policy of the Reich.

## Czernin's study of the Kaiser

Czernin devotes a whole chapter of *Im Weltkriege* to Wilhelm II, drawing on his own dealings with him in 1917 and 1918. He stresses Wilhelm's belief in the divine right of kings, his *Gottesgnadentum* — the conviction that his authority rested on God's appointment rather than on any contract with his people. Czernin treats the emperor crisis of 1908 (the *Daily Telegraph* affair, when Wilhelm's tactless interview exposed the isolation of the throne), his bearing before the war, at its outbreak, and at Kreuznach, his doubts about the war's outcome, and his oscillations in war aims. He describes Wilhelm at Kiel Week, his nature, and "the tragedy of his life": a man who combined great personal gifts with a temperament unfitted to the grim endurance the war demanded. Czernin records how the German Crown Prince became a pacifist in 1917, and how Ludendorff's will and judgment pressed upon the throne.

## Prisoner of the generals

The central judgment of Czernin upon Wilhelm II in the war is that he was from the first day "the prisoner of his generals." The complete displacement of the political factors by the military — the German Supreme Command (*Oberste Heeresleitung*) seizing all the command authority in the state — was, in Czernin's view, an unhappiness; had the political factors of Berlin been heard, there would have been neither the invasion of Belgium nor the [[unrestricted-submarine-warfare-1917|unrestricted submarine war]], and these two omissions would have saved the Central Powers. The blind faith in the invincibility of the army, like so much else, was an inheritance from Bismarck's legacy, and the "Prussian lieutenant that nobody in Germany can imitate" became the Kaiser's undoing. The whole German people believed in victory, and an emperor who had thrown himself across the path of his generals would have taken on himself a responsibility beyond the measure of the bearable. So Wilhelm let his generals act; when Hindenburg and Ludendorff became gods for the German people, they were far more powerful than the Kaiser, and he less than ever able to oppose them. Recouly strikes the same note: the war became nearly inevitable the day the civil chiefs of Germany, "the Kaiser first of all," seized by a contagious collective intoxication, were persuaded that Germany had not in the world the place to which it had a right.

## The failure of 1918 and the abdication

At the end, the Kaiser's helplessness was total. Recouly recounts how, after the defeat of 8 August 1918, Ludendorff — who had lost all confidence in victory — himself offered his resignation to the Kaiser, who naturally refused it, and how the German leaders sought to hand the burden of peace to the civilians they had so long treated with contempt. The military defeat unravelled the throne: the revolution of November 1918 swept away the monarchy, and Wilhelm II fled across the frontier into exile in the Netherlands, abdicating on 28 November 1918. Czernin's closing reflection on the German state of mind after the defeat — the assertion that the army had not been vanquished in the field but stabbed in the back by the revolution — is the legend by which Wilhelm's empire and its generals sought to escape responsibility (see [[stab-in-the-back-legend]]). The exile of the last Kaiser closed, in this corpus's frame, the story that had begun with the dismissal of Bismarck: the empire founded by the grandfather undone by the grandson's war.
