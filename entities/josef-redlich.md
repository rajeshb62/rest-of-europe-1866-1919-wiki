# Josef Redlich

Josef Redlich (1869–1936) was an Austrian constitutional jurist, historian and politician who, as professor of public law at the University of Vienna and a former minister, wrote the culminating Carnegie study of the Habsburg state's government and administration at war. His *Österreichische Regierung und Verwaltung im Weltkriege* (1925) is the authoritative account of how the Dual Monarchy governed during the Great War — from the pre-war system of emergency decrees, through the militarisation of the civil administration under the Army High Command, to the dissolution of the Reich and its legacy to the new national states. He was himself briefly minister of finance in 1918, in the last months of the monarchy, so that his history is also that of an insider who had held office at the hour of the collapse.

## The constitutional jurist

Redlich's work opens by announcing its subject: the Austrian war government as both political organ — "the bearer and expression of a particular conception of the Austrian state" — and as supreme administrative organ. He notes the peculiar pre-war condition of Austrian government: the ministry in Vienna had long in practice exercised the legislative power too, meeting the unavoidable needs of legislation through the emergency-notification provision of the constitution (Article 14) by imperial decree. The ministry had resolved to keep this system through the war, the Reichsrat having been sent home in the spring of 1914 on the renewal of obstructionist tactics among the Slav parties. Redlich frames the whole as the last chapter of three and a half centuries of Habsburg government and administration, for the state that bore it disappeared entirely with the war, "the Allied war aim of destroying the Habsburg realm having been realised in the peace treaties."

## The pre-war system

Redlich's first chapter analyses the pre-war foundations of state power: the Emperor and the question of the monarchy, the ministries and ministerial responsibility, the Reichsrat with its upper and lower houses and the nationality struggles within them, the organs of self-government and the provincial (crown-land) administration, and the political character of the civil service. He describes an administration already working largely by Article-14 decree and marked by deepening nationality conflicts. His third chapter, on the last peace government, records the disappointment at the supposed healing effect of the universal suffrage of 1907, the influence of the annexation of Bosnia in sharpening national antagonisms, the perpetual keeping-down of the nationalities by the Magyar gentry, the growth of an anti-parliamentary mood at court and among the generals, and the unceasing accession of national radicalism among Czechs, South Slavs and Ruthenians.

## The war government and its historian

The central chapters trace the war government itself: the suppression of parliament at the outbreak, the issue of the emergency decrees, the passing of civil power to the Army High Command over wide areas, the transformation of civil into military administration, the consequent permanent tension between government and army command, the war-surveillance office (*Kriegsüberwachungsamt*), and the system of the Austrian war government down to the assassination of the minister-president Count Stürgkh in the autumn of 1916. Redlich emphasises the supremacy of the Hungarian government and of Count Tisza, the subordination of Austria, and the "strictly bureaucratic character of the war government with its ruinous effect on the whole public condition of the state." He treats at length the war economy and the "state socialism of the war," the rationing of food, the central authorities for the food supply, and the war associations that militarised industry — a war economy that failed, in his judgement, "as a consequence of the overstraining of the physical, moral and spiritual forces of the population and of the excessive length of the war."

## The second period and the dissolution

The eighth chapter opens with the death of Kaiser Franz Joseph and the accession of Emperor Charles, the weakening of the army command's influence, and the succession of ministries from Koerber through Clam-Martinitz. It traces the convocation of parliament and the appearance of separatist strivings among Czechs and South Slavs, the amnesty granted by Charles and the resentment it provoked, the October Manifesto, the paralysis of parliament and its dissolution into purely national groups, and the beginning of the catastrophe in September 1918. The ninth chapter treats the fall of the Reich and the new national states. Redlich concludes that the October Manifesto of Emperor Charles was "the formal, legally valid dissolution of the historic unitary state": by his word the Emperor himself, exercising for the last time the right of autocratic constitutional legislation that the house of Habsburg had always claimed, called the complex of new national states into being. The dissolution went forward on a formally legal path; the last act of the dynasty gave the new states, ready formed in the person of their national officials, the organs with which to begin independent government. As minister of finance in 1918 Redlich was among the last bearers of that old-Austrian officialdom whose quiet continuance of work through the collapse he records with a historian's detachment.
