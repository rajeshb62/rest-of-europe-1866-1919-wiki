# Achille Bazaine (1811–1888)

**François-Achille Bazaine** was a marshal of France and the commander of the French Army of the Rhine in the war of 1870. His name is bound to the greatest disaster of the campaign after Sedan: the siege of Metz, into which the largest intact French army allowed itself to be shut, and the capitulation of that army in October 1870 while France still hoped for relief. He is the most contested figure in the corpus's account of the war, because on him converged the accusations of the generals, of the parliamentary opposition, and of the postwar courts — a controversy that is itself one of the sources' recurring themes, with Ollivier weighing the evidence for and against him.

## Command of the Army of the Rhine

After the defeats at Wissembourg, [[battle-of-woerth-1870]] and Forbach in the first days of August 1870, the French high command was thrown into confusion, and Marshal Bazaine emerged as the central figure of the army assembled at Metz. Ollivier records that the Emperor, whose health had broken and whose personal command had proved disastrous, was stripped of his authority and the command imposed on Bazaine, who had at first refused it; on 5 August the Emperor had met Bazaine at Boulay and taken no decision, and by mid-August Bazaine held the 2nd, 3rd and 4th corps under his orders as commander-in-chief. The plan of concentrating the army at Metz was Bazaine's, and it was there, before the fortress and the surrounding German armies, that the fate of the campaign would be decided.

## The question of responsibility at Forbach

Ollivier's analysis of the opening battles turns on the question **"Qui est responsable: Frossard ou Bazaine?"** — who was responsible, Frossard or Bazaine. General Frossard, beaten at Forbach on 6 August, tried to cover his failure by charging that Bazaine had not succoured him in time. Ollivier examines whether Bazaine was bound to come onto the field, weighing Frossard's claim that the combat was going in the French favour until four in the afternoon and that timely reinforcements would have turned the reverse into a victory. The episode established a pattern: Bazaine's slowness, his careful dispositions, and the accusation that he let an opportunity slip for lack of decision. Mac-Mahon, by contrast, "accused no one" and silently accepted full responsibility for his own defeat — a contrast Ollivier draws to the discredit of the Metz command.

## The siege of Metz, August–October 1870

After the defeats, the Emperor and the army fell back toward Châlons and Metz. Hanotaux records the fateful decision of the war of movement: Mac-Mahon's army, marching to the northeast to effect its junction with Bazaine, was forced into the funnel of Sedan by the speed of the Prussian armies and destroyed there on 1 September, while the Army of the Rhine remained shut up in Metz. From that point Bazaine commanded the last great intact French force, besieged by the Germans through September and October while Paris itself was invested. Hanotaux notes that Bazaine announced a sortie toward Montmédy, and that it was partly the hope of this junction that drew Mac-Mahon into the trap; but the promised breakout never decisively relieved the army, and the siege wore on. The surrender of Metz — a whole army laying down its arms — was, in the French reading the sources record, a catastrophe of the same order as Sedan, for it delivered the strongest fortress and the largest force to the enemy and left the national defence, after Sedan and the fall of the Empire, with almost nothing.

## Capitulation and the postwar trial

With the capitulation of Metz in October 1870, Bazaine passed into German captivity. After the war and the restoration of order, the Republic pursued him. Hanotaux records that at the apogee of Thiers's government, in the session of 1872, the National Assembly opened an inquiry on the capitulations and **Marshal Bazaine was sent before a court-martial**. The trial became a national reckoning over the disaster: the court found him guilty of surrendering an army and a fortress to the enemy in a manner contrary to honour, and condemned him. He was then a prisoner on the Île Sainte-Marguerite off the Mediterranean coast, from which, as the sources' frame implies, he escaped — condemned in his absence and spending his remaining years in exile, dying in Spain in 1888. The controversies of his command — his disputed slowness, his intentions, the question of what he owed Frossard and what the army owed France — remained a live wound in French politics, the theme that the participant sources (Ollivier) and the later national history (Hanotaux) both return to as they assign the blame for the fall of the Empire.
