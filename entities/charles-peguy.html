Charles Péguy

Charles Péguy (1873–1914) was a French poet, essayist and the founder of the Cahiers de la Quinzaine, the review through which he gave voice to the Dreyfusard generation of the Third Republic. Born at Orléans of modest stock, he arrived in Paris a socialist and a republican by heredity, and his public career was an unbroken argument with the France of his own time — an argument he carried on as a man of letters, editor and publisher rather than as a party man. In the corpus’s frame he stands chiefly for one idea: the distinction between a mystique and the politique into which it decays, and the conviction that the Republic of his fathers had been a thing of faith which his own generation had allowed to become a thing of office. He died on the Marne in September 1914, in the first weeks of the war that closed the period.

Founder of the Cahiers

Péguy was, above all, a man of the review. The Cahiers de la Quinzaine was not a newspaper but a continuing book, a series of pamphlets issued a dozen times a year, each devoted to a single work — often Péguy’s own, often the writings of his collaborators Daniel Halévy and Jean Variot and the circle gathered around him. It was, as he insisted, “une compagnie parfaitement libre d’hommes qui tous croient à quelque chose,” a free company of men who all believed in something. He ran the enterprise in poverty and defiance, printing what he chose regardless of the market, and in so doing made the Cahiers one of the rare organs of the intellectual Left that answered to no party discipline. Its survival through years of financial difficulty and isolation was itself, for Péguy, a profession of faith.

The mystique of the Republic

The central act of Péguy’s public life was the Dreyfus affair, which he called “our youth.” A Dreyfusard of the first hour, he treated the fight for the revision of Captain Dreyfus’s conviction not as a juridical quarrel but as the last surge of republican heroism — “le dernier sursaut, le soubresaut suprême de cet héroïsme et de cette mystique.” In Notre jeunesse (1910) he set down what the affair had meant. His generation, he held, was “l’arrière-garde,” a rear-guard a little isolated, the last that possessed the republican mystique — the lived faith for which “les républicains étaient héroïques et que la République avait les mains pures.” His fathers had possessed this faith as an organic instinct; the young had it only as a set of propositions. “La mystique républicaine, c’était quand on mourait pour la République; la politique républicaine, c’est à présent qu’on en vit” — the republican mystique was when men died for the Republic; the republican politique is when they live off it. That single sentence condensed his whole indictment of the Republic that had triumphed.

Mystique and politique

The distinction between mystique and politique was Péguy’s most famous legacy. A mystique, in his usage, was a living faith, a deep movement of the soul; politique was what the parties made of it once they took it over. The political parties, he wrote, can speak only the political, parliamentary language, and they translate — traducunt, tradunt, they translate and they betray — everything that is done for the mystiques. The corruption of Dreyfusard mystique into Dreyfusard politique was for him the emblem of the age: the generous enthusiasm of 1898 had become the spoils of office. He recalled that since that degeneration his circle had known fifteen years of poverty defending Christian consciences and republican liberties, only to find reactionary politicians forbidding them to be republicans and republican politicians forbidding them to be Christians.

Men of eternal and temporal salvation

Beneath the political quarrel Péguy found a religious one. The adversaries in the affair spoke the language of reason of state, of the temporal salvation of the people and the race — that one must not sacrifice a whole people for one man. The Dreyfusards, by contrast, spoke in a deep Christian movement: “une seule injustice, un seul crime… suffit à rompre tout le pacte social, tout le contrat social,” one injustice, one crime suffices to break the whole social pact. He drew the division between “les hommes du salut éternel” and “les hommes du salut temporel,” the men of eternal and the men of temporal salvation; the Dreyfusards did not wish France constituted in a state of mortal sin. To make the point he invoked Saint Louis, who told his clerk he would rather be a leper thirty times over than commit a single mortal sin. In the same spirit he honoured Bernard-Lazare, “né à Nîmes le 14 juin 1865; mort à Paris le premier septembre 1903,” as the prophet of the affair, the first defender of Dreyfus, whose word — “Il est innocent, c’est déjà beaucoup,” he is innocent, that is already much — he called “le mot décisif de l’affaire.”

Against the modern world

Péguy’s quarrel extended beyond the parties to the age itself. He set against his own generation “le monde moderne,” those who “ne croient plus à rien, qui s’en font gloire et orgueil,” who make a glory and a pride of believing in nothing — “pas même à l’athéisme.” He saw the de-republicanisation of France and its de-Christianisation as one single movement of “démystication,” by which the people no longer believed either in the Republic or in God, a “stérilité moderne” that dried up alike the political and the Christian city. It was, he wrote, “la première fois dans l’histoire du monde que tout un monde vit et prospère, paraît prospérer contre toute culture.” Yet he did not despair: “cette race en a vu bien d’autres,” and he hoped the coming generation would be “enfin une génération mystique.” He would not, for all his grief at the Republic’s decay, take refuge in derision — no culture is founded, he held, on sarcasm — and he closed Notre jeunesse on a note of defiant loyalty to the Republic, warning the royalists that if they ever became a real menace they would see what his company was still capable of doing for France.

Significance

Péguy stands in this corpus as the conscience of the Dreyfusard generation and the great theorist of the mystique-politique distinction. His essay Notre jeunesse is a defining text of the intellectual history of the Third Republic between the affair and the war, aligning the republican mystique with the Christian, setting the eternal above the temporal salvation of the state, and warning against a modern world that prospered against every culture. Killed on the Marne in 1914, he belonged both to the France that had come out of the affair and to the Catholic-renewal current that Sabatier surveyed on the eve of the war; his death made him one of the emblematic figures of the generation the war consumed.
