# Ferenc Deák

Ferenc Deák (1803–1876), whom his countrymen called *a haza bölcse*, "the sage of the fatherland," was the Hungarian statesman who prepared and carried through the Compromise (Ausgleich) of 1867. He had been the author of the April Laws of 1848 that set up the responsible Hungarian ministry; after the failure of the revolution and the years of absolutism that followed, he became the architect of the settlement that restored Hungarian self-government within a dualist monarchy. Beksics's *A dualismus* is in effect a monument to Deák and to the conception that bears his name — the work that, as Beksics concludes, "answers the interests of both Hungary and Austria" and "gave form to a centuries-long historical development." The great creation "on which Deák's name is written," Beksics insists, "need not be feared from the weak breezes of internal quarrels."

## The author of the April Laws

The constitutional settlement of 1848, by which the Hungarian state acquired a responsible ministry and its own ordering of the common affairs with Austria, was Deák's work. It was this foundation — the establishment of Hungarian statehood on the basis of the historical constitution and the Pragmatic Sanction — that the revolution of 1848–49 overran and that the reaction after Világos swept away. When the war ended in disaster and the *provisorium* of absolutist administration followed, Deák's programme of legal, constitutional opposition was the alternative to the insurrectionary path of Lajos Kossuth, whom Deák condemned. In the revolutionary years, as Sosnosky notes, Deák was counted among the ministers of the settlement, and his moderation stood in contrast to the passionate self-assertion of the Magyar movement.

## The architect of the Compromise

After the defeats of 1866, Deák resumed the negotiations between the Crown and Hungary. He could boast, as Sosnosky records, that even after the severe humiliation and weakening of Austria he asked no more for Hungary than before the war. His moderation was not generosity but prudence: he would not overdraw the bow, being certain he could obtain no more whatever he asked, and what he asked was already considerable — nothing less than the conversion of the old unitary empire into a novel double state. The Crown's first serious scruples concerned the unity of the army. In the drafting of the much-contested § 11 of the Hungarian Compromise act, Deák's first proposed wording spoke only of "the army"; the opposition in the subcommittee, led by Kálmán Tisza and Ghyczy, demanded that a separate Hungarian army be recognised. Deák answered that he too would wish for a separate Hungarian army, but that the law article of 1715 (which provided for a paid army maintained by Hungary but recruited from foreigners as well as Hungarians and at the ruler's disposal) stood in the way. He denied the historical right of a distinct Hungarian force with the famous words: "What is that, the Hungarian army? Such a thing has never existed, neither in law nor in practice; there was only ever talk of a block of Hungarian regiments." With these words, writes Sosnosky, "the sage of the nation," celebrated by the Magyars themselves as their greatest statesman, deprived the Magyar demands of their historical justification, and one of their main supports collapsed.

When the opposition still refused to accept the necessity of unity, Deák spoke the momentous words: "That would be personal union! Let us say the word, and the curse will fall upon us." Andrássy supported him. Yet Sosnosky notes a "fatal inconsistency": Deák was strangely induced, to satisfy Ghyczy, to weave the "Hungarian army" into the wording of the paragraph after all — an inconsistency that only the pressure of the moment explains, but which kept the dispute alive. The Compromise act, with these and other modifications, was adopted by the Hungarian lower house on 30 March 1867 by 257 against 117 votes, and shortly after by the Magnates. The Austrian Reichsrat, never asked to consent to the transformation of the whole monarchy, tacitly acquiesced — sending a deputation to the coronation of Franz Joseph as King of Hungary that amounted to a formal recognition of the Hungarian settlement.

## The "sage of the fatherland"

Sosnosky, though sharply critical of the Ausgleich as a work, is at pains not to wrong its creators. "Deák and Andrássy," he writes, "were moved by the best intentions and accomplished an extremely laborious work that put their patience and their working power to a hard test"; but patience and force of will are not enough to create a great work, which requires also success, and success "was not granted to the work of Deák and Andrássy," for it already carried in itself the visible germ of strife. Beksics takes the opposite view, defending the Compromise as the natural and indestructible outcome of centuries of development: the dualism existed before 1848, the common affairs and their management existed, and through all the centuries only one thing had proved firm and immovable — "the tie that binds Hungary to Austria through the dynasty, and its consequence, the union of the two mutually independent states for mutual defence and for that alone." On this foundation Deák's work rested, and against it the "weak breezes of internal quarrels" could not prevail. Whatever their difference of judgement, both writers agree on the scale of the man: the constitution-maker who stood at the head of the Hungarian nation in its settlement with the dynasty, and whose name gave its character to the Dual Monarchy that endured until 1918.
