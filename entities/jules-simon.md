# Jules Simon (1814–1896)

The philosopher-politician of the Third Republic — the successor of Dufaure, head of the ministry whose dismissal by Marshal Mac-Mahon on 16 May 1877 touched off the [[seize-mai-1877]] crisis. Jules Simon, a professor of philosophy and a republican of the liberal centre, governed in the difficult passage after the Constitution of 1875, caught between a conservative president and a republican Chamber. His fall at the hands of the Marshal became the decisive test of the parliamentary regime.

## The philosopher and the republican

Jules Simon was that rare figure in French politics: a professor of philosophy who turned to public life. Hanotaux records his standing as a thinker and writer on education and his long career in the liberal opposition under the Empire; he had been one of the ministers who, in the last days of the liberal Empire, had accepted the office that the imperialists finally wrecked. By 1876 he was a veteran republican of the centre, a man of moderate and constitutional opinions, at once too republican for the right and too cautious for the extreme Left.

## Dufaure's successor

After the elections of 1876 delivered a republican Chamber, Dufaure governed the first parliamentary ministry of the new regime; when he resigned at the close of 1876, unable to stand between Chamber and Senate, Mac-Mahon called on Jules Simon to form the ministry. Simon took office in December 1876, the first head of government chosen by the Marshal from the republican majority. His position was delicate: he had to satisfy the republican Chamber that had placed him there and the conservative president who had appointed him. He was, Hanotaux notes, embarrassed by his past and pressed on all sides — by the extreme Left demanding reform and amnesty, by Gambetta watching from the budget commission, by the Marshal testing how far he would resist.

## The religious question and 'le cléricalisme, voilà l'ennemi'

The decisive issue was religion. Simon's ministry had to deal with the ultramontane question — the interpellation on ultramontane manœuvres, the growing confrontation between the republican state and the Catholic hierarchy loyal to Pius IX. In the great interpellation of the spring of 1877, Jules Simon and Gambetta made common cause, and it was then that Gambetta launched his resounding phrase: *« Le cléricalisme, voilà l'ennemi ! »* ("Clericalism — there is the enemy!"). Simon, though himself a liberal who respected conscience, was pushed toward the anticlerical programme that the Chamber demanded. This was more than Mac-Mahon and the *ordre moral* party could bear: the Marshal's ministry, it seemed, was drifting into the arms of the enemies of religion.

## The letter of 16 May

Mac-Mahon, resolved to break with the Left majority, dismissed Simon on 16 May 1877 — by letter, in the brusque manner that gave the crisis its name. The president charged that Simon had not resisted the clerical-baiting of the Chamber, that he could not govern with a ministry the president could not trust. Hanotaux treats the act as the Marshal's deliberate assertion of the presidential power over the ministry. Jules Simon's dismissal, rather than quieting the crisis, provoked it: the republicans of the Chamber united against the Marshal, interpellated, refused the budget, and carried the country with them in the elections of 14 October 1877. Simon, the mild philosopher dismissed by a soldier, had provided the occasion on which the parliamentary Republic defeated the presidential interpretation of the [[french-constitution-1875]]. He remained thereafter a figure of the republican centre, respected but passed by the greater events of the consolidating Republic.
