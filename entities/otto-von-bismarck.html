Otto von Bismarck (1815–1898)

Otto von Bismarck, Prussian minister-president from 1862 and the first chancellor of the German Empire, is the central statesman of this corpus’s frame. Born in the year of Waterloo into the Prussia of Friedrich Wilhelm III, he spent a youth that Lenz’s biography opens against the background of an old, semi-feudal monarchical state, and lived long enough to overthrow Austria in 1866, to build the north-german-confederation, to turn the war with France of 1870–71 into the foundation of the german-empire-proclaimed-1871, and then to be dismissed by a young emperor on 18 March 1890. The arc of his story in this period is the creation, consolidation, and finally the breaking of the instrument he himself had forged — a state so shaped around his own genius that when the hand that guided it was removed, the structure outlived him. Lenz closes by writing that the Reich continued to praise its master, and that even the parties Bismarck had fought as mortal enemies came at last to submit to the Reichsgedanke, the imperial idea that he had made inseparable from the faith in the power of the monarchy.

The path to power: from Junker diplomat to minister-president

Bismarck came to the ministry late and reluctantly. Lenz’s account follows him through the Revolution of 1848, his service as Prussia’s envoy at the restored Frankfurt Diet, and his embassies at Petersburg and Paris, before the summons of 1862. The instrument of that summons was the constitutional conflict. Since 1858 the Prince Regent (the future wilhelm-i) and his war minister albrecht-von-roon had pursued an army reorganization that the liberal Chamber of Deputies refused to finance; the confrontation over the military budget drove crown and parliament apart. Roon, isolated in a liberal ministry and working to bring his friend into the cabinet, engineered the crisis of June 1861 over the question of whether the new king should celebrate his accession by an homage in the old ständische, or estate, forms — a move that would have flouted the constitutional state and affronted the liberal majority. On 26 June he wrested from the king the commission to look about for new ministers, and on the next day wrote to Bismarck at Petersburg.

Bismarck’s reply, quoted at length by Lenz, shows him already the man he was to be. The occasion seemed too trivial to him to justify a breach with the Chamber; what he insisted on was not the homage but the program. He demanded a full swing of foreign policy, the abandonment of the legitimist course that the king’s personal will had imposed on the conduct of Prussia’s external affairs, and — above all — the bringing of the German question into the center of action. “One must hold firm in the military question, break with the Chamber over it, dissolve it,” he wrote, “and then show the nation how the king stands with the people.” It was already the program with which he appeared in September 1862. On the decisive principle he never wavered: the crown’s power must be upheld at all costs, and he would rather fight for it than see Prussia’s strength consumed in the petty quarrels that had sapped crown and land alike.

The overthrow of Austria (1866)

For four years Bismarck governed against the budget, ruling without the approval of the Chamber while the constitutional conflict dragged on, before he turned the contest of domestic factions to the service of a German policy. The german-question — whether Germany was to be unified, and by which of the two great powers — was the field on which he chose to fight. His diplomacy from 1864 onward dismantled the old order of the German Confederation step by step. The conquest of Schleswig-Holstein from Denmark in 1864 left Austria and Prussia as joint administrators of the Elbe Duchies, and their rivalry over that inheritance, as Sybel’s fourth volume shows, poisoned relations between the two German powers throughout 1865. The Convention of Gastein (August 1865), which partitioned the administration of the Duchies as a temporary expedient, proved no remedy; it only postponed the clash that both governments were preparing.

Bismarck’s decisive stroke was the alliance with Italy. Sybel’s account of the spring of 1866 describes how he negotiated a Prussian–Italian treaty by which the Kingdom of Italy would enter the war against Austria in return for the prize of Venetia. While Vienna sought to secure the neutrality or the support of France through secret negotiations, Bismarck worked to isolate Austria and to fix the moment of war. On 14 June 1866 Prussia pronounced the federal constitution broken and ordered its armies to march against the states of the Confederation; the austro-prussian-war-1866 had begun. The northern campaign swept away Hanover, Electoral Hesse, and Saxony, and the main war was decided in Bohemia. At the battle-of-koniggratz-1866 on 3 July, the Prussian armies converged on the Austrian position behind the Bistritz, and the arrival of the Crown Prince’s Second Army on the Austrian flank turned the day. Austria was thrown back, and the preliminary Peace of Nikolsburg led to the Peace of Prague (23 August 1866), which dissolved the German Confederation, excluded Austria from Germany, and left Prussia to annex Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, and Frankfurt. The old order was gone; the road to a new Germany lay open.

The North German Confederation and the road to empire

Out of the victory Bismarck built the north-german-confederation, a federal state of the north German states under Prussian presidency, with a Reichstag and a Bundesrat. Sybel’s sixth volume traces the constitutional work of 1866–67: the drafting and revision of the federal constitution, the renewal of the zollverein treaties by which the customs union was made an instrument of Prussian policy, and the conclusion of military conventions with the southern German states that placed their armies under Prussian command in time of war. The question of the south — the great powers of Bavaria, Württemberg, and Baden stood outside the new federation — remained open, and with it the unresolved problem of the kleindeutsch-grossdeutsch division of Germany.

The breach was made by France. Sybel’s seventh volume traces the mounting tension from 1867, beginning with the Luxembourg crisis, in which Napoleon III’s attempt to purchase Luxembourg from the Netherlands collapsed before the threat of war and a London conference instead guaranteed the Duchy’s neutrality. The years 1869–70 brought the hohenzollern-candidature-1870: the offer of the Spanish crown to Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, which Bismarck promoted and then had to disavow when France protested. The crisis came to a head at Ems, where the French ambassador Benedetti pressed the old king wilhelm-i for a guarantee that no Hohenzollern would ever again seek the Spanish throne. Wilhelm’s courteous refusal, telegraphed to Berlin, was edited by Bismarck into the curt ems-dispatch-1870 that so stung French honour that Napoleon III, goaded into war, declared it on 19 July 1870. The southern German states, bound by the new national feeling and their military conventions, marched with the north. The war that followed — the franco-prussian-war-1870-71 — brought the annihilation of the French army at Sedan, the capture of Napoleon III, and the siege-of-paris-1870-71.

In the midst of it, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on 18 January 1871, the German Empire was proclaimed. Lenz records the laborious negotiations with the South German princes that went on “until the morning of 18 January,” and the scene when, before the victorious banners, the Kaiser’s son-in-law, the Crown Prince, raised the first Kaiserhoch. Bismarck, who had shown no reluctance to brush aside German princes when it served Prussian power, now took no village more for his king. Lenz marvels at the tact with which he grasped “the positive of power,” leaving the allied dynasties the emblems and rights that gave a gentle appearance of self-rule without disturbing the real substance of the whole.

Kulturkampf and the break with the Liberals

The first task of the new Reich, as Lenz frames it, was to secure in peace what the war had won. From the turn of the 1870s Bismarck turned against the Catholic Church in the Kulturkampf (“culture struggle”) — the assault on the influence of the clergy and the Centre party, which he saw as a hostile, ultramontane power within the German state. But the struggle wore him down, and by the end of the decade he executed the sharpest reversal of his career. The turning point came with the attacks on the life of the Emperor. Lenz describes how, when the news of the second attempt — Nobiling’s assault in June — reached him in the Sachsenwald, Bismarck’s first words were “Now we dissolve the Reichstag!” He seized on the wave of popular horror to carry an exceptional law against the revolutionary movement, and to break the National Liberals, who had previously refused him. Their independence was destroyed, their internal unity shattered, and the split that Bennigsen had long staved off could no longer be prevented. From 1879 Bismarck governed on a new basis: a tariff reform, a reconciliation with conservatism and the Church, and the abandonment of the liberal alliance. The Kulturkampf was abandoned, and the Centre, once his enemy, became one of the pillars of the new order.

Foreign policy and the fall of 18 March 1890

After 1871 Bismarck’s foreign policy was the maintenance of the peace that Germany’s victories had secured. Lenz’s chapter on foreign policy in the new Reich describes the alliance system by which he sought to keep France isolated and the great powers bound to Germany. He presided over the congress-of-berlin-1878, which redrew the map of the Balkans after the Russo-Turkish war. But his last years were shadowed by the rise of a new emperor. Wilhelm II, who came to the throne in 1888, was of another temper than his grandfather, and the clash of Kaiser and Kanzler grew inevitable. In the divergence of their political directions Lenz locates the “tragic solution of 18 March”: Bismarck, the convinced monarchist, was dismissed by his monarch in 1890, after the young Kaiser chose a policy of conciliation and social reform rather than the chancellor’s renewed conflict with the Reichstag.

Retirement to the forest quiet of Varzin and Friedrichsruh, which Lenz calls the havens he had freely sought and longed for, brought him no rest. He remained the fighter he had always been, unable to keep silence, meeting his enemies “with full force and the pride that a life of unheard-of victories justified.” The loneliness of his first months yielded to a growing tide of homage; the love of the nation for the founder of its unity flamed ever hotter. “And when the giant oak of the Sachsenwald fell,” Lenz writes of his death in 1898, “friend and foe bowed in admiration and reverence before the sublime shade of the man who, equal in war and peace, had opened to his people the path to power.”
