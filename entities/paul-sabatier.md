# Paul Sabatier

Paul Sabatier (1858–1928) was a French Protestant pastor and historian, best known for his *Vie de saint François d'Assise*, which earned him the Institut's grand prix décennal in 1905. In the corpus's frame he appears as the sympathetic observer of the religious life of the Third Republic in the years after the Separation of Church and State — the author of *L'orientation religieuse de la France actuelle* (1911), a study of the contemporary religious movement of France written neither as an ecclesiastic nor as a polemicist but as a historian of the "mouvement social contemporain." A friend of the Catholic Modernists and an enemy of anticlericalism as much as of clerical reaction, he sought to discern, beneath the surface of indifference and political conflict, a genuine religious orientation that France itself had created.

## The historian of Saint Francis

Sabatier's authority as an observer of the religious movement rested on his biography of Saint Francis of Assisi, the work that had made his name and won the Institut's grand prix décennal Lefèvre-Deumier in 1905. He wrote, he insisted in the Introduction to *L'orientation religieuse*, not as an ecclesiastic but in the spirit of the series in which the book appeared — "faire connaître et comprendre le mouvement social, exposer les faits et les interpréter, sans jamais se substituer à eux," to make known and to understand the social movement, to set out the facts and interpret them without ever substituting himself for them. He took up the antique formula *Historia magistra vitæ* and gave it an ampler meaning: history no longer gives only examples of individual conduct, it rises to the conception of the living germs that the past contains and that the present must select as the seeds of a new century.

## Religion and its orientation

Sabatier distinguished "orientation religieuse" from "affaires religieuses." The latter designated what is most exterior in religious and ecclesiastical facts, what they become when carried onto the political markets and used in the quarrels of parties — a chronicle written day by day and left to the newspapers. The book proposed instead to discover "des aspects de réalité et de vie," aspects of reality and of life. What many simple minds sought in religion, he found, was a fundamentally moral foundation; the religious movement always worked itself out at the expense of the churches; and the Church, for all her rights to the recognition of France and her activity through organs like the *Bonne Presse* to conquer the direction of political opinion, had not succeeded in arresting a new religious orientation that was taking shape outside her.

## The circumstances of the orientation

Sabatier located the origins of the present religious situation in the disasters of 1870. The war against Prussia dealt, in his judgement, an irreparable blow to the prestige of the Reformation in France — the conquest was turned into a moral as much as a political question — while the Church, whose individual priests had shown themselves admirable citizens, did not on the morrow of the disasters know how to rise to the height of its task. He rejected the formula that patriotism had become the religion of France, and counted among the factors that shaped the religious orientation the laic school, accused by its enemies of being a nursery of crime; the Church, he noted, by appealing ceaselessly to the limitless devotion of her children, answered alone the most powerful instinct of the human heart.

## The deficits of antireligion

Sabatier was as severe toward the antireligious Left as toward the clerical Right. Free thought, he insisted, must not be confounded with anticlericalism. He observed the profound antipathy for clericalism found among peasants who yet refused to join the anticlerical organisations, and the incapacity of anticlericalism to understand what the Catholicism of the rural populations actually was — a religion that gave the sense of discipline, of solidarity, of harmony. The efforts of scientific religion had little success. He noted the negative impression produced on some spectators by the missionary zeal of certain converters, and excluded from his study the eccentric and the morbid.

## The contemporary philosophy and the movement

The philosophers, Sabatier held, had not created the present religious orientation. Christianity at its origins was a popular movement, not provoked by philosophical views; the efforts of Pillon, Renouvier and Secrétan, though of high value in intellectual circles, had no appreciable practical influence. It was rather because Bergson, Boutroux and William James answered the sense of life that the present generation attached itself to them. He dwelt on Guyau's *Irréligion de l'avenir*, a work of ever-growing importance, and on Boutroux, who went still further in the way of realism — seeing in the churches an effort toward the ideal and manifesting to all a sympathy at once disinterested and efficacious, very new among philosophers.

## The manifestations in church, Protestantism and free thought

Surveying the movement's workings, Sabatier found two catholicisms coexisting in the Church, and the Modernism struggling against a Roman administration he described as a committee of public safety; the diocese of Milan and the University of Fribourg were alike accused of being nurseries of modernism. In Protestantism he found rather the absence of manifestations — the Separation had permitted new schisms in the Reformed Church, while the most evolved Catholics showed themselves severe toward it. In free thought he found something not essentially anti-religious; he noted the significance of the success of the *Cahiers de la Quinzaine*, and the union of freethinkers and free believers. He devoted a long section to the creation of the laic school, whose soul, he argued, came from farther and higher than the war of 1870 — it was "la nouvelle incarnation d'un effort religieux séculaire," and its course in 1882 was far from anticlerical.

## Significance

Sabatier closed on two facts in appearance contradictory: the rapid progress of indifference and an unexpected awakening of religious aspirations, often to be observed in one and the same individual. France, he believed, had arrived at an epoch of reconstitution, "une époque de reconstitution, de revanche religieuse," analogous to the origins of Christianity. Though the Roman Church appeared struck more than any other by the political and intellectual crisis, it was the Church that had sowed in the heart of the present generation the sentiments of the mystery, of unity, of tradition, of duty — and it alone, despite its failures, kept in the conscience of the elite of the coming generation a unique place. His book is a central document of the religious question of the Third Republic between the Separation of 1905 and the war, written from the borderland between Protestantism, Catholicism and free thought.
