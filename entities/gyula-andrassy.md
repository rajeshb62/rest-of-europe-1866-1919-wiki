# Gyula Andrássy

Count Gyula (Julius) Andrássy the elder (1823–1890) was the Hungarian statesman who, after Ferenc Deák had prepared the ground, carried the Compromise of 1867 into being as the first Hungarian minister-president under the dualist system, and then, as joint foreign minister from 1871 to 1879, pursued the Balkan programme and concluded the alliance with Germany. Sosnosky counts him "the most significant statesman" to have directed the foreign policy of the realm since Prince Felix zu Schwarzenberg — a minister who, unlike the mere officials who usually held the office, knew what he wanted and did it. (His son of the same name, Gyula Andrássy the younger, was to be foreign minister again in 1918, in the last months of the monarchy.)

## The maker of the Compromise

Andrássy stood at the side of Deák in the negotiations of 1866–1867. When the Hungarian opposition, in the subcommittee drafting the common affairs, insisted on a separate Hungarian army, it was Deák who denied the historical right of such a force and Andrássy who supported him — reminding the opposition, as Sosnosky records, that with two legislatures and two governments a modern state could not also have two armies. As the first Hungarian minister-president under the settlement he took the lead in the great transformation that Beksics describes as the restoration of Hungarian statehood within the dualist frame: Hungary became once more a self-governing state, joined to Austria by the shared person of the ruler and by the common affairs of foreign policy, war and finance, for mutual defence and that alone.

## The Eastern programme and the occupation of Bosnia

As joint foreign minister from 1871 Andrássy turned the monarchy's attention to the East. He had recognised, as Sosnosky puts it, "that for the monarchy there was nothing more to seek in Germany, that its role there was played out forever, and that its future tasks lay in the Orient, on the Balkan." He pursued so purposeful an Oriental policy that Prince Gortschakow found it advisable to keep on good terms with him. In the conventions of Reichstadt (8 July 1876) and Budapest (15 January 1877) Austria-Hungary and Russia fixed their spheres of influence in expectation of a Russo-Turkish war, and Andrássy secured for his realm the possession of Bosnia and Herzegovina — important, above all for military reasons, as the hinterland of Dalmatia — as the compensation for Russia's expected acquisitions.

When Russia, after defeating the Turks, imposed the Treaty of San Stefano with only a modest role allowed to Austria-Hungary, Andrássy refused to accept this breach of the earlier conventions and, with England as a powerful ally, forced the settlement to be revised on the European stage. The Congress of Berlin of 1878 restricted Russia's claims and conferred on Austria-Hungary the mandate to occupy Bosnia and Herzegovina. But the occupation proved far harder than Andrássy expected. The story was current that he said a single company of infantry with the regimental band would suffice to subdue Bosnia; whether the jest was really his or was put into his mouth, Sosnosky notes, his hope of acquiring the provinces without blood and treasure proved an "egregious error." Not one company but a whole army corps and a division were needed, and after more than two months of costly fighting the fanaticised population was at last mastered — resistance secretly supported by the Porte, which allowed Ottoman troops to make common cause with the insurgents while the two states were officially at peace.

## The alliance with Germany

Andrássy did not annex Bosnia — a "grave omission" which, Sosnosky holds, was to exact a bitter reckoning thirty years later — and he did not long remain in office after the occupation. He resigned in 1879 for reasons his biographer Wertheimer was expected to clarify. But before he left the field he bequeathed to the realm its most valuable legacy: the *Schutz- und Trutzbündnis* (defensive and offensive alliance) with Germany, concluded shortly before his retirement to secure both states against an attack by Russia. This, Sosnosky argues, was a deed that in part outweighed his error in the Oriental question. The Dual Alliance of 1879, as the same work shows in its survey of Austria-Hungary and the German Empire, became the foundation of the monarchy's place in Bismarck's and then Wilhelmine Germany's system of alliances. Andrássy had laid the basis of the alliance "in union with Bismarck," with whom he went together, "in defiance of all revanche-lusts."
