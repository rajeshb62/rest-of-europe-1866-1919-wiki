# Franz Ferdinand

Archduke Franz Ferdinand Karl Ludwig Joseph Maria (1863–1914), nephew of Emperor Franz Joseph and, after the death of his father Archduke Karl Ludwig, heir presumptive to the Habsburg throne, was at his fiftieth birthday in December 1913 the object of an extraordinary national attention — the man "who would one day be called to direct the destinies of the realm." In the seven months that followed he was shot dead with his wife at Sarajevo on 28 June 1914, and the shots set the world war in motion. In the last years of the Dual Monarchy he had become the focus of a programme of military strengthening and of a possible reorganization of the state — the circle of hope for those, like Conrad von Hötzendorf, who wished to see the monarchy gird itself for the coming struggle.

## Birth and ancestry

Sosnosky, writing the biographical portrait in the *Österreichische Rundschau* Sonderheft, records that Franz Ferdinand was born on 18 December 1863 in the Herberstein palace in Graz, "a Christmas gift" to his parents, Archduke Karl Ludwig and Archduchess Maria Annunziata, a Bourbon of the Two Sicilies. His father, the brother of the Emperor, had withdrawn from political life two years before and lived as a private man in Graz. By his descent, as a genealogist had shown, the archduke united in his veins the blood of no fewer than a hundred and twelve ancestral houses, and in eleven generations counted over two thousand verifiable ancestors — making him one of the most richly-ancestored princes in Europe.

## The soldier and inspector-general

The military essays of the Sonderheft ground the heir's martial zeal in the traditions of the dynasty — from the founder Kaiser Rudolf, through Maximilian the knight and Charles V, to Maria Theresia. Voinovich, the general of infantry, argues that the archduke was convinced "that the foremost means to the greatness of the state lies in the sharpening of the sword," and that he gave the chief weight of his labours to the armed power of the monarchy, labouring without rest to raise it. The marine essay recalls that it was reserved for the heir to give the navy, neglected in detail by his war-lord uncle, his more particular care. As inspector-general of the armed forces Franz Ferdinand worked for the unity of all the forces of the realm and the suppression of "all destructive tendencies and separatist strivings" — the very programme with which Conrad, as he records, found himself in agreement when the archduke sounded him out on a long carriage-drive after manoeuvres in 1901.

## The traveller to the East

The world journey was, in the estimate of both Sosnosky and Rear-Admiral Koudelka, who accompanied him, the decisive turning-point of his life. Sailing from Europe on 15 December 1892 in his twenty-ninth year, he returned with rich ethnographic and natural-scientific collections and, more than that, "a direction for his later years" — a knowledge of foreign lands and peoples that, as the soldierly essay insists, only strengthened his love of the fatherland. His patronage extended to the academies of sciences of Prague and Cracow and to a series of sciences, above all botany and the study of home, while at Konopišt in Bohemia he built up, over more than twenty years, a park of some 340 hectares and collections of armour, trophies and works of art celebrated throughout Europe.

## The South Slav problem and the trialist ideas

In the last years of his life Franz Ferdinand stood at the centre of the debate over the future of the monarchy. The South Slav question, Conrad records, was among the questions the heir put to him in 1901, and Conrad urged a settlement favourable to the Croats. Sosnosky, surveying the possible futures of the monarchy on the eve of 1914, sets out trialism — the conversion of the dual state into a triple state by the addition of a South Slav unit — as the idea "on the agenda," and notes that its adherents looked to a strong hand at the head of the state to impose it against the resistance of the Magyars, who would lose Croatia-Slavonia and their only access to the sea. The heir's reputation as a champion of military and central power made him the natural patron of such a project, even though, as Sosnosky observes, the obstacles on the Austrian as much as the Hungarian side made its realisation doubtful. Redlich, writing after the catastrophe, judged that the death of the archduke at Sarajevo destroyed the possibility of breaking the Magyar supremacy from within the monarchy — the "second possibility" of reform that vanished with him.

## Sarajevo

On 28 June 1914 the heir to the Habsburg throne and his wife fell to an assassin's bullets in Sarajevo, the capital of the annexed province of Bosnia-Herzegovina. The shots at once extinguished the one figure around whom the hopes of military and constitutional reform had gathered, and they provided the occasion for the July crisis that carried Europe into war. The Monarchy that marched to war in the summer of 1914 was thus deprived of the very man who, in the judgement of his contemporaries, had been the strongest will for its renewal.
