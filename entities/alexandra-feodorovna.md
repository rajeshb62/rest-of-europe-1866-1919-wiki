# Empress Alexandra Feodorovna

**Alexandra Feodorovna** (born Princess Alix of Hesse-Darmstadt, 1872–1918), wife of the last Emperor **Nicholas II** and Empress of Russia from 1894, appears in the ecclesiastical court memoirs of **Nikolai Zhevakhov** as a figure of intense religious devotion whose piety drew her into the shadowy circles of the Imperial court on the eve of the revolution. Her reign as consort coincides almost exactly with the closing generation of the Russian autocracy that this corpus documents from within.

## The Empress and the Church

Zhevakhov, a deputy ober-procurator of the Holy Synod and a loyalist witness of the Imperial household, presents Alexandra as a sincerely and deeply religious woman whose Orthodox faith was the governing impulse of her private life. Her audiences with churchmen, her interest in the affairs of the Synod, and her support of pious foundations run through his recollections of the war years, 1915–1917. In the same pages she is inseparable from the figure of the starets **Grigori Rasputin**, whose spiritual influence over the Tsarina was a scandal that the court could neither silence nor explain. Zhevakhov's account is that of an insider who reveres the Empress yet cannot conceal the entanglement that discredited the dynasty.

## The war years and the fall

In the months of the war the Empress's authority at Tsarskoye Selo grew as the Tsar was away at the Stavka; her letters and her interventions, always cast by her defenders as maternal concern for her son's health and for the dynasty, were read by contemporaries as the hand of a woman governed by the starets. The February Revolution of 1917 put an end to the monarchy she embodied; she was arrested with her family in March, and the corpus's sources close with the household's overthrow rather than its fate. The last glimpse is the Empress as a figure of the old court at the moment of its dissolution.

## Place in the corpus

For this knowledge base Alexandra is the female pole of the Rasputin question and the most intimate emblem of how the religious life of the Imperial court, in the years 1915–1917, had come to appear both fervent and fatally compromised. Her story binds the church-ecclesiastical thread of Zhevakhov's memoirs to the collapse of the monarchy narrated by **Denikin** and **Witte**.
