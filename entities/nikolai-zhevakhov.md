# Nikolai Zhevakhov

Prince Nikolai Davidovich Zhevakhov (1874–1946) was a senior official of the imperial State Chancellery who became deputy ober-procurator of the Holy Synod in the autumn of 1916 and served in that post through the last months of the monarchy until his arrest in the February revolution. He is the author of two volumes of memoirs, *Vospominaniya tovarishcha ober-prokurora Svyateishago Sinoda* (\"Memoirs of a Deputy Ober-Procurator of the Holy Synod\"), which record the Church, the court, and the fall of the dynasty from a loyal, monarchical, and deeply religious standpoint. He is at once a witness of [[grigori-rasputin]] and the Rasputin scandal, of the Synod in the revolutionary days, and of the Church's fate through the civil war and emigration.

## The official and the court

Zhevakhov's first volume opens in September 1915 with his mission to deliver the Nechaian Icon of the Mother of God to the imperial headquarters (Stavka) at Mogilev — a journey that carried him to audiences with Nicholas II and [[alexandra-feodorovna]], and into the circles of the court and the Church that the war had brought together. An official of the State Chancellery, he was close to Count Rostovtsov and the court intermediaries, and his portrait of the Sovereign is that of a pious, unassuming man whom the author defends against the calumnies of the age. His account of Metropolitan Pitirim, of the ober-procurator A. N. Volzhin (with whom he broke over the Synod's finances), and of his own appointment as deputy ober-procurator in the autumn of 1916 gives the reader a view of the synodal establishment in its last months.

## The Rasputin excursus

The heart of the first volume is an extended meditation on Rasputin and on Russian "God-seeking." Zhevakhov, writing as an apologist for eldership and for the imperial family, insists that the danger lay not in the starets himself but in those who used his name to destroy the dynasty — that, as he put it to a court lady, "talk about Rasputin is more harmful than Rasputin himself." He records the hysteria of the capital, in which every appointment and every high position was ascribed to Rasputin's influence and men sought to clear themselves of the suspicion of nearness to him; he records also the faith of the Empress, and of the Tsar himself — "if not for the prayers of Grigory Efimovich, I should long ago have been killed," the sovereign is said to have told a lady of the court. His account of the murder of Rasputin in December 1916, and of the "exposures" against the court, is written from the official side, as the record of a man who saw the attack on the starets as an attack on the dynasty. The second volume, in the same key, carries the theme into its wider historical frame.

## The Synod in the February days

Zhevakhov's narrative of the collapse is that of an insider. Returning to Petrograd on 24 February 1917, he watched the revolution gather; his account of the memorable sitting of the Holy Synod of 26 February — when he urged the hierarchy to issue a threatening pastoral appeal to the people and was refused by Metropolitan Vladimir — shows the Church, in his telling, failing to answer the state's need at the decisive moment. Arrested on 28 February on the oral order of [[alexander-kerensky]], he was led through the hostile crowd to the Tauride Palace and imprisoned in the Ministerial Pavilion of the Duma, where the arrested dignitaries of the old regime heard the news of the abdication. His release, the scenes of the February street, and his reflections on the "fateful epoch" close the volume.

## Exile and the Church after the revolution

The second volume carries Zhevakhov through 1917–1920: the months in Kiev, the siege of the city, the murder of Metropolitan Vladimir (January 1918), the German arrival and the Hetmanate of Skoropadsky, and his movement through the South with the retreat — Pyatigorsk, Ekaterinodar, where Metropolitan Pitirim died, and Novorossiysk, whence he departed for emigration. The second half of the volume turns to publicistics: the "methods of extermination" of the Cheka and famine, the condition of the Church under the Soviet power — the Council and the restoration of the patriarchate, the election of Patriarch Tikhon, the persecutions, and the split between the Moscow Synod and the Church Abroad. His central historical thesis, developed in his chapters on the nature of autocracy, is that Russian autocracy was "not a political but a religious" idea — the people's faith in the anointed tsar — and that the revolution marked the ruin of that faith. As a memoirist he is the wiki's indispensable source for the Church's side of the Russian Troubles.
