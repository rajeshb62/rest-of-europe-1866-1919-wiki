# Count Leopold Berchtold (1863–1942)

Leopold Graf Berchtold, Austro-Hungarian minister of foreign affairs from 1912 to 1915, presided over the monarchy's diplomacy through the Balkan wars and into the catastrophe of 1914 — the man who, as the hand on the Ballhausplatz during the [[balkan-wars-1912-13]] and the [[july-crisis-1914|July crisis]], dispatched the ultimatum to Serbia that opened the First World War. His story in this corpus's frame is the attempt to hold the monarchy's Balkan position by a policy of peace and of standing fast — the status-quo diplomacy of the Balkan wars, the creation of Albania, the securing of Rumania — that ended in the declaration of war against Serbia in July 1914. Sosnosky closes his account of the Balkan wars with a chapter on "Berchtold's Balkan policy," and Czernin's memoir opens its account of the catastrophe on "Berchtold and the ultimatum to Serbia."

## The status-quo diplomacy of the Balkan wars

Berchtold came to the foreign ministry in 1912 in the shadow of the first [[balkan-wars-1912-13|Balkan war]], when the Balkan states had formed their league and thrown themselves upon Turkey. His policy was one of the status quo and of the preservation of the monarchy's legitimate interests in the peninsula. The watchword that the Balkan peoples advanced — "the Balkans for the Balkan peoples" — he answered with the insistence that Austria-Hungary too had legitimate interests that could not be ignored. His diplomacy sought to hold the great powers together, to prevent the war from spreading, and to secure for the monarchy a share in the settlement that its position in the East demanded.

His most important constructive act was the creation of Albania. When the Balkan war threw up the Albanian question and the rising of 1912, Berchtold championed the formation of an independent Albania as a barrier to the advance of the Balkan states and a check on the Serbian and Montenegrin ambition to reach the Adriatic. Sosnosky's account of the struggle over Albania and of Montenegro's attempt to seize Scutari, with the naval demonstration before that town, belongs to the story of this policy. Berchtold's refusal to occupy the Sandjak of Novi Pazar, his championship of Albania, and his handling of the Prochaska affair were, Sosnosky judged, the elements of a policy that sought peace without surrendering the monarchy's standing.

## Rumania and the securing of the rear

A central object of Berchtold's diplomacy was Rumania. With the Balkan war forcing Austria-Hungary to reckon with the possibility of warlike complications and with Russia, it became advisable to secure Rumania for that case while not letting the thread with Bulgaria — Rumania's jealous rival — break. Berchtold, Conrad records, was unremittingly concerned to shape the policy of the monarchy in this sense: to bring Rumania and Bulgaria into accord and to have Rumania actively at its side with all its forces for the eventual war against Russia. It was on this ground that in mid-November 1912 the archduke-heir charged Conrad with his mission to Bukarest, to work in this sense under the pretext of conveying the Kaiser's condolences to King Carol. The policy of securing Rumania was pursued down to the eve of the war, and its failure — Rumania's eventual entry on the other side — belongs to the larger tragedy of the monarchy's position.

## The ultimatum to Serbia

The gravest act of Berchtold's tenure came in the summer of 1914. After the assassination of the archduke-heir at Sarajevo, it was Berchtold who framed the demands to Serbia and dispatched the ultimatum, and Czernin's memoir treats the catastrophe with an account of "Berchtold and the ultimatum to Serbia," of the warning of the German ambassador Tschirschky — who, Czernin held, more than Bethmann Hollweg drew Austria-Hungary into the war — and of the opposition of the Hungarian minister-president Stephen Tisza to the sharp ultimatum. The ultimatum, Czernin observed, did not mean that war was irrevocably willed but was conceived as a diplomatic demand to be pressed to the last; when Serbia accepted it only incompletely, the ground was laid for the war that followed. Czernin, who judged the monarchy's greatest misfortune to be the German invasion of Belgium and who regarded Austria-Hungary's disintegration as by then irrevocable, places the responsibility for the ultimatum squarely on Berchtold's period of office.

## Resignation and legacy

Berchtold's tenure did not survive the opening of the war. He resigned in 1915, succeeded in a foreign policy that the war had swept beyond the control of the diplomatists, and his name remained attached to the ultimatum that had set the catastrophe in motion. In the corpus he stands as the last of the Ballhausplatz statesmen of the pre-war generation — the heir of [[alois-aehrenthal]], against whose successor [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]] again pressed the preventive war he had urged all along, and the predecessor of [[ottokar-czernin]], who carried the diplomacy of the monarchy through the middle of the war it had helped to unleash.
