# Anton Denikin

General Anton Ivanovich Denikin (1872–1947) was a commander of the Russian army in 1917, the successor to General [[lavr-kornilov]] at the head of the Volunteer Army in 1918, and the author of the *Ocherki Russkoi Smuty* (\"Essays on the Russian Troubles\") — the great staff memoir-history of the collapse of authority and the army. His testimony frames this wiki's account of the Russian revolution from the side of the command: as a general on the Romanian front in February 1917, as quartermaster-general at Stavka under Alekseev through the spring, as a prisoner of Bykhov with Kornilov in the autumn, and as the man who took up Kornilov's work and led the [[white-movement]] after his death.

## Stavka and the collapse of the army

Denikin came to the revolution as a corps commander on the Romanian front, absorbed in local concerns and isolated from the capital. The February revolution and the abdication of [[nicholas-ii]] he learned of "afterward"; his first volume records the slow ruin of the army under the new order. The decisive blow was Order No. 1 of the Soviet, which set up soldiers' committees and broke the authority of the officers; Denikin's account of the supplementary Order No. 2 and of the whole committee system shows the discipline of the ten-million-strong army dissolving within months. Arriving at Stavka on 25 March, he was made quartermaster-general by General Alekseev on the frank understanding that he would speak his mind. His impressions of Petrograd at the end of March, the "trifles of life at Mogilev," and his portrait of General Markov — the second quartermaster-general, bound to him "inseparably" until his "glorious death" at the head of the Markov division — give the reader the texture of the command that tried, and failed, to hold the army together.

## The Kornilov affair and Bykhov

In the struggle of the summer of 1917, Denikin was drawn wholly into the orbit of Kornilov. His second volume — *The Struggle of General Kornilov* — is at once a defence of the Kornilov movement, an indictment of [[alexander-kerensky]], and a participant's account of the rising. He was arrested with the other commanders after the collapse of the [[kornilov-affair-1917]] and imprisoned at Bykhov, where the general staff of the coming White movement passed the autumn in confinement. His account of Kerensky's "victory" — the solitary premier, the soviets' growing power, the army without leaders — is among the sharpest analyses in the whole source. When the Bolsheviks took power in October and the old army dissolved, the Bykhov prisoners escaped to the Don and found their way into the Volunteer Army that Alekseev and Kaledin were forming.

## Successor to Kornilov

At Ekaterinodar in the [[ice-march-1918]], when Kornilov was killed on 31 March 1918, Denikin — his chief of staff — was asked whether he would take command. His answer, recorded in the memoir, is terse: "Yes." He lifted the siege of Ekaterinodar, led the army back through the Kuban to the Don, and there, joined by the Drozdovtsy and with the Germans already appearing on the Don, began the longer war that his later volumes would carry beyond this wiki's sources. His speech at Ekaterinodar in 1919, printed as the preface to the second volume, is the emblem of his cause: a curse on those who in Kornilov's lifetime awaited his execution and promised flowers on his grave, and a tribute to the general "who loved Russia more than himself." Denikin survived the White defeat to die in exile in America in 1947, his *Essays* remaining the indispensable record of the Russian Troubles from the side of those who fought to hold the state together.
