# Comte de Chambord, Henri V (1820–1883)

The legitimist pretender to the throne of France, known to his partisans as Henri V, whose refusal to abandon the white flag of the Bourbons blocked the monarchical restoration of 1873 and thereby sealed the survival of the Republic. The comte de Chambord, grandson of Charles X, was the last serious claimant of the elder line; his letter of 27 October 1873, refusing the tricolour, destroyed the fusion of the two branches of the house of Bourbon and forced the monarchical majority to fall back on the [[septennat-1873]] that handed the Republic its foundation.

## The pretender

The comte de Chambord was the posthumous son of the duc de Berry, born at the height of the Restoration in 1820, and the recognized head of the elder Bourbon line. Exiled after 1830, he had lived at Frohsdorf and the Austrian and Italian courts, the perpetual claimant of a throne France had not offered him. Hanotaux records his character as a man of principle and of the old monarchy, faithful to his faith and to his flag, who regarded himself as the trustee of the legitimacy of the Bourbons. His sense of royal honour, and of the divine right on which he believed the crown rested, was absolute.

## The hopes of 1873

After the fall of [[adolphe-thiers]] on 24 May 1873 the monarchical majority of the National Assembly believed the restoration was at hand. The programme was "fusion": the union of the two branches of the house of Bourbon, by which the comte de Paris, head of the Orléans branch, would defer to the elder pretender, and the crown of a restored monarchy would pass in time to the Orléans. The comte de Paris came to Frohsdorf; the Orléans princes were elected deputies; the three monarchical parties — legitimist, Orléanist, and Bonapartist — plotted together. Hanotaux frames the whole campaign as a high cause ruined in verbal dissension, a tragicomedy of representative illusion disposing of France without consulting her.

## The mission to Salzburg

The decisive episode was the delegation of Charles Chesnelong to the comte de Chambord at Salzburg in October 1873. The monarchists, anxious to secure the pretender's acceptance of the tricolour flag of the Revolution, sent their emissary to obtain his agreement to the constitutional conditions and above all to the flag. Chesnelong's interviews at Salzburg seemed to promise much; he returned on 16 October believing the campaign could go forward. But the pretender's prolonged silence, and the reports that filtered out, alarmed the monarchical chiefs who had already committed the army and the country.

## The letter of 27 October

On 27 October 1873 the comte de Chambord wrote his fateful letter, refusing the tricolour and insisting on the white flag of the Bourbons. "The only sacrifice I cannot make is that of my honour," he declared; by his unshakable fidelity to his faith and his flag, he argued, it was the honour of France itself that he upheld. The letter killed the restoration at a stroke. The comte de Paris's unpublished letter of 15 October had already sketched the line of retreat toward the Marshal; the monarchical campaign was abandoned, and the council of ministers pronounced instead for prorogation of Mac-Mahon's powers. The pretender's intransigence had done what all the republicans could not: it had cleared the way for the Republic.

## The man who saved the Republic

In the aftermath Chambord still contemplated a direct adventure, and he came to Versailles, where Blacas carried his cause to the Marshal — who refused to receive him. The monarchical chiefs, their plans in ruins, clung to the septennat; the extreme right was left in embarrassment, and Chambord left Versailles. Hanotaux's conclusion is that the expectant policy once more profited the Republic: by refusing the one concession that could have crowned a king, the legitimist pretender condemned the monarchical cause and assured that the Republic would be voted — by a majority of one voice on 30 January 1875 — in a hostile Assembly. The comte de Chambord, who believed he was defending the honour of France, was in the event the unwitting founder of the Third Republic. He died in 1883, the monarchy never restored.
