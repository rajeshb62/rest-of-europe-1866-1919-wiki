# Ottokar Graf Czernin (1872–1932)

Ottokar Theobald Otto Maria Graf Czernin von und zu Chudenitz, Austro-Hungarian minister of foreign affairs from December 1916 to April 1918, was the monarchy's last-but-one foreign minister and the author of its most sustained peace efforts in the First World War. His story in this corpus's frame runs from his embassy at Bukarest on the eve of the war, through his elevation to the Ballhausplatz at the end of 1916, his peace feelers of 1917, the treaty of Brest-Litovsk, and the quarrel over the Sixtus affair that brought his resignation in April 1918. His memoir *Im Weltkriege* (1919), written in the year of the armistice, is his account of the diplomacy of the war and of the men — [[franz-ferdinand]], [[wilhelm-ii]], the Emperor Charles, the German generals — among whom he moved.

## The minister in Bukarest

Czernin's first great appointment was as Austro-Hungarian minister in Bucharest, from the autumn of 1913. His memoir of Rumania is a portrait of the court of King Carol, the aged pro-German sovereign, and of the Rumanian statesmen among whom the Austrian envoy moved in the months before the war. On the day of Sarajevo, and still more after the ultimatum to Serbia — "Austria has gone mad," was the cry that passed through Rumanian society — a wave of hatred swept over the country, and Czernin, who judged that Bratianu was never truly neutral and that Queen Marie was for war, watched Rumania slip from the alliance the monarchy had laboured to secure. He was interned at the outbreak of war, recalled to Vienna, and passed in due course to the foreign ministry. His judgment of Hungarian policy in these years was that Hungary should make concessions to the Rumanians while [[stephen-tisza|Tisza]] refused — a refusal he regarded as one of the monarchy's great mistakes.

## The peace efforts of 1917

Czernin became minister of foreign affairs in December 1916, in the fourth winter of the war, and at once confronted the decision on unrestricted submarine warfare. He records the peace offer of the Central Powers, the judgment of [[wilhelm-ii]] that the Entente had slapped him in the face, and the triumph of the submarine war against which he and Prince Hohenlohe had warned; and he set down the warning of the German leaders, still optimistic even in April 1918, and the judgment that Germany had misjudged the situation.

The longest chapter of his memoir treats the peace efforts of 1917, which form the substance of his policy. In February 1917 came peace feelers from tsarist Russia; then the outbreak of the Russian revolution, and new fears on the Thames. In April 1917 he reported to the Emperor on Austria's exhaustion, the dull grumbling of the masses, America's intervention, and the necessity of peace. He sought to reach the German Reichstag, worked with Erzberger and Südekum, and witnessed the Reichstag peace resolution of 19 July 1917 and the fall of Bethmann Hollweg, its victim. But the demands of the German supreme command — military control of Belgium, of Liège and the Flemish coast — and the belief of Paris and London in the dissolution of the Fourfold Alliance combined to defeat the peace wish in the Entente, and the efforts failed. His Budapest speech on world disarmament and his flirtations with a separate peace were interrupted, in the autumn of 1917, by President Wilson's Fourteen Points and the approach of [[treaty-of-brest-litovsk-1918|Brest-Litovsk]].

## Brest-Litovsk and the peace of Bucharest

Czernin's account of the peace of Brest-Litovsk is among the most vivid passages of his memoir. He travelled to Brest-Litovsk to meet the Bolshevik negotiators — Joffe, Kamenev and Frau Bizenko — and then Leon Trotsky, whose arrival he awaited and whose acceptance of the ultimatum he records. He demanded a free hand for himself in case the Germans broke with the Russians, and he watched the German generals, Hoffmann and Ludendorff, press for a harsher peace than he favoured, and the German Kaiser's telegram demanding Livonia and Estland. He describes the peace with the Ukraine and the *Brotfriede* (the "bread peace") that was to relieve the famine catastrophe in Austria. His advocacy of a peace without annexations stood against the German appetite, and the divergence between the German and the Austrian conceptions of the settlement runs through his whole account of the negotiations.

After Brest-Litovsk came the peace of Bucharest, the separate peace with defeated Rumania. Czernin treats the Rumanian and Hungarian questions of that settlement — the message of the Emperor Charles to King Ferdinand, Austria's claim to parity, and his own constraint before the Hungarian frontier demands and Germany's economic demands — and he describes his conversations with King Ferdinand and the ministers who made the peace. The separate treaties of the Central Powers seemed to crown his diplomacy; but they were the last successes, and they were followed swiftly by the collapse of the Austrian war effort.

## The resignation

Czernin's tenure ended in April 1918 in the "letter affair" — the public quarrel over the revelations of the Emperor Charles's secret peace overtures, the "Sixtus letters," which the German generals and the Austrian court could not forgive. He offered his demission, which the Emperor at first refused, and the full constitutional responsibility for the affair was, as he records with irony, afterwards claimed by a minister who had known nothing of it and had not even been in office at the time. He resigned in April 1918, and his place passed to others as the monarchy slid toward its dissolution. After his demission, he wrote, Germany stood at the height of its military power, but the possibility of a peace of understanding had already been lost.

His closing reflection on the war is fatalistic: the longer the world war lasted, he wrote, the more it lost the character of a work directed by individual men and took on that of a cosmic event that escaped the interference of even the most powerful individuals; the promises given to the peoples and the artificially bred boundless hatred made every single man a small pebble of a stone avalanche that plunges down leaderless, choiceless and aimless. The Vienna peace settlement that followed the war, he held, had destroyed what little possibility of a just order remained, and he closed on the warning that anarchy and Bolshevism were siblings and a danger to the coming generation.
