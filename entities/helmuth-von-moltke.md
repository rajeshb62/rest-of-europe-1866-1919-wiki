# Helmuth von Moltke the Elder (1800–1891)

Helmuth von Moltke the Elder, chief of the Prussian General Staff, was the military architect of the victories of German unification — the man who planned and directed the campaigns that threw Austria out of Germany in 1866 and brought France to defeat in 1870–71. This page concerns the elder Moltke, the strategist of Königgrätz and Sedan, and not his nephew, the Chief of Staff of 1914 whose name became attached to the failure of the opening campaigns of the First World War. In the wars of 1866 and 1870 the elder Moltke's planning, his system of concentration, and his disciplined delegation to his subordinates gave Prussia an advantage that outweighed numbers and that his contemporaries, in the words of the sources, could only describe as a masterpiece of operational art.

## The staff system

The foundation of Moltke's achievement lay in the institution he commanded. As chief of the Prussian General Staff he made of that body a permanent school of strategic thought and detailed planning, in which the movement of armies, the supply of troops, and the alternatives of the campaign were worked out in peacetime so that in war the execution might follow the design. Lenz's biography shows Moltke's place in the military order that the army reform of [[albrecht-von-roon]] and [[wilhelm-i]] had created: he was, in Lenz's reckoning of the great organs of the state, one of the men of the military class who thought alike — "Moltke just as much as Roon and Edwin Manteuffel" — in whom the crown's reorganized army rested. The Army Reform had filled the officer corps with the military families; Moltke, from that corps, gave the professional direction that the enlarged army needed.

## The planning of 1866

The Bohemian campaign of 1866 displayed Moltke's method at its most characteristic. Prussia disposed its field forces against Austria in three separate armies — the Elbe Army, the First Army, and the Second Army — advancing by different routes through the passes into Bohemia. The risk of such an arrangement was that the enemy might concentrate and fall on the separate parts before they could unite; the whole art lay in bringing the three masses together at the decisive point and moment. Sybel's account emphasizes how Moltke had to judge the enemy's dispositions, correcting the assumption that the Austrians would gather in Bohemia when in fact they concentrated far to the east in Moravia, at Olmütz. "Moltke explained to him very calmly," Sybel writes of one such interchange, "that the enemy stood not in Bohemia but assembled in Moravia" — a knowledge that governed the whole design of the advance.

At the climax, before Königgrätz, Moltke's instructions to the commanders showed the balance of his system: a precise statement of the great purpose, and full freedom in the choice of means. On the evening of 2 July, learning that the Austrians had crossed the Bistritz in strength near Sadowa and that a meeting with the First Army might be expected the next morning, he wrote to the Crown Prince ordering him at once to make his dispositions to support the First Army, to advance against the right flank of the enemy's probable march, and to intervene as soon as possible. The earlier orders given "under different circumstances" were, he added, no longer binding. Sybel comments that the direction that was to decide Germany's future was, again, entirely in Moltke's manner — "specific statement of the great object, full freedom in the choice of means." It was the exact opposite of the Austrian commander Benedek's procedure.

## Königgrätz

The risk of the plan was grave. The total Prussian strength, as Sybel computes it, was not greater but slightly less than the enemy's: 221,000 men against 222,000 Austrians, the latter massed on a narrow space, the former converging on the battlefield from miles away. The Crown Prince's divisions had four to seven hours of marching over different and everywhere bad roads before they could reach the enemy; a delay, and the First Army might be crushed by nearly double its number. What Prussia had to set against this was, in Sybel's words, "merely an imponderable magnitude: the moral strength of the Prussian army." And in that moment, after the victories already won, it was permitted to count on it. There was no distrust of self or of comrades; there was neither waywardness nor lack of independence among the leaders, and all looked with entire devotion to the "genial and rapid decision-power of the supreme direction." Discipline, honour, and patriotism combined in overwhelming aggressiveness and inexhaustible endurance.

So it proved. The battle of [[battle-of-koniggratz-1866]] on 3 July 1866 — the decisive day that Sybel says was a day of world-historical significance — began with the First Army forcing the crossing of the Bistritz and seizing the villages along the Austrian front, while the Austrians held the commanding heights of Chlum and the Swiepwald behind Sadowa. The fight was long and bloody; the weight was carried by the Prussian infantry armed with the quick-firing needle-gun, which outmatched the muzzle-loaders of the Austrians. The crisis came when the Prussian centre seemed to have exhausted itself against the Austrian line. Then, in the afternoon, the Crown Prince's Second Army arrived on the Austrian flank and rolled up the defence; the position at Chlum, seized at the height of the battle, decided it. The Austrian army, its centre broken, streamed back toward Königgrätz in retreat. A defeat of that magnitude ended the war at a stroke.

## From 1866 to Sedan

The victory of Königgrätz made Moltke's reputation, and the same system was applied on a grander scale against France in 1870. When the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]] broke out in July of that year, Moltke directed the concentration of the German armies on the French frontier and their march across it in a series of great enveloping movements. The wars against [[napoleon-iii]]'s empire were decided by the same convergence of separate armies on the decisive point: the battles of August (which Lenz invokes alongside Sedan as scenes graven on the memory) destroyed the main French armies in the field, and the encirclement at Sedan in September 1870 — the "judgment of God at Sedan," as Lenz calls it — compelled the surrender of the Emperor himself with his whole army. The fall of Sedan ended the Second Empire and left Paris to be besieged. Moltke's planning had carried the war to a conclusion in weeks, with a completeness that astonished Europe.

In the new German Empire, Moltke remained the symbol of the military power that had made the Reich possible. He is remembered here, with Roon and the King, as one of the three men whose separate arts — the statesman's, the organizer's, and the strategist's — combined to win the victories of 1866 and 1870. He died in 1891, in the first years of the new Reich he had helped to create.
