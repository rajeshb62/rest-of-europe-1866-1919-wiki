# Agostino Depretis (1813–1887)

Agostino Depretis, leader of the parliamentary Left and prime minister of Italy through most of the 1880s, was the master of the political practice that his own era named [[trasformismo]] — the absorption of opposition deputies into the government majority and the dissolution of clear party lines. Born in 1813, a veteran of the Risorgimento and of the first parliaments of the kingdom, he governed the new Italy for a decade, presided over the African conquest that ended in the disaster of Dogali, and died in 1887, leaving the stage to [[francesco-crispi]]. Where Crispi was the strong man and the author, Depretis was the adroit parliamentary manager; Oriani, who judged the post-Cavour ministries harshly, wrote that Depretis "conserved of Cavour the parliamentary dexterity and the practice of all the portfolios, yet like all the merely able was small."

## The parliamentarian of the Left

Depretis's rise was through the Left of the Italian Parliament, the party that had opposed the long dominance of the Right since unification and that finally came to office in 1876 with the "advent of the Left." Oriani's ninth book, "Le due monarchie," records the exhaustion of the Right, the fall of the last Minghetti ministry, and the accession of the Left under Depretis — a change of government rather than of constitution, which nevertheless gave the country a new political generation. Depretis brought to the premiership the habits of the old parliamentary world: flexibility, patience, and a genius for holding majorities together by concession rather than by force. He was the incarnation of the practice Oriani describes as legislative empiricism — the continuous, improvised innovation by which a government that had to innovate in order to conserve kept the machine running.

## Trasformismo

Depretis's distinctive contribution to Italian politics was [[trasformismo]] itself. Rather than rule through a disciplined party, he drew deputies of the Right and of every nuance into his government majority, so that government and opposition ceased to correspond to any stable principle and the Chamber became a shifting mass around the prime minister. Fortunato, who fought against the practice from the South, recorded it in his very first speeches: the "dissidence" that broke the Depretis majorities in 1880, and the elections of 29 October 1882 in which trasformismo reached its fullest expression — the absorption of old enemies, the levelling of the historical parties, and the confusion of every line of political principle. For Fortunato, Depretis's method was the ruin of the parliamentary regime, dissolving the parties into a shapeless court of the premier; for the state's defenders it was the necessary art of governing a country of incompatible regions and interests. Either way, it was the politics of Italy for a generation, carried on after Depretis by Giolitti.

## The African conquest and Dogali

Depretis's last years were occupied with the extension of Italian power into Africa, in the wave of European colonial expansion that Oriani treats as "la conquista africana." The kingdom took the Red Sea port of Massawa in 1885 and pushed inland, and on 24 January 1887 an Italian column was cut to pieces by the Ethiopians at Dogali — a defeat that Oriani's account places at the climax of the volume, the first great humiliation of Italian imperialism. The disaster fell at the end of Depretis's career, when his health was failing; he died in 1887, before the consequences could be pursued, leaving the colonial question and the government to Crispi. Fortunato, looking back from the aftermath, saw in the whole colonial turn the origin of the calamities that followed, and his speeches are a running indictment of the policy that Depretis had launched and Crispi would carry to catastrophe.
