# Count Alois Aehrenthal (1854–1912)

Alois Lexa von Aehrenthal, Austro-Hungarian minister of foreign affairs from 1906 to his death in 1912, was the author of the annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina, the decisive act of the monarchy's Balkan policy in the years before the First World War. His story in this corpus's frame is the effort to reassert the Habsburg position in the East by a bold stroke — the tearing up of Article 25 of the Treaty of Berlin — and the international crisis that stroke set off, together with his long rivalry with the chief of the general staff, [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]], over the question of war against Serbia. Sosnosky's survey of the monarchy's politics opens its account of foreign policy on the road "from Schwarzenberg to Aehrenthal," and closes its chapter on the annexation with an assessment of "Ährenthal's annexation policy," marking him as the figure on whom the Eastern orientation of the monarchy converged.

## The making of the foreign minister

Aehrenthal came to the Ballhausplatz in 1906 as the heir of a tradition of Austrian policy that had, since the withdrawal from Germany after 1866, shifted the weight of the monarchy toward the East. Sosnosky's account of the monarchy's foreign affairs reviews this road from the age of Prince Felix zu Schwarzenberg and the reaction after 1848 down to Aehrenthal's chancellorship, and presents the annexation of October 1908 as the culmination of the shift toward the Balkans. Aehrenthal's instrument was the settlement of Berlin: the monarchy had occupied and administered Bosnia-Herzegovina since 1878 under the mandate of the [[congress-of-berlin-1878]], but the two provinces remained under the nominal sovereignty of the Sultan, and the legal condition of the occupation was provisional. To give the administration a secure constitutional footing and to end the ambiguity, Aehrenthal conceived the plan of annexing the provinces outright.

## The annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina (1908)

The act of annexation was accomplished on 6 October 1908 — a day, Sosnosky notes, of ominous association in Austrian history, for it was the date of the murder of Field Marshal Count Latour by the Viennese mob in 1848. The Kaiser's hand-written letter to the minister of the exterior, dated at Budapest on 5 October 1908, declared that the cultural and political purposes for which the monarchy had undertaken the occupation and administration of Bosnia-Herzegovina, and the successes of the existing administration bought with heavy sacrifices, could be durably secured only by the granting of constitutional institutions, for which the creation of a clear and unambiguous legal standing of the two lands was the indispensable prerequisite; he therefore extended the rights of his sovereignty over Bosnia and the Herzegovina, set the succession order in force for them, and, in token of his peaceful intentions, ordered the evacuation of the Sandjak of Novi Pazar. A proclamation to the inhabitants followed, promising constitutional institutions and a share in the decisions over the affairs of their homeland. The formal announcement appeared in the *Wiener Zeitung* of 7 October, while the daily papers printed the text in their morning editions of the preceding day.

The annexation tore up Article 25 of the Treaty of Berlin and provoked an international crisis. There followed, as Sosnosky recounts, the crisis with the Porte, the storm in Serbia and Montenegro, and the stand of the great powers. In Belgrade the extra sheet of the *Politika* announced the annexation, and the Serbian foreign ministry, as Conrad recorded, demanded immediate mobilisation and war to the death against the monarchy. But the crisis was weathered: Austria-Hungary asserted itself, the war preparations of 1909 were completed, and the storm subsided.

## The Friedjung trial

The annexation crisis carried with it a celebrated internal trial. The historian Dr. Heinrich Friedjung, who had been in communication with Count Aehrenthal and had used documents supplied to him in an attack on the South-Slav leaders, was confronted in the course of 1909 with the charge that these documents were forgeries, and the libel proceedings that followed — the [[bosnian-annexation-crisis-1908|Friedjung trial]] — became a scandal of the crisis. Sosnosky, who knew Friedjung and used his writings, records the affair and its bitter effect on Friedjung's judgment, observing that Friedjung's own opinion of Aehrenthal was by no means turned by his painful experience with him. The trial, in which the forged character of the documents against the South-Slav leaders was exposed, damaged the moral standing of the annexationist cause and became one of the enduring embarrassments of Aehrenthal's policy.

## The rivalry with Conrad

Aehrenthal's most fateful domestic contest was with the chief of the general staff. Conrad, on entering office, sought an intimate accord with the foreign minister, writing him a courteous letter that set out his view of the connection between external policy and war preparation; the reply was formally correct and obliging, but the accord was never reached on all questions. The two men divided above all over Italy and Serbia. Conrad pressed for a preventive war against Serbia and regarded Italy as a latent enemy against whom one must prepare; Aehrenthal, pursuing a policy of diplomatic assertion that sought to avoid a general war, resisted the army's demands. The divergence over the army budget deepened through 1911 until, in November 1911, the conflict with the minister of foreign affairs contributed to Conrad's dismissal from the post of chief of the general staff. The clash between the soldier who urged war and the statesman who sought to win by a bold but limited stroke is one of the central tensions of the period, and it was cut short only by Aehrenthal's death early in 1912, shortly before the outbreak of the Balkan wars that vindicated, in Conrad's eyes, his warnings.
