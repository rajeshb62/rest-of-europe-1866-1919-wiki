# Konstantin Pobedonostsev

Konstantin Petrovich Pobedonostsev (1827–1907) was the reactionary conscience of the last two Romanov reigns — Ober-Procurator of the Holy Synod from 1880, the tutor of both [[alexander-iii]] and [[nicholas-ii]], and the most influential advocate in Russia of autocracy and Orthodoxy as inseparable supports of the state. He was the man who, in the weeks after the assassination of Alexander II, turned the new emperor from reform toward the manifesto of 29 April 1881 that reaffirmed the *samoderzhavie* (\"autocracy\"); and he lived on into the reign of Nicholas II, where his successor in the Synod's service, [[nikolai-zhevakhov]], could look back on him as the great proponent of the very order that collapsed in 1917.

## The tutor and the counter-reformer

Pobedonostsev's influence rested on a paradox Kornilov notes: he had been one of the authors of the judicial statutes of Alexander II — a reformer by formation — yet by 1881 he had become the head of the reaction. His power came from his place as the former tutor of the emperors, who trusted him as they trusted no minister. When Alexander III hesitated in March 1881 over the constitutional project of Loris-Melikov, it was Pobedonostsev who at the conference of 8 March denounced the whole course of the previous reign as the work of "talking-shops" — the zemstvos, the new courts, the licentious press — and warned that the proposed representative commission would be a "supreme talking-shop" preparing the ruin of Russia. With the Moscow publicists Katkov and Aksakov as his allies, he worked on the emperor's own conservative instincts, and by the end of April had obtained from him, secretly and to the surprise of the other ministers, the commission to draft the manifesto. Its words — that the emperor was summoned by the voice of God to stand firmly to the work of government in the trust of "the strength and truth of autocratic power" — set the course of the whole counter-reforming reign.

## Ober-Procurator of the Holy Synod

As Ober-Procurator, the lay head of the Holy Synod — the government's eye over the Church — Pobedonostsev stood for the identification of Orthodoxy with the state and for the defense of the established order against all that threatened it. His influence extended through the Church into education and the press, and his was the voice that championed the autocracy as the one form of government fitted to Russia. Kornilov's account of the reaction of the 1880s repeatedly marks his role — in the restriction of the zemstvo, the persecution of non-Orthodox and non-Russians, the whole narrowing of the public life that the Great Reforms had opened.

## The Zhevakhov legacy

For the witness of the Synod in the last years, Zhevakhov supplies the later testimony. Writing as a loyal church official of the monarchy's final years, Zhevakhov treats the religious and conservative ideal that Pobedonostsev represented as the true foundation of the Russian state. In the second volume of his memoirs he expounds at length his thesis that Russian autocracy was "not a political but a religious" idea — the people's faith in the anointed tsar as the basis of power — a thesis that is in effect the Pobedonostsev doctrine restated at the moment of its ruin. By the time of the February revolution the dynasty had long outlived the strength of that idea, and the Church itself, as Zhevakhov's account of the February days shows, no longer answered the state's appeal for support. Pobedonostsev, who had died in 1907, had lived to see the failure of his school; it was his successors, not he, who watched the autocracy fall.
