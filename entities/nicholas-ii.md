# Nicholas II

Nicholas II (1868–1918), the last Emperor of All the Russias, reigned from the death of his father Alexander III in October 1894 to his abdication in March 1917, and died with his family at Ekaterinburg in 1918. His reign is the story of an autocrat who inherited a state already under strain and watched it pass through defeat in war, revolution, a grudging and then retracted constitution, and finally the collapse of the dynasty and of Russia itself in the Great War. The three witnesses of this wiki see him from three angles: [[sergei-witte]], who served him and who records an emperor he found unprepared, indecisive, and in his later years surrounded by men who ruined what he himself had built; [[nikolai-zhevakhov]], the loyal synodal official, who saw the collapse as a "fateful epoch" in which the loyalties of the court and the Church were consumed; and [[anton-denikin]], the staff general, who traces how the revolution and the army's collapse overthrew a throne already fatally compromised.

## The unprepared heir

Witte is explicit that Nicholas II came to the throne unprepared. As a boy he had been the carefree tsesarevich whom the great prince dragged about by the ears at the Kiev station; Alexander III, never expecting to die so soon, had put off the political education of a son he still thought too young. When the father died in the autumn of 1894 the young emperor, as Witte writes, "by nature a very kind man, especially toward his family," mounted the throne without the schooling his role demanded. The first years of the reign are told by Witte in a long series of episodes — the coronation, the Khodynka catastrophe, the negotiations with Li Hung-chang and the treaty with China, the seizure of Liaodong, the visit of Franz Joseph, Wilhelm II, and President Faure to Petersburg, the illness of the sovereign. Witte's running complaint is of the influence of an irresponsible circle around the throne and the court's constant undermining of the ministries.

## Khodynka and the Far East

The coronation of 1896 was shadowed by the disaster of Khodynka, the field outside Moscow where a vast crowd gathered for the promised distribution of gifts and, in the press that followed, some two thousand people were killed and maimed. Witte, arriving at the field, found the traces of the catastrophe already smoothed away as if nothing had happened, the bodies removed and the festivities proceeding — a memory that fixed for many the callousness of the new reign. In foreign affairs the reign opened the era of Far Eastern adventure: the treaty with China and the Chinese Eastern Railway, the occupation of Port Arthur and Dalny, and the growing influence of the "Bezobrazov clique," the adventurers around the throne who pushed a reckless policy in Korea and Manchuria. Witte, who had made the loan and the railway his own, found himself forced out of the finance ministry in 1903, as the war party gained the upper hand — and it was this course that carried Russia into the [[russo-japanese-war-1904-05]].

## The war, Bloody Sunday, and 1905

The war with Japan ended in defeat and revolution. From Witte's account the paralysis of the government ran through every stage: the pressure of Trepov and the great duke on the cabinet, the police despotism of Plehve and then the "spring" of Svyatopolk-Mirsky, the ukase of 12 December 1904, and the catastrophe of 9 January 1905 — Bloody Sunday, when the workers' procession before the Winter Palace was fired upon and the image of the tsar was broken in the eyes of his people. Tsushima completed the military disaster. The revolution of 1905 — the [[revolution-1905]] — forced the emperor to his greatest concession: on 17 October 1905 he issued the manifesto granting civil liberty, a legislative [[duma-constitutionalism]], and responsible government, with [[sergei-witte]] as the first premier. Witte's memoirs record with bitter irony how the court later whispered that he had "wrung" the manifesto from an unwilling sovereign. The revolution was repressed, the Fundamental Laws of 1906 framed, and the state entered its brief constitutional experiment under [[pyotr-stolypin]].

## Stolypin and the retracted constitution

Under Stolypin the emperor presided over the [[duma-constitutionalism]] of the Third Duma and the land reform, but also over the hangings and the coup of 3 June 1907, by which the electoral law was changed to produce a Duma "not chosen but picked" for the government. Witte, dismissed after his half-year premiership, watched Stolypin's regime with a contempt he did not conceal. The murder of Stolypin in Kiev in 1911 removed the one strong minister, and Nicholas II fell back into a governing style that Witte, from a distance, described as surrounded by mediocrities and self-interested courtiers. The last years before the war saw the rise of [[grigori-rasputin]], the starets whose hold over the empress [[alexandra-feodorovna]] and, through her, over the sovereign, scandalized Petersburg and corroded the dynasty's prestige.

## The Great War and the collapse

The war of 1914 brought the ruin. In August 1915, under the influence of the empress's circle and Rasputin, Nicholas II took upon himself the supreme command — a step Denikin describes as taken against the fruitless representations of eight ministers, uniting in his person the headship of the state and the conduct of the war in a moment of retreat. The [[total-war-economy]] strained food, industry, and transport; the empress and the court sank into the Rasputin scandal; the Duma's opposition, voiced by Miliukov's speech of 1 November 1916, demanded a responsible ministry. Zhevakhov, at the Synod through the last months, watched the authority of the crown drain away. When the February revolution broke out in Petrograd in March 1917, the sovereign at Mogilev — in Denikin's phrase "alone, without family, without intimates" — found no one able or willing to save the throne, and on the advice of the generals and the Provisional Government he abdicated. The [[february-revolution-1917]] thus closed the Romanov autocracy. The emperor and his family were held by the Provisional Government and then by the Bolsheviks, and in 1918, as the civil war mounted, they were shot at Ekaterinburg.
