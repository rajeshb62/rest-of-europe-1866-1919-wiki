# István (Stephen) Tisza

Count István (Stephen) Tisza (1861–1918), son of the long-serving minister-president Kálmán Tisza, was the foremost champion of Magyar supremacy and of the Compromise of 1867 in the last generation of the Dual Monarchy. He was Hungarian minister-president from 1903 to 1905 and again from 1913 to 1917, and as wartime premier he carried the kingdom through the first three years of the Great War by what Redlich calls his "dictatorial will and his courage." He embodied in the highest degree the passionate, domineering temper of the Hungarian political nation of which Sosnosky writes — a statesman who defended the dualist tie to Austria against the independence party while pursuing with relentless rigour the Magyar national state over the subject peoples. He was assassinated in October 1918, at the moment of the monarchy's dissolution.

## The champion of the Compromise

Tisza's father, Kálmán Tisza, had been a member of the committee that drafted the Compromise act of 1867 and had argued in its deliberations that under the Pragmatic Sanction only a common defence, not a common army, was necessary — a view against which Deák and Andrássy contended. The son inherited the father's lifelong defence of the dualist settlement, but against a more dangerous opposition. By the opening of the twentieth century the independence movement, drawing on the Kossuth tradition and the cry of *Los von Österreich* ("Away from Austria!"), was pressing for a personal union in which Hungary would share nothing with Austria but the ruler. Sosnosky describes how, in the struggle over the army, the common force was the key point of the Austrian position and its last bulwark, and how the Magyar attacks upon it, now in "wild assault, now through sly enveloping manoeuvres," had already gained footholds and even breached the wall.

## The crisis of 1903–1904

The struggle over the army reached its first crisis under Tisza's own premiership in 1903–1904. The Hungarian parliament, through obstructionist tactics, withheld the annual recruits' quota, and the military leadership had to keep the third year-class under the colours and call up the younger reserve class-years to cover the shortfall — a measure that struck the conscripts and their families bitterly. Tisza found himself attacked not only from Vienna and by the obstruction but also by members of his own party, whom the fear of unpopularity, as much as blindness to his real aims, turned against him. One of the most eminent Liberals, Count Albert Apponyi, broke with him for personal reasons, visible in repeated fierce verbal duels between the two statesmen in the session of 23 February 1904. Tisza did not flinch: he threatened to show the "strong hand" attributed to him and to propose an alteration of the parliamentary rules of procedure that would defeat obstruction. The obstruction, seeing its popularity ebb as public resentment at the resulting *ex-lex* state grew, drew back and on 10 March 1904 permitted the normal recruits' quota for the current and the past year — a truce that closed the first and longest act of what Sosnosky calls the sensational drama of the "defence bills."

## The Magyar national state

It was in this second premiership that Tisza's policy toward the nationalities of the Hungarian kingdom found its most ruthless expression. Redlich, surveying the last peace government before the war, writes that under Tisza's regime, "the last Hungarian minister-president appointed by Kaiser Franz Joseph, in whom the shortsighted and embittered national hatred of the Magyar noble against the Slav and Romance peoples of the Hungarian state territory once more, before the catastrophe, embodied itself in the most ruthless way," the misfortune for the dynastic Reich idea in the south and south-west of the Hungarian state developed irresistibly. In Croatia the long struggle took a dangerous turn under the administration of Khuen-Héderváry and worsened under Tisza; in Transylvania the Romanian intelligentsia, solidly at one with the Romanian peasant masses, had learned to regard the complete political helotisation of their people as its permanent fate so long as the supremacy of the Magyars created in 1867 was not broken. This, Redlich notes, could be undone only by an unsuccessful war or by the dynasty thrusting back the Magyar preponderance from within — the second possibility which the death of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo seemed to remove forever.

## Wartime premier

In the war Tisza's supremacy was, in Redlich's judgement, the decisive fact of the Austrian as much as the Hungarian war government. He refused from the very first day of mobilisation to recognise the Vienna war-surveillance office (*Kriegsüberwachungsamt*) as competent for Hungary, excluding the kingdom completely from its operation, while still building his own collaboration between the civil authorities and the military commands against the "unreliable" populations — the Serbs, even the Croats, the Ruthenians, Slovenes and Romanians. He was resolved, Redlich writes, "to conduct the inner war policy that he found necessary in Hungary with all his force, but without the least interference from Austrian factors, and strictly in the sense of the Magyar interests." The Austrian minister-president Count Stürgkh was, we are told, "from the first a great admirer of the political genius and the force of Count Stephen Tisza," and the subordination of Austria to the Hungarian government which Redlich describes as a cardinal feature of the war government was in large measure the work of Tisza's will. Even the war economy bore the mark of the dualism: Hungary's complete economic isolation of Austria in the matter of food exports was one of the great difficulties of the Austrian administration.

## Assassination and fall

Tisza's regime, like the monarchy itself, did not survive the fourth year of the war. Redlich notes his retirement was connected with the accession of Emperor Charles and the movement toward a political (rather than merely bureaucratic) war government, the attempt at reconciliation and the amnesty that so embittered German-Austrian opinion. In October 1918, as the monarchy dissolved into its national states, Tisza — the man who had embodied Magyar supremacy to the last — was assassinated, at the very moment when the Magyar domination he had defended was collapsing with the Dual Monarchy he had championed.
