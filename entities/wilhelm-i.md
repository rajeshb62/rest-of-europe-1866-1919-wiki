# Wilhelm I (1797–1888)

Wilhelm I, King of Prussia from 1861 and German Emperor from 1871, was the sovereign through whose reign Prussia overthrew Austria, defeated France, and founded the German Empire. In this corpus's frame his story is that of the army monarch — the man who, as Prince Regent from 1858, made the reorganization of the Prussian army the central question of his reign, who stood through the constitutional conflict of the 1860s, and who lived to wear the imperial crown he had helped to win. The contrast with his grandson [[wilhelm-ii]] runs beneath the whole later part of the story: Wilhelm I was the monarch who took Bismarck's hand in his hour of greatest need and kept it to the end, whereas the young Kaiser of 1890 let the chancellor fall.

## The army and the throne

Wilhelm was never guided by military expediency alone. As Lenz's biography makes plain, he was thoroughly convinced that the army — "the creation of his ancestors" — must rest in the hand of the ruler and stand at his absolute disposal; only so, he believed, was the freedom, that is the sovereignty, of the crown guaranteed. This conviction was, Lenz says, the very basis on which the idea of the reform he always rightly called his own had grown in him. When he took up the regency in 1858, he entrusted the execution of this program to [[albrecht-von-roon]], who in July of that year submitted to him a memorandum on the army constitution that opened his way into the ministry. Roon was "a man after Wilhelm's heart," obedient while the regent steered a liberal course, but readier still to hold him upright in conflict — a wedge, from the first hour, driven into the liberal ministry.

The reform itself was a sharp reorientation of the Prussian military system. Roon pressed for a reduction of the militia-like *Landwehr* in favour of a standing professional army, precisely because, as he argued in his first memorandum, the Landwehr made one not master in one's own house: at every conflict of opinion between government and governed, one would have to reckon with the effect of contested measures on the armed part of the people, binding the government's hands and exposing it to the wind of public opinion. The reorganization that resulted was, as Lenz notes, carried through wholly in the interest of the old military families and the Junker class: the Landwehr officers and the bourgeois officer candidates were pushed back, and the upper classes of the cadet houses were emptied to secure the new posts for the sons of military families. Crown and nobility, drawn together again by the shock of revolution, stood joined in the defence of the throne.

## The constitutional conflict

The reform required money, and the liberal Chamber of Deputies refused it. There followed the constitutional conflict of the early 1860s, in which the crown governed without a lawful budget while the parliament withheld its approval. It was the supreme test of Wilhelm's conception of kingship. Lenz's account of the crisis of June 1861 shows how Roon, seeking to strengthen his isolated position in the cabinet, persuaded the king to look about for new ministers and wrote to [[otto-von-bismarck]] at Petersburg. The occasion was the question whether the new king should mark his accession by an homage in the old *ständische* forms, as his brother had done in 1840 — in a constitutional state a palpable anomaly, and an affront the conservatives offered their constitutional opponents. The liberal ministers resisted, and the crisis for a time ended in compromise: the homage was given up for a solemn coronation, and the liberals kept their places.

But the conflict could not be staved off forever. In September 1862, in the extremity of the confrontation over the military budget, Wilhelm took the decisive step that Lenz marks as the turning of his reign: he reached out and took Bismarck's hand, because Bismarck alone of all his servants promised him help in the matter most sacred to his life. As Lenz puts it, looking back from the fall of 1890, "King Wilhelm then seized Bismarck's hand, because he alone of all his servants promised him help in the dearest concern of his life, and it was done for his salvation." Bismarck governed for years without the approval of the Chamber, holding the military question firm, breaking with parliament, and dissolving it — the very program he had sketched in his letters from Petersburg.

## The wars of unification

The constitutional conflict was resolved, not in the Chamber, but on the battlefield. With Bismarck as minister-president and the reorganized army under the command of [[helmuth-von-moltke]], the chief of the General Staff, Wilhelm carried Prussia through the wars that unified Germany. In 1866, against Austria, the King — then seventy years old — rode with his troops into Bohemia. Sybel's account of Königgrätz shows him rising at five in the morning of 3 July after three hours of rest, driving from Gitschin in an open carriage, and arriving to the jubilant hurrahs of his soldiers on the heights opposite Sadowa shortly before half past seven. The victory of [[battle-of-koniggratz-1866]] threw Austria out of Germany and made possible the [[north-german-confederation]]; the Peace of Prague of 23 August 1866 dissolved the old Confederation and left Prussia dominant north of the Main.

Four years later the confrontation with France decided the fate of the German question. At Ems in July 1870, while the King was taking the waters, the French ambassador Benedetti pressed him to guarantee that no Hohenzollern would ever again seek the Spanish throne. Wilhelm's courteous refusal was the kernel of the [[ems-dispatch-1870]] that Bismarck's editing turned into a provocation; France, stung, declared the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]]. Wilhelm commanded in the field, was present at the great victories, and — in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles on 18 January 1871 — was proclaimed German Emperor, the *Kaiserproklamation* by which the German princes placed the crown of the new Reich upon his head. Lenz describes how the crown of Germany, for all its brilliance, did not destroy the Hohenzollern crown but raised it to new meaning: the gold that gave truth to the new glory was still the old genuine gold of the Prussian crown, and the power of Prussia and the peculiar character of its monarchy were preserved.

## The old Kaiser

The last years of Wilhelm's reign were those of the consolidation of the Empire. His will, with that of his chancellor, was, as Lenz records in the Kaiserproclamation and the opening of the first German Reichstag, "to build out in peace what had been won." He survived two attempts on his life in 1878, and the second of them — Nobiling's assault in June — was the occasion Bismarck seized to dissolve the Reichstag and break the power of the National Liberals. Lenz notes that the Crown Prince, who took up the regency for the wounded sovereign and who still sympathised with the liberals, could not oppose measures meant to shield his father and the throne from murder and overthrow; and the rapid recovery of the Kaiser destroyed whatever hopes the liberals might have drawn from the bloody interlude.

Wilhelm I died in 1888, the year of the three Kaisers, leaving the throne in quick succession to his son Friedrich III and then to his grandson [[wilhelm-ii]]. The contrast between the two sovereigns frames the ending of Bismarck's story: the old king, who in the deepest need had taken Bismarck's hand and kept faith with him to the last, was succeeded by a young emperor who feared no conflict, hoped rather through a policy of conciliation and social reform to win back the masses hostile to the state, and in 1890 dismissed the man who had made his empire. Lenz, writing of that fall, sets the grandfather's salvation against the grandson's rupture: "King Wilhelm then seized Bismarck's hand ... and it was done for his salvation. His grandson, however, feared no conflict at all."
