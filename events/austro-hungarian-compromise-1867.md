# The Austro-Hungarian Compromise (1867)

**13 February–12 June 1867**

The Compromise (*Ausgleich* in German, *kiegyezés* in Hungarian) of 1867 re-founded the Habsburg lands as the Dual Monarchy of Austria-Hungary, establishing the constitutional relation between the Hungarian kingdom and the Austrian half of the empire that governed the monarchy until 1918. The settlement, negotiated in the months after the defeat of 1866 and associated above all with the name of Ferenc Deák, rested on the Act XII of 1867 and restored Hungary's statehood while joining it to Austria in a personal union for the common defence and the common affairs. It is the constitutional foundation of the whole 1866–1919 period of this wiki, and the frame within which the era's Austrian and Hungarian history — the nationality conflicts, the rise of [[dualism-austria-hungary|dualism]], and the politics of [[balkan-policy-austria|the East]] — unfolded.

## Background: Hungary's statehood and the Pragmatic Sanction

The Compromise was the product of a centuries-long constitutional relation, and its defenders grounded it in the history of Hungarian statehood. As Beksics set out in his juridical defence of the dualism, the Hungarian nation was always one with its king: the nation was the source, the royal power the stream that flowed from it, and in Hungary not only political power but every right had the nation itself for its source. The German doctrine of the state, which set up kingship as an independent, primordial source of right, exerted, he held, a harmful reaction on Hungarian public-law science, for the Hungarian kingship never possessed such powers.

The constitutional tie that bound Hungary to Austria was the Pragmatic Sanction of 1723, by which the succession of the House of Habsburg and the inseparable and indivisible possession of the kingdoms was settled, and on which the whole subsequent relation of Hungary to Austria rested. The settlement of 1848 had established a responsible Hungarian ministry and ordered the common affairs between Hungary and Austria; but the failure of 1849 brought the *provisorium*, the period of provisional and absolutist administration that followed, and left Hungary without the restoration of its constitutional life until the negotiations that issued in 1867.

## The making of the Compromise

The negotiations that produced the Compromise, carried through after the catastrophe of 1866, issued in the constitutional work associated with the name of Ferenc Deák. Beksics traced the origin of the dualism — how the dualist system came into being, the negotiations and the constitutional work that issued in the Compromise, and the sense in which the settlement preserved, and did not impair, the statehood of the Hungarian state. The central instrument was Act XII of 1867, whose public-law meaning became the subject of the endless disputes that Beksics wrote to settle: the claim, then beginning to be taught in the schools, that between Hungary and Austria there is a real union, and that Hungary's statehood has been impaired by the dualist system, he declared to be a misunderstanding of an act whose meaning, rightly read, preserved the national sovereignty.

Deák's achievement, in Beksics's framing, was to bridge the gulf between the king and the nation. He defended the settlement as the form given to a centuries-long historical development: the dualism had existed before 1848, the common affairs and their management had existed, and through all the great changes — Hungary freed from the Turkish yoke, triumphant over the attempts to absorb it, its two great wars of liberty — only one thing had proved firm and immovable: the tie that binds Hungary to Austria through the dynasty, and its consequence, the union of the two mutually independent states for mutual defence and for that alone.

## The constitutional arrangements

The Compromise created two independent states joined in a personal union — the tie binding them being the identity of the ruler — with a set of common affairs managed in common. Beksics examined the several parts of the arrangement in detail: the conduct of foreign affairs under the dualist system and Hungary's standing as an international personality; the common military establishment and the army, and alongside it the Hungarian *honvéd* (home defence) force and the extraordinary levy; the financial and economic autonomy of the Hungarian state and the division of fiscal and economic matters between the two states; and the national royal household of the Hungarian kingdom.

The common affairs were managed by the three common ministries — of foreign affairs, of war, and of finance for the common expenditures — and by the Delegations, the joint parliamentary bodies by which the common affairs of the two states were conducted. Beksics devoted a chapter to the Delegations, their powers and their proceedings, and compared the handling of the common affairs in 1848 and in 1867. He also corrected what he called the erroneous public-law designations that distorted the true character of the dualist relation, insisting that the union was a personal union of the two crowns — a union of the two mutually independent states for mutual defence and for that alone.

## Croatia and the nationalities

The internal bearings of the dualism reached to Croatia and the nationalities. The Hungarian-Croatian settlement, the *nagodba* of 1868, defined Croatia-Slavonia's relation to the Hungarian kingdom, and the nationalities within Hungary found their place under the dualist state. Sosnosky, writing from the German-national vantage of the eve of 1914, treated the same ground more critically: he described the Magyar conception of the national state, the pan-Magyarism and the Magyarization of the subject peoples — the Germans, Romanians, Slovaks, Ruthenians and Serbs — and the struggle over the common army, its language of command, and the Hungarian military demands that repeatedly provoked crisis. The Compromise, in his view, had created a system whose national frictions were among the deepest malady of the monarchy.

## The future of the dualism

The Compromise, Beksics concluded, could not be destroyed by the weak breezes of internal quarrels, not because it was intellectually perfect but because it answered the interests of both Hungary and Austria, not because it was a new creation but because it gave form to a centuries-long historical development. Only in the single case of the cessation of the identity of the dynasty would new arrangements follow, an eventuality he placed entirely outside the range of political possibility. But the German-national reading of the eve of the war saw the matter otherwise: Sosnosky surveyed the possible futures of the monarchy — a revision of the dualism, the greater-Hungarian solution, [[trialism|trialism]] with the South Slavs, or the federal "Greater Austria" of Aurel Popovici — and argued that the monarchy's future lay in an inner reform that should reconcile the peoples with the state, the watchword being unity through multiplicity, *viribus unitis* ("with united forces"). The debate between the Hungarian defence of the [[ausgleich-1867|Ausgleich]] and the German demand for reform defined the constitutional politics of the Dual Monarchy down to its end.
