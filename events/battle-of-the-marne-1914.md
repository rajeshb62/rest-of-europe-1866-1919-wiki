# The Battle of the Marne (6–12 September 1914)

**6–12 September 1914**

The battle on the river Marne, east of Paris, in which the French and British armies stopped the German wheel on the capital and forced the German retreat, ending the German hope of a short war. Recouly calls it the decisive turn: "the skill and sang-froid of the French generalissimo, and the mistakes that excess of confidence made the German generalissimo commit, turned the situation; the battle of the Marne was lost, and at a stroke the German machine was half-dismantled — the war that was to have been short instead embarked on a long duration." Conrad, from the other side, judged that the defeat on the Marne was the signal for Italy, Rumania and the other opponents of Germany and Austria-Hungary to seek to join the Entente, and thereby an overwhelming superiority came to the enemy side.

## Background

The battle grew out of the [[war-plans-1914]] and the opening campaign. The German plan — the Schlieffen plan — had flung the mass of the German army at the French left, through Belgium, intending a great enveloping battle (*Vernichtungsschlacht*, the battle of annihilation) against the principal adversary while the East was held defensively. The first battles of the frontiers (August 1914) seemed to give the Germans reason for the confidence that had brought them to the edge of Paris. But the German command, in the person of the younger Moltke, was already in difficulty. Recouly describes a command that was "much less a principle of direction than a resultant": Moltke, directed more than directing, let his army commanders — above all the Crown Prince of Bavaria, "the most egoistical, the most demanding" of them — press their own local triumphs at the expense of the plan of the whole. After the frontier victories the German leaders shouted cries of triumph, mistaking their adversaries for far more beaten than they were.

## The German march on Paris

The German right wing, under von Kluck and von Bülow, pursued the retreating French and British toward Paris. Recouly stresses how Moltke, believing wrongly that the main mass of the French armies was massed on his right, shifted the balance of his forces — drawing divisions toward the East and toward the secondary theatre of East Prussia, which his plan had always meant to sacrifice to the principal one. Meanwhile, at the German headquarters, a more precise and reflective vision of the facts succeeded the first exaltation: on 27 August Moltke wrote to his armies that one could not yet say what capacity of resistance the Franco-British army possessed. Kluck and Bülow, set in a temporary and badly borne subordination of one to the other, engaged in frictions and clashes; certain decisions of Bülow, disapproved by Kluck, were executed only grudgingly and with delay. The left wheel that should have closed on Paris instead left a gap between the two armies toward which the Allied counterstroke was directed.

## The decision of the French command

On the French side it was [[joseph-joffre]], the generalissimo, who turned the retreat into the counter-offensive. Recouly recounts the decisive days: Joffre, decided to risk all though fully conscious of the enormity of the risk, drove to the château of Vaux-le-Pénil near Melun for a moving interview with the British commander-in-chief, who finally promised his cooperation; Franchet d'Espérey, "a magnificent soldier," declared himself ready to march; and the last hesitations lifted, Joffre ordered the great battle engaged for 6 September and signed for his troops his "immortal order of the day." Foch, commanding on the left, was among the principal executants, and it was his maxim — *« Une bataille perdue est une bataille qu'on croit perdue »* — that Recouly cites to capture the psychological factor that carried the day. The French reserves that had been drawn toward the East were the very forces whose absence, Recouly notes, might have changed the result had they been present behind the German right wing during the battle.

## The battle and its consequences

The battle, fought from 6 to 12 September, was a confused and chaotic struggle along the Marne — Recouly compares following it in detail to the hero of *La Chartreuse de Parme* wandering blind across the plain of Waterloo. Its great lines were simple: the German right wing, checked and threatened on its exposed flank, drew back; the whole German army fell back from the Marne, and the attempt to reach Paris had failed. The consequences were immense. The war that was to have been decided in a few weeks on the western front instead settled into the long struggle; the two sides raced to the sea and entrenched in the trenches that would hold for four years. Conrad's retrospective gives the strategic meaning of the Marne in the whole war: the decision lay in the failure of the plan agreed between Germany and Austria-Hungary for the decisive first action, and the defeat on the Marne was the signal for Italy and Rumania to seek the Entente. The short war had failed, and the war of attrition had begun.
