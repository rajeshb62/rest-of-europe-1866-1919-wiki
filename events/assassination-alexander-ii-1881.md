# Assassination of Alexander II (1881)

**1 March 1881**

## Background

The [[russo-turkish-war-1877-78|Russo-Turkish war]] and the humiliation of the
[[congress-of-berlin-1878|Berlin Congress]] deepened the estrangement between the government of
[[alexander-ii]] and Russian society, and gave fresh life to a revolutionary movement that the
reaction of the 1860s and 1870s had only strengthened. From the disillusionment of the war the party
of the [[narodnaya-volya|People's Will]] took shape, finally organizing itself after the congresses
at Lipetsk and Voronezh. Its terrorist attacks were directed above all at the person of the tsar.
Against [[alexander-ii]] this small but extraordinarily energetic organization of revolutionary
terrorists carried on what Kornilov calls a systematic siege — a whole network of mines, ambushes,
and the like, in which the revolutionaries did not stop before digging tunnels, blowing up bridges,
trains, and houses in the cities, even though carrying these attempts out threatened the lives of
many dozens of people. Nothing stopped them; they sacrificed their own lives without the slightest
hesitation.

On one side stood revolutionaries convinced in their blindness that a successful attempt on the
tsar's life would serve as the signal for a popular revolution that would radically change all the
conditions of political and social life in Russia — and no arguments of reason could shake them. On
the other stood a government at a loss, unable to find any point of support in a population whose
needs and rights it was not accustomed to consider, convulsively adopting various repressive
measures that only sharpened the life-and-death struggle and placed peaceful citizens in an
intolerable position, under martial law and the arbitrary will of coarse and unceremonious satraps
and police agents.

## From Solovyov to the Winter Palace

After Solovyov's attempt on the emperor's life in 1879 and a series of other attempts, it became
clear to everyone that the government could not fight the revolutionaries by repressive measures
alone, and could not restore peace and order. At the same time the government saw that its
self-confident appeals to society for assistance could lead to nothing, so long as it was not
disposed to reckon with the interests and rights of that society. Doubts about the correctness of
the established policy began to appear even among some members of the government.

In the Kharkov governor-general, Loris-Melikov, who had been made one of the temporary
governors-general after Solovyov's attempt, there appeared an experiment in a somewhat modified
policy: he tried to restrain the arbitrariness of the administration subordinate to him and to win
the sympathy and respect of the local population by a solicitous protection of their interests,
while continuing to persecute the revolutionaries energetically. In parallel, the chairman of the
committee of ministers, Count Valuev, began cautiously to suggest to the emperor the necessity of
reconciling the government with society, drawing from the archives his own project of 1863 for
giving representatives of society a part in state affairs. Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolaevich,
chairman of the State Council, likewise recalled a project composed by Prince Urusov in 1866 tending
toward some embryo of a consultative representative institution.

While these projects were being discussed, the government continued to rule by means of harsh
punishments and executions, and the arbitrariness that provoked universal irritation continued and
grew. The revolutionaries organized an explosion in the Winter Palace itself — in the tsar's own
dwelling. The explosion occurred at the very moment when the whole imperial family was preparing to
take part in the gala dinner given in honor of the arrival in Petersburg of the Bulgarian prince
Alexander Battenberg. It took place on 4 February 1880 and failed of its aim only through purely
accidental circumstances. The revolutionaries had succeeded in bringing a sufficient quantity of
dynamite into the Winter Palace, storing it over the course of some months in the basement, inside a
stove that stood directly under the imperial dining room — a fact established from a plan of the
palace that they had procured in time. The only thing they could not see from the plan was that
between the basement and the dining room there was a mezzanine chamber, in which at the moment of
the explosion were the guards, several dozen of whom became its victims. The blast did not reach the
floor of the dining room with sufficient force. Moreover the imperial family had not yet sat down at
table, because the train bringing Alexander Battenberg happened to be late, and the dinner was to
take place half an hour later.

Had it succeeded, not only the emperor but the whole imperial family would have perished, and with
them all the lawful heirs — a catastrophe that might have raised the question, quite unexpectedly
for the country, either of electing a new dynasty or, as the revolutionaries dreamed, of completely
changing the form of government. The culprit was a young worker, Khalturin, who had lodgings in the
palace as a cleaner, lived there for six months, brought in the dynamite that Zhelyabov supplied to
him, and quietly waited for the right moment, postponing the explosion until he had in his hands
exact data that it would achieve its full purpose. The investigation revealed that this was no
accidental penetration: the revolutionaries had made a firm nest inside the palace.

It was a complete fiasco for the police system of protection. A few weeks before the explosion a
plan of the Winter Palace, marked with a cross at the place of the explosion, had been found in the
papers of a revolutionary at a search — yet the police, which made peaceful Petersburgers' lives
impossible and imposed obligatory doorkeeper duties on the population at a cost of up to a million
rubles a year, paid no attention to so simple a fact.

## The Supreme Administrative Commission

The fiasco revealed to the emperor himself the impossibility and extreme danger of the existing
position, and there occurred in the highest spheres a decisive turn in views on the tasks of
internal policy. The tsesarevich, Alexander Alexandrovich — the future [[alexander-iii]] — had
already shown at the conference held before the explosion that he was not at all on the side of
Valuev, and for the first time displayed clearly those political views he would profess throughout
his reign, declaring himself decisively against all constitutional plans. After the explosion, at a
new conference of 8 February 1880, the heir himself came forward with a definite plan: to establish
a supreme investigating commission on the model of those of 1862 and 1866, but with broader powers
covering the whole of Russia.

[[alexander-ii]] at first received the proposal negatively, but, persuaded by the insistent heir,
agreed. He declared that he considered exceptional measures unavoidable and would create a special
temporary dictatorial institution, the "Supreme Administrative Commission," with extraordinary
powers chiefly for the struggle against sedition, but also charged with considering the intolerable
situation and finding a way out of it. At its head the emperor placed General Loris-Melikov — the
one governor-general who, together with considerable energy against the revolutionaries, had shown
the ability to attract the sympathy of ordinary people. The commission was to be obeyed by all
administrative authorities, even the ministers, including the war minister, who had previously been
Loris-Melikov's direct superior.

Immediately upon taking office on 14 February 1880, Loris-Melikov published a proclamation to the
inhabitants of Petersburg, declaring that while firmly seeking the eradication of criminals who
tried to shake the state order, he wished to calm and protect the lawful interests of the
well-disposed part of society. "On the support of society," he wrote, "I look as on the principal
force that can aid the government in restoring the regular course of state life, an interruption of
which most threatens the interests of society itself."

Kornilov notes that those who knew Loris-Melikov — Dr. Belogolovy, who talked with him after he had
left office, and A. I. Koshelev in his memoirs — describe him as a sincerely liberal, thoroughly
honest man, inclined to meet the loyal social movement halfway and to satisfy the just demands of
the zemstvos and of liberal-minded people, up to granting society some participation in state
affairs. It is beyond doubt, however, that the basic tasks of his commission were above all
repressive: it was established before everything else for the struggle with the revolutionaries, and
on that struggle Loris-Melikov was prepared to concentrate all his forces. He was not, as has been
unfairly supposed, a constitutionalist; his outlook remained that of an administrator who saw his
police tasks with unusual breadth.

Under Loris-Melikov the reactionary Count D. A. Tolstoy resigned, senatorial inspections were begun,
the peasant question was taken up, and the constitutional movement gathered force. His programme
culminated in a report to the emperor proposing the establishment of a special preparatory
commission — the first step toward drawing elected representatives of society into legislation.

## 1 March 1881

The execution of the plan was prepared with care. The bombs were made by the talented technician
Kibalchich. When at the end of February Zhelyabov and Trigoni were arrested, Sofia Perovskaya, left
at liberty, judged it necessary not merely to continue the attempt but to bring it to completion as
quickly as possible, foreseeing that with Zhelyabov's arrest the whole existing revolutionary
organization threatened to collapse.

On 1 March, Alexander II, not heeding Loris-Melikov's warnings — who had already twice held him back
from his Sunday outing, sensing that something was being prepared — set out for the parade of
troops, and afterwards went to the Mikhailovsky Palace (where the Alexander III museum now stands)
to see Grand Duchess Ekaterina Mikhailovna, and from there meant to return to the Winter Palace. The
revolutionaries under Perovskaya showed such energy that, not knowing exactly which way the emperor
would go, they maneuvered their bombs through the streets, and Perovskaya repeatedly shifted the
bomb-carriers so that they would certainly fall on the sovereign's route. The circumstances of the
catastrophe are generally known. On that Sunday the bombs struck, and [[alexander-ii]] was killed.

## Consequences: the end of reform, the onset of reaction

The catastrophe was a fatal outcome for the whole system of measures projected by Loris-Melikov. The
emperor who mounted the throne, [[alexander-iii]], had shown, at the conferences preceding the
Winter Palace explosion, that he promised to be a sovereign of no liberal inclination. So it turned
out in the sequel; but the first steps of his reign were subject to great vacillations, and the
course that finally established itself under him in the 1880s was not set at once. Two tendencies
contended in the highest governing circles. At the conference of 8 March 1881 the fate of
Loris-Melikov's constitutional project was decided: under the influence of
[[konstantin-pobedonostsev]], who agitated against the constitutional plans as ruinous, and with the
support of the publicists Katkov and Aksakov in a conservative vein, the project was rejected. The
manifesto of 29 April 1881 proclaimed the unshakable [[autocracy-russia|autocracy]] in terms that
left no room for a representative assembly.

Loris-Melikov and some other ministers resigned, and the ministry of N. P. Ignatiev followed — a
government that promised measures to improve the economic condition of the people, including
compulsory redemption and a reduction of redemption payments, under the finance minister Bunge, who
abolished the poll tax. But the liberal experiment was short-lived, and the reign settled into
reaction: the reactionary Count D. A. Tolstoy returned to power in the Ministry of the Interior, a
new press law of 1882 muzzled the press, the law of 12 July 1889 on land captains (земские
начальники) placed the peasantry under the control of noble officials, and the statute on zemstvo
institutions of 12 June 1890 subordinated the [[zemstvo-great-reforms|zemstvos]] to the nobility and
the government.

The death of [[alexander-ii]] thus closed the era of reform in Russia. The
[[zemstvo-great-reforms|Great Reforms]] were not overturned, but their further development was
arrested, and the hope of drawing society into government perished with the tsar. The
[[narodnaya-volya|People's Will]] had destroyed the one figure who had begun, at the end of his
life, to move toward reconciliation — and in so doing delivered Russia to the reaction it had meant
to overturn. Among contemporaries the shock was general. S. Yu. Witte, then a young railway official
in Kiev, heard the news in the theatre and wrote to his uncle, General Fadeev, comparing the "steam
hammer" of the workshops with the powerlessness of the state against anarchists; he joined the "Holy
Brotherhood," a private society organized after the assassination to defend the autocracy.

## Key participants

- [[alexander-ii]] — Emperor of Russia, assassinated on 1 March 1881.
- [[alexander-iii]] — Tsesarevich who pressed for the exceptional commission and then, as emperor, rejected constitutional reform.
- [[narodnaya-volya|People's Will (Narodnaya Volya)]] — the terrorist party; Zhelyabov, Perovskaya, Kibalchich, Khalturin, Trigoni.
- Loris-Melikov — head of the Supreme Administrative Commission, author of the abortive reform programme.
- [[konstantin-pobedonostsev]] — the tutor and adviser who urged the new emperor toward reaction.
