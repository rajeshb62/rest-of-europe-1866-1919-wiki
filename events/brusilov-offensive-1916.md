# The Brusilov Offensive (4 June – September 1916)

**4 June – September 1916**

The great Russian offensive in Galicia in the summer of 1916, launched by the Russian general Brusilov, which broke through the Austro-Hungarian front, captured hundreds of thousands of prisoners, and reduced the Austro-Hungarian army to dependence on its German ally. Recouly calls it "the last flaring-up of the energy, of the military force of the Russians": after it the Russian front was to fall asleep, awaiting its collapse. In this corpus's frame the offensive is the decisive demonstration both of Russia's power and of the fundamental weakness of the [[dualism-austria-hungary|Austro-Hungarian army]] that the war had exposed.

## Background

The offensive of 1916 grew out of the Allied plan concerted at [[joseph-joffre|Joffre's]] headquarters, which bound the Allies to attack the Central Powers as soon as they were ready. The Austro-Hungarian army had opened a great offensive against Italy in May 1916 — the attack between the Val Sugana and the Lake of Garda, on a front of about forty kilometres, which drove about ten kilometres into the Italian line before the Italians, clinging to the last mountain spurs, made their situation critical. The Italian high command therefore asked to be relieved by a Russian offensive. It was planned by the inter-Allied plan; the Russian attack, begun on 4 June in Galicia, preceded it by some ten days.

## The offensive

Recouly's account is compact and vivid. Brusilov — "the most enterprising, the most mordant of their generals" — launched on 4 June in Galicia four armies that struck against four Austro-Hungarian armies and one German army. The offensive developed with full success through June and July: the Russians captured more than 200,000 prisoners and hundreds of cannons. The zone of attack, between the Pripet and the Rumanian frontier, presented a development of some 500 kilometres. The Russian successes were especially marked on the two wings, where the advance quickly passed fifty kilometres: in the south the battle of Czernowitz won the Bukovina, and in the north the battle of Lutsk carried the Russians through Volhynia, though a German counter-attack halted their advance on that side. Toward the south, on the Austro-Hungarian frontier, the progress continued.

## The consequences for Austria-Hungary

The strategic meaning of the offensive lay in what it did to the Austro-Hungarian army. Recouly frames the whole eastern war with the judgment that the German army, even with relatively reduced forces, almost always beat the Russian army, which was clearly inferior in high command, manœuvre, instruction and material — but that the Russian army, in return, "almost always beats, without any difficulty, the Austro-Hungarian army," over which it affirmed its superiority from the very beginning. The reason, Recouly insists, was national: "only a vigourously organised nation, perfectly united and coherent, can have an excellent army"; Austria was "an empire made of distinct races, of heterogeneous peoples," not properly a nation. The summer of 1916 brought this weakness to its crisis. The Austrians in peril resigned themselves to accept, or rather to suffer, the unity of command: from that point Hindenburg alone was master over the whole extent of the eastern front, and the Austro-Hungarian army passed into dependence on the German. The unexpected Russian successes, which put Austria in danger again, provoked among the Allies the happiest surprise.

## The exhaustion of Russia

Yet the offensive, for all its success, was also the summit of Russian power. Recouly marks it as "the last flaring-up of the energy, of the military force of the Russians." The enormous losses of the campaign, the strain of a war of three years, and the failing cohesion of the empire's own institutions left the Russian army exhausted after the summer of 1916; the front was to fall asleep in expectation of its collapse. The offensive thus looks two ways in the story of the war: it wrecked the Austro-Hungarian army and brought the Central Powers to the edge, but it also spent the last great reserve of Russian strength, preparing the collapse that the revolutions of 1917 would complete (see [[february-revolution-1917]] and [[october-revolution-1917]]). With the Somme on the western front and the Italian relief, the summer of 1916 was, for Recouly, the moment of the Allied "redressement" — yet the relief of pressure on Italy and the ruin of Austria-Hungary were bought at the price of the Russian army that had delivered the blow.
