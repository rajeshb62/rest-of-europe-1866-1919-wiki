# The Austro-Prussian War (June–August 1866)

**June 14 – August 23, 1866**

## Background

The Seven Weeks' War grew out of a quarrel that the great powers of the German Confederation had
been nursing ever since they had jointly torn the Elbe Duchies from Denmark in 1864. The joint
administration of Schleswig and Holstein became a running wound between the two German great powers,
and Heinrich von Sybel's account of the years 1865–1866 describes the mounting *Spannung zwischen
Österreich und Preußen* — the tension between Austria and Prussia — as it rose almost to the point
of rupture. Each court suspected the other of seeking to turn the settlement of the duchies to its
own advantage, and the occupation of the conquered provinces, meant to be temporary, hardened into a
permanent grievance.

In August 1865 the two governments patched together the *Gasteiner Konvention* (Gastein Convention),
a temporary expedient that partitioned the administration of the duchies without settling their
final fate. Holstein went under Austrian administration and Schleswig under Prussian, in a division
that satisfied nobody. The arrangement was received without enthusiasm by the German states and the
European powers alike, and Sybel treats it as precisely what it proved to be: a stopgap that served
only to postpone the inevitable clash, while the underlying rivalry remained unresolved.

## The Break and the Arming

The rupture came more quickly than most observers expected. Both sides set about preparing for war
"on all sides," as Sybel titles one of his chapters, and the winter and spring of 1866 were given
over to mobilisation schedules, the concentration of troops, and the financial measures that a great
war required. Prussia, under King Wilhelm I and his minister-president Otto von Bismarck, and
Austria, under the Emperor Franz Joseph, each calculated that the German question could be settled
only by arms.

A last attempt at accommodation was nevertheless made in the spring of 1866. Napoleon III, the
French emperor, proposed a European congress to settle the German question peaceably, hoping to turn
the Austro-Prussian rivalry to France's advantage. At the same time the Gablenz mission ran a final
errand of Austro-Prussian reconciliation. All of it came to nothing. Behind the scenes Vienna
entered into secret negotiations with Paris, sounding the French for neutrality or support in
exchange for territorial compensation, while Berlin looked in the opposite direction, toward Italy.

## The Prussian-Italian Alliance

The decisive diplomatic stroke was Prussia's. Through the spring of 1866, Bismarck negotiated an
offensive alliance with the Kingdom of Italy, concluded in April 1866. The terms bound Italy to
enter a war against Austria on Prussia's side, with Venetia as the prize — the province that Italy
expected as its reward for joining the struggle against Vienna. For the Italians, who had long
coveted the Venetian lands still held by the Habsburgs, the offer was compelling enough to draw them
into a war whose other stakes were, to them, remote. The alliance gave Prussia a second front and
forced Austria to divide its strength between Bohemia and Italy, and it meant that the Austro-
Prussian struggle would be fought on both land and sea.

## Opening Moves and the End of the Confederation

Hostilities opened in June 1866 with Prussia's invasion of the territories of the German
Confederation. The Prussian armies overran Hanover, Saxony, and Electoral Hesse in short order; the
Hanoverian army, cornered at Langensalza on 27 June 1866, fought with courage but was compelled to
capitulate, and the Kingdom of Hanover passed effectively under Prussian control. These swift
campaigns in north Germany, which Sybel describes under the heading *Unterwerfung Norddeutschlands*
("the subjugation of North Germany"), dismantled the Confederation piecemeal and demonstrated that
the medium German states could not stand alone against Prussian arms. One after another they were
overrun, occupied, or compelled to change sides, and with them the old federal structure collapsed.

## The Two Theatres of War

Austria's main force, the Northern Army under Feldzeugmeister Ludwig von Benedek, concentrated at
Olmütz and faced the principal Prussian invasion through Bohemia. There the war was decided. The
Prussians advanced in three separate armies — the Elbe, First, and Second Armies under Herwarth von
Bittenfeld, Prince Friedrich Karl, and Crown Prince Friedrich Wilhelm — and Helmut von Moltke's plan
was to concentrate them on the battlefield. After the preliminary engagements of Gitschin (Jičín)
and Münchengrätz, the three columns converged for the climactic Battle of Königgrätz on 3 July 1866,
a decisive Prussian victory that shattered the Austrian army in a single afternoon.

The southern front went differently, and the Italian ally fared badly. At the Battle of Custoza on
24 June 1866 the Austrians under Archduke Albrecht defeated the Italians, and on 20 July the
Austrian fleet under Admiral Wilhelm von Tegetthoff routed the Italians off Lissa. Italy
nevertheless reaped the political prize: with Prussia's victories, it occupied Venetia "without a
struggle," as Sybel put it, crossing the provincial frontiers once Austria was beaten in the north.
Italian greed for Welschtirol (Trentino) and irritation at the five-day armistice Bismarck had
concluded without Italian consent drew only Bismarck's dry retort that if Prussian troops rested
five days, they did no more than the Italian army had done for weeks after Custoza.

## Consequences

The war was decided at Königgrätz and in the negotiations that followed. After the armistice, the
preliminaries were signed at Nikolsburg, and the definitive Peace of Prague on 23 August 1866 ended
the conflict. The German Confederation was dissolved, Austria was excluded from Germany, and Prussia
annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, and the free city of Frankfurt. The foundations of the new
Reich were laid in the North German Confederation, bringing the German question to its Kleindeutsch
resolution under Prussian hegemony and setting the stage for the war with France four years later.
The Austro-Prussian War, fought in a matter of weeks, had remade the map of central Europe.
