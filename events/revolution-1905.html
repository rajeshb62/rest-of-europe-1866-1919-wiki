Russian Revolution of 1905

1905

Background

The revolution of 1905 was the explosion of long-accumulated social and political tensions under the impact of the disastrous war with Japan. In S. Yu. Witte’s analysis, the rule of nicholas-ii through a “court-noble camarilla” had denied the people the economic and social reforms they demanded; and when the government systematically refuses such demands, he argued, the people come to the conviction that their wishes cannot be satisfied under the existing regime, their economic and social demands are pushed aside, and political demands ripen as the means of obtaining transformation. If the government does not wisely regulate this current, and especially if it commits some madness such as the Japanese war, revolution breaks out. The war proved that madness.

The ground had also been prepared by the police-created workers’ organizations introduced by Interior Minister Plehve and known as “zubatovshchina,” in which the priest Gapon was invited to act as leader of the workers. Witte had predicted that these organizations, designed to keep the workers in police hands, would in time shake off their police direction and take up, in one degree or another, socialist principles of struggle with capital — not only by peaceful means but by force. When in 1903 the vague revolutionary ferment began, all the workers’ organizations absorbed the revolutionary spirit, and the wave of assassinations that struck the ministers of the reaction — Bogolepov in 1901, Sipyagin in 1902, Plehve himself in 1904 — signaled the collapse of the police system.

The “spring” of Svyatopolk-Mirsky and the ukase of 12 December 1904

After the murder of Plehve, the new minister of internal affairs, Prince Svyatopolk-Mirsky, attempted a policy of conciliation — a “spring” of good will toward society. His course culminated in the ukase of 12 December 1904, which promised various measures toward the “establishment of legality.” But the concessions came too slowly and too late to satisfy the demand for a representative assembly, and the defeats of the war continued to inflame opinion. The government, Witte recalled, was caught between reaction and concession, issuing measures that were at once “the most retrograde and cruel, and at the same time madly liberal by their untimeliness.” The emperor and the empress, Witte noted, believed that the whole people stood behind the autocracy and only the “intelligentsia” against it — a conviction that led the government to appeal over the head of educated society to the peasantry.

Bloody Sunday (9 January 1905)

The crisis of the old order came on 9 January 1905. The immediate occasion was a traditional ceremony: on 6 January, at the Epiphany procession when the emperor stood with the clergy and brilliant suite at the blessing of the water, one of the cannon firing from the Peter and Paul Fortress across the Neva turned out to be loaded with a live, if very antiquated, charge. The shot could have caused a great catastrophe had it reached the pavilion, but the investigation showed a simple blunder and a mere accident; nicholas-ii treated the men who had made the mistake with great kindness. Yet in many strata of society the incident was treated as an attempt on the tsar’s life or at least on his peace.

Less than three days later came the well-known procession of workers under the leadership of the priest Gapon. The workers prepared a petition to the emperor, containing demands touching their existence — one-sided, exaggerated, not without a certain coloring of revolutionism, though written in a fairly seemly form. Gapon was to lead many thousands of workers to the Palace Square to bow before the emperor; they imagined that they would see his majesty, hand him their request, and quietly depart. The police chief, General Fullon, who had trusted Gapon entirely, treated the movement as nothing serious.

On the evening of 8 January the government met to decide what to do. Witte, as chairman of the Committee of Ministers, was deliberately not invited, at the request of Kokovtsov, so that he would not support the workers’ interests. Instead a delegation came to him — including the honorary academician Arsenyev, the writer Annensky, and Maxim Gorky — begging him to take measures so that the emperor should appear before the workers and receive their petition, or else bloodshed would follow. Witte, whose office gave him no authority in the matter, declined to intervene; the delegation left dissatisfied.

On the morning of 9 January Witte saw from his balcony a great crowd of workers with banners, icons, and flags — many women and children among them, and many curious onlookers — moving toward the Trinity Bridge. As he watched, a shot was fired, several bullets flew past, and then a systematic series of shots followed. Within ten minutes a large crowd streamed back along the Kamennoostrovsky Prospekt, many carrying wounded and dead, adults and children. The decision taken on the evening of the 8th had been that the demonstrating workers should be allowed up to certain limits near the Palace Square but not onto the square itself. When the workers approached, troops met them and demanded they go no further or turn back, warning that otherwise they would be fired on. The workers, not believing they would be shot, did not withdraw; everywhere volleys followed, and, as Witte recalled, more than two hundred people were killed and wounded.

The event, he wrote, was the first blood shed in considerable quantity, and it seemed to inaugurate the broad current of what was called the Russian revolution of 1905. It was used by those who led the unrest to inflame the people further; exaggerated reports that thousands had been killed spread over the whole of Russia. Even among prudent people the affair made a very bad impression, and it produced confusion not only in society but in the ranks of the government itself, which criticized the weak-kneed minister Svyatopolk-Mirsky. Gapon disappeared and fled. “Bloody Sunday,” as it came to be called, marked the irreparable breach between the autocracy and the people it had once commanded.

From Bloody Sunday to the Bulygin Duma

Through the spring and summer of 1905 the unrest widened into strikes, agrarian disorders, mutinies in the fleet, and assassinations of officials. The government, attempting to answer the demand for representation, issued in February 1905 a manifesto on disorders and a ukase to Bulygin “on drawing in the best people,” and on 6 August published a law on a consultative Duma — the Bulygin Duma — based, on the insistence of the empress, the tutor Pobedonostsev, and State Controller Chobko, on giving the decisive voice to the peasantry, whom the court believed loyal to the throne. The electoral law rested on the belief that the Duma should be predominantly a peasant assembly, and that this was the historical foundation of conservatism. The law satisfied no one, and the failure of the war, crowned by Tsushima, and the continued denial of real reform drove the movement on. Witte’s verdict on the peasantry’s supposed conservatism was blunt: the first two Dumas returned “the most extreme left elements and a mass of revolutionaries.”

The general strike and the soviets

In October the unrest became a national general strike. On 8 October 1905 the movement on the railways converging on Moscow ceased; on the 10th the Kharkov railway junction stopped, on the 12th the Petersburg junction, and in the following days movement on all the other railways, with the telegraph, died away. By 17 October almost the whole railway network was at a standstill. Nearly all the factories and mills of the large industrial centers had stopped work; in Petersburg the factories began to strike on 12 October, and by the 15th the business life of the capital had entirely ceased.

It was in this atmosphere that the Soviet of Workers’ Deputies (Совет рабочих депутатов) arose in Petersburg. The idea of instituting it took shape at the beginning of October and was propagated by the press. Its first meeting was held on 13 October at the Technological Institute, which issued an appeal to the workers calling for a strike and for extreme political demands; a second meeting followed on the 14th, at which the chairman elected was Nossar (Khroustalev), an assistant of a sworn attorney of Jewish origin who had entered a factory as a weaver for the purpose of propaganda. Almost all the Petersburg workers began to obey the Soviet’s decisions unquestioningly. At its meeting of 17 October, held in the hall of the Free Economic Society, the number of members already exceeded two hundred, and an executive committee of thirty was elected; on the same day the Soviet’s newspaper, the Izvestiya of the Soviet of Workers’ Deputies, a purely revolutionary organ, began to be printed in the various printing-houses. The Soviet’s control over the printers, Witte noted, placed the daily press in a certain dependence upon it, so that the newspapers hung on the will of the workers even for their very appearance.

The Manifesto of 17 October

Under the pressure of the general strike, and after long hesitation, nicholas-ii on 17 October 1905 issued the Manifesto “On the improvement of the state order” — the October Manifesto — granting civil liberties and promising a legislative Duma elected by a widened franchise, and appointing Witte chairman of the Council of Ministers. Witte’s account is explicit that the manifesto was the work of the sovereign, pressed upon him by events; Witte himself had urged the safer course of a rescript setting out a programme rather than a manifesto read aloud in all the churches, precisely so that responsibility would fall on his ministry and not bind the emperor.

The manifesto, however, did not immediately quiet the country. On 18 October the Soviet of Workers’ Deputies met and resolved to propose a general strike, since the manifesto did not satisfy the working masses. Yet by 19 October the strike in Moscow and elsewhere began to subside and the railways resumed movement, and the Soviet itself resolved on the same day to end the strike from 21 October. In the days after the manifesto there were clashes on the streets between revolutionaries, troops, police, and counter-revolutionaries, in which several people were killed and, among others, the Petersburg professor Tarle was wounded in the head near the Technological Institute. The government permitted calm processions in honor of 17 October but suppressed any that aimed at disorder, and within a few days quiet was restored in the capital.

Consequences

The revolution of 1905 forced the transformation of the autocracy into a constitutional monarchy on paper. The Manifesto of 17 October, the Fundamental Laws of 1906, and the convening of the State Duma were the institutional fruits of the upheaval. The government of Witte lasted only about half a year as a “cabinet”; he resigned in April 1906, to be succeeded by the ministries of Goremykin and then Stolypin. The regime survived the immediate crisis through a combination of concession and repression — Witte described the empire as descending into “complete confusion,” with punitive expeditions against the disorders — and through the great foreign loan of 1906, which liquidated the war expenditures and stabilized the shaken finances.

But the revolution was not wholly suppressed. Witte’s bitter retrospective judgment was that the opportunity was thrown away: had the emperor made the peasant and other reforms himself after Portsmouth, there would have been no 17 October; and because, after the revolution was put down, the government “played to the right and to the left,” the country slid toward anarchy. The first experiment in constitutionalism, the First and Second Dumas, ended in the “state coup” of 3 June 1907, which altered the electoral law to secure a conservative majority. The revolution of 1905 established the soviets as a form, foreshadowed the revolution of 1917, and left unresolved the very conflicts — over land, labor, nationality, and political power — that had produced it.

Key participants

  • nicholas-ii — Emperor, who conceded the October Manifesto and appointed Witte.
  • sergei-witte — Chairman of the Council of Ministers after the manifesto; author of the programme of concession.
  • Father Gapon — priest who led the 9 January procession.
  • Svyatopolk-Mirsky — liberalizing interior minister discredited by Bloody Sunday.
  • Nossar (Khroustalev) — chairman of the Petersburg Soviet of Workers’ Deputies.
