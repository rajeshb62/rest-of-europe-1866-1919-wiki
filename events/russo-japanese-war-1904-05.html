Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905)

1904–1905

Background

The war with Japan grew out of the Far Eastern policy pursued by Russia in the reign of nicholas-ii. The impulse that drove Russia into the Pacific, S. Yu. Witte later traced to a fatal divergence. As finance minister, Witte had concluded the treaty with China, secured a loan guarantee, and built up the Russo-Chinese Bank and the Chinese Eastern Railway as the instruments of a Russian sphere in northern China and Manchuria. The object of the new course was an outlet to the open sea in the Far East — a scheme that Witte connected with the late Alexander III’s attraction to the Russian North and his own advocacy of a naval base on the Murman coast, a plan Nicholas II failed to adopt. In 1897 the government seized the Liaodong Peninsula with its naval base of Port Arthur and the adjoining Dalny. From that step, in Witte’s retrospective judgment, everything followed: “if we had not sought an outlet to the open sea in the Far East,” he wrote, “there would have been no such ill-fated step as the seizure of Port Arthur, and then, as we went ever downward, step by step, we should not have reached Tsushima.”

The conduct of the policy fell increasingly into the hands of an adventurer group around Bezobrazov, and into conflict with the finance ministry that had built the railways and banks of the new sphere. Witte, who pressed the view that Russia’s strength lay in the peaceful economic development of the Far East and in agreement with Japan, found himself opposed by a court circle that sought expansion on the cheap. In 1903 the influence of the Bezobrazov clique grew, and Witte, who opposed its rash course, resigned as finance minister, remaining as chairman of the Committee of Ministers — a change of position he came to regard as the moment when the emperor passed into the hands of the adventurers. War Minister Kuropatkin remained in the government, and the negotiations with Japan over Korea and Manchuria were conducted through the viceroy of the Far East, Alekseev, and dragged on in a fashion that exasperated the Japanese government.

The road to war

Witte’s memoirs preserve the immediacy of the slide into conflict. The Japanese envoy in Petersburg, Kurino — a clever man whose whole aim as envoy was that there should be no war — warned Witte that the negotiations were being conducted so that Russia evidently wanted war. Japan gave answers immediately, Russia only after weeks or months. Count Lamsdorf, the foreign minister, referred questions to Alekseev; Alekseev and Rosen referred them to the emperor, who was away. In July 1903, a month before Witte’s resignation from the finance ministry, Kurino presented to him and to Lamsdorf a draft agreement between Japan and Russia on Far Eastern affairs which, had it been accepted, would have removed the resolution of the Far Eastern question by means of war. Witte found the agreement perfectly acceptable and insisted on it, but his insistence came to nothing: the draft was sent to the viceroy Alekseev for his opinion and there it stuck. Endless negotiations dragged on from July to January and ended in nothing.

In mid-January, at a court ball, Kurino approached Witte to warn him that he should urge the foreign ministry to give a speedier answer to Japan’s last declaration; that a whole week had passed without a reply, the negotiations being evidently deliberately slowed down; and that Japan had been driven beyond patience — if no answer were given within a few days, war would break out. No timely answer was given. On 26 January Japanese ships attacked the Russian squadron near Port Arthur and sank several of the vessels; on 27 January the manifesto of war followed. The next day there was a solemn thanksgiving service in the Winter Palace, and Witte notes the gloomy, oppressive mood. When the emperor passed General Bogdanovich, who shouted “hurrah,” only a few voices joined in. The war, Witte insists, was extremely unpopular from the first; the people did not want it, and most cursed it. The administration organized a series of street demonstrations, but they met with no sympathy whatever; from this alone, he wrote, no good results from the war could be expected.

Character of the war

Witte’s account is a portrait of miscalculation at the top. Interior Minister Plehve, when Kuropatkin reproached him as the only minister who had wanted the war and had joined the band of political adventurers, answered: “Alexei Nikolaevich, you do not know the internal position of Russia. To hold back the revolution we need a little victorious war.” Witte comments acidly on this “state wisdom and perspicacity.” nicholas-ii himself, he says, was deeply convinced that Japan, though perhaps with some effort, would be beaten to pieces, and that there was nothing to fear over money, since Japan would repay everything by means of an indemnity. In his early resolutions the emperor’s usual expression for the Japanese was “these macaques,” a name taken up by the so-called patriotic newspapers maintained on public money.

The command was as ill-conceived as the policy. The commander-in-chief was first the viceroy Alekseev, who had never been a soldier, had had nothing to do with land forces, and had made his naval career more by his diplomacy than by naval service. He was later withdrawn from Mukden and replaced by Kuropatkin. The war was, in Witte’s verdict, “the unhappiest of unhappy wars,” and its natural sequel was the revolution long prepared by the police-noble regime. He pitied the tsar, “a good and not unintelligent man, but without will,” on whose weakness of character developed his vices as an autocratic and unlimited ruler.

The war was fought at sea and on land across Manchuria and the approaches to Port Arthur, and step by step the Russian position collapsed. The seizure of Port Arthur proved to be the fateful step that dragged Russia from one defeat to the next, until the fleet was lost at Tsushima — the disaster that gave its name to the chapter of Witte’s memoirs on the oscillation of opinion. The defeats at the front ran parallel with the growth of domestic unrest, and the longer the war continued the less confidence the government commanded. A reserve army was raised and held under arms, and the prolonged employment of troops in police service at home “in considerable degree demoralized” the forces that remained inside the empire — an element of the coming collapse.

Tsushima and the approach of peace

Witte frames the defeats as both military and political. The revolutionary unrest gathered strength as the war went badly, and the reverses in the Far East, by exposing the weakness of the autocracy, fed the demand for internal change. By the summer of 1905 Russia, beaten at sea and failing on land, and menaced even at home, had reached a point where continuation of the war threatened the loss of Vladivostok, Kamchatka, and the adjacent islands, mastery over which had passed wholly into the hands of the Japanese.

The disaster of Tsushima, and the impossibility of retrieving the position, brought the government to seek peace. President Theodore Roosevelt of the United States offered his mediation, and Witte was dispatched to America to conduct the negotiations at Portsmouth. His mission succeeded where the war had failed: the peace concluded at Portsmouth — in Witte’s phrase, “a peaceful victory” — stopped the senseless and unsuccessful bloodshed with relatively insignificant sacrifices, saving Vladivostok and Kamchatka and ending a war that threatened to crown Russia’s misfortunes with the loss of its remaining Far Eastern possessions. The immediate cost was high: the lease of Port Arthur and the Liaodong Peninsula passed to Japan, together with the railway line of South Manchuria, and the position in Korea was given over to Japanese preponderance. Yet the peace restored at least the outward integrity of the empire, and the public, under the impression of this act of “belated state wisdom,” breathed easier; the name of the man who had won an external peace naturally came to mind for the task of internal pacification. For his services Witte was raised to the rank of count.

Consequences

The Portsmouth peace came too late to save the government at home. The revolution of 1905 had already broken out, and the war had been one of its principal causes: it had drained the treasury, discredited the autocracy, and left a reserve army whose men, held under arms after the peace, began to ferment against an order that kept them from home. The failures of the war discredited the old ministerial system and opened the way for the internal transformation of the empire — the concessions that culminated in the Manifesto of 17 October 1905 and the institution of the Duma.

In Witte’s retrospective judgment the war was the great “madness” that, on top of the refusal of necessary reform, precipitated revolution. Had the emperor himself, after the Portsmouth peace, on his own initiative carried out a broad peasant reform in the spirit of Alexander II and granted the long-overdue freedoms — had he, for example, freed the Old Believers from their restraints and stood on the principle of religious toleration and the removal of unjust restrictions on the non-Russian peoples — there would have been no need of 17 October. The Russian Revolution, he wrote, was “long prepared by the police-noble regime”; the war brought it to the surface.

Key participants

  • nicholas-ii — Emperor of Russia, who carried the responsibility for the Far Eastern policy and the conduct of the war.
  • sergei-witte — Finance minister who opposed the adventurist course, then as envoy concluded the Portsmouth peace.
  • Kuropatkin — War minister, then commander-in-chief in the field.
  • Alekseev — Viceroy of the Far East, first commander-in-chief.
  • Plehve — Interior minister, advocate of the “little victorious war.”
  • Theodore Roosevelt — American president, mediator at Portsmouth.
