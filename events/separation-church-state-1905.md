# Separation of Church and State (1905)

**9 December 1905**

The Separation of Church and State, enacted by the French law of 9 December 1905, ended the Concordat regime that had bound the Catholic Church to the state since Napoleon and set the churches free to organise themselves outside the public power. It was the culmination of the anticlerical programme of the Third Republic and the event around which Paul Sabatier's *L'orientation religieuse de la France actuelle* (1911) is organised — the moment at which the religious question in France was posed anew.

## Background

The Separation closed a long republican struggle. Since Gambetta's cry that clericalism was "the enemy" and the Ferry school laws of the 1880s, the Republic had progressively reduced the Church's hold on public life — the closing of the congregational schools, the expulsion of the religious orders, the secularisation of the state school. The laic programme, as Sabatier stresses, was never a mere anticlerical negation: "by the laic school our country has sought to conquer itself," a new incarnation of a secular religious effort. The Separation of 1905 carried this programme to its logical conclusion, ending the concordat and the state's endowment and regulation of the clergy, and declaring the Republic neutral in matters of religion.

## Narrative

The law of 9 December 1905 separated the churches from the state. It abrogated the Concordat and the other religious laws of the old regime; the state would no longer recognise, endow, or regulate any religion. The churches were to organise their own affairs through the *associations cultuelles* — the "worship associations" that the law provided for the administration of religious property and worship. The Catholic Church refused, for a time, to accept the associations, and the new regime was contested and difficult; Sabatier records with a certain irony the "Cultuelles, attempted toward 1905," the efforts to found new religious institutions under the law, born with the favour of the ministry and of the great newspapers.

The Separation was not, in Sabatier's analysis, a mere political decree. It belonged to a wider religious re-orientation of France, a movement that the war of 1870, the laic school, and the crisis of the Church had shaped. He distinguishes free thought from anticlericalism and warns against confounding the two: the Republic's laicism, in the better sense, was an effort to give the citizen a moral formation rather than a war upon religion. The event of 1905, in this reading, ended the political entanglement of the Concordat and opened a new epoch in which religion would be a free matter of conscience.

## Consequences

The Separation completed the laic Republic and marked the definitive end of the Concordat regime. It was the culmination of the anticlerical program that ran from the Ferry school laws and article 7 through the decrees of 1880, and it stood as the settled achievement of the republican governments that followed the Dreyfus affair. For Sabatier, writing six years later, the result was not a decline of religion but a re-orientation: the "rapid progress of indifference and an unexpected awakening of religious aspirations," often in one and the same individual, an epoch "of reconstitution, one might say of religious revenge, somewhat analogous to the origins of Christianity." The Church, though stripped of its political power, retained "a unique, incomparable place" in the conscience of the coming generation.

For Péguy, writing in 1910, the religious question had a more sombre reading. The de-republicanisation of France and its de-Christianisation were one and the same movement of "demystification" — the modern world that believes in nothing and "appears to thrive against all culture." The Separation, in his eyes, belonged to that movement which dried up equally the political and the Christian city. Yet even Péguy hoped that the republican and Christian mystiques would flower again together, in a single deep movement, in the generation to come.
