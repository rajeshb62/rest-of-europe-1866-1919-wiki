# The Kornilov Affair (1917)

**27 – 31 August 1917**

The attempt of the Supreme Commander-in-Chief [[lavr-kornilov]] to impose order on a disintegrating Russia by a march of loyal troops on Petrograd at the end of August 1917, and its sudden collapse in the space of a few days — the episode Kerensky branded a "mutiny," and which Denikin preferred to call, for 27–31 August, the *kornilovskoe vystuplenie* ("Kornilov rising"), reserving the wider name *kornilovskoe dvizhenie* ("Kornilov movement") for the whole of the summer's gathering of the officer corps against the revolutionary disorder. It was the decisive rupture between the Provisional Government and the command, and its failure cleared the road to the [[october-revolution-1917]].

## Background: the divergence of paths

By the summer of 1917 the revolution had split into a many-sided struggle. Denikin generalised the forces of the revolution into two resultants — the Provisional Government and the Soviet — but by July and August this simple division no longer held. After the July days and the crisis of power, Kerensky formed a third government, formally emancipated from the soviets; but power itself was slipping. The poles of the struggle were three: the government, the central executive committee of the Soviet, and the supreme command.

The June offensive had failed, and the army was dissolving under the pressure of the soldiers' committees and the reforms of the revolutionary command. The officer corps, humiliated and disarmed, had moved into opposition to a power that could neither win the war nor restore the front. Denikin insisted that in the struggle between Kerensky and Kornilov there was an "absence of direct political and social slogans" that would divide the two sides; the conflict was not one of programs but of wills and of the desperate question of who should save the country. He stressed that the terms later used — "affair," "rising," "conspiracy," "mutiny" — were all too narrow, and that the events were far more complex than a mere plot, embracing wide circles of the Russian public.

## The Supreme Commander

On 19 July [[lavr-kornilov]] was appointed Supreme Commander-in-Chief, and at his appointment he demanded of the government recognition of his responsibility "only before his own conscience and the whole people" — an extraordinary, almost sovereign scheme of military command. He saw in dictatorship the only way out of the situation created by the spiritual and political prostration of the power. Denikin insisted that Kornilov had no definite political program before the rising; the "Kornilov program" circulated under that name was, in his view, the fruit of later collective work. But the movement gathered the secret organizations of the officers, the "political encirclement," and a good part of the Russian public — a "three-sided conspiracy," Denikin called it, between the command, the organized right, and the Allied representatives, all pressing toward a "strong power."

Through August Kornilov pressed the "memorandum" on army reorganization — the restoration of the disciplinary authority of the officers, the subordination of the committees — while Kerensky wavered, now against the soviets with Kornilov, now with the soviets against Kornilov. The agent and intermediary between them was Savinkov, the former terrorist who served the government, weighing on both sides. Krymov, the general whose troops stood nearest the capital, had no doubt that force of arms would settle the matter; Kornilov himself, Denikin records, had decided on a change in the structure of power, though not yet on the methods.

## The rupture and the march on Petrograd

The explosion came through the mission of V. N. Lvov, a former member of the Duma and of the Provisional Government, who carried to Petrograd Kornilov's demands for the transfer of full military and civil power, on which he would form a new government. Kerensky, whatever the real tenor of the message, chose to read it as a demand for the overthrow of the government and declared the Supreme Commander a traitor. His communique began: "On 26 August General Kornilov sent to me the member of the State Duma V. N. Lvov with the demand that the Provisional Government transfer to him the whole of military and civil power."

Through the 27th, amid a fever of appeals, the government and the revolutionary organizations branded the movement "mutiny," "treason to the fatherland and to the revolution," "baring of the front," and set about stopping the advance of the troops General Krymov had brought toward the capital. Denikin catalogued the outrage of the appeals, above all the socialist manifesto of Chernov that invited the "peasants in grey soldiers' greatcoats" to remember "the accursed name of the man" who wished to strangle freedom and take from them the land — a mobilisation of the rank and file against their own commanders. In all these appeals, Denikin noted, there was no true, factual and legal basis; they reflected rather the choleric temper of their authors. The government also acted: Kerensky ordered first Lukomsky and then Klembovsky to assume the supreme command, and both refused. The new military governor of Petrograd, meanwhile, and the committees of the railway junctions worked to halt Krymov's echelons.

## The collapse

The rising foundered without a battle. Krymov's troops, halted by the railway workers and turned back by the committees and the soviets' agitation, refused to march; the officer corps, though sympathetic, could not overcome the general disintegration. Kerensky ordered first Lukomsky and then Klembovsky to assume the supreme command, and both refused. Stavka was liquidated by a detachment sent for the purpose, and Kornilov was arrested and imprisoned in the town of Bykhov together with his chief associates — Denikin among them, sent from the "Berdichev group" of arrested officers. Krymov himself shot himself, unable to bear the disgrace of the episode. Denikin's chapter heading gave the verdict in a phrase: Kerensky's victory was the "prelude to Bolshevism." For by destroying the command and the last instrument of authority, Kerensky had disarmed the only force that might have resisted [[vladimir-lenin]]'s party; the government had won against Kornilov only to fall a few weeks later to the [[october-revolution-1917]].

## Consequences

The Kornilov affair completed the ruin of the army and the prestige of the command. The officers emerged from it humiliated and hunted, the soldiers' committees and the Bolsheviks greatly strengthened, and the authority of Kerensky — who had seemingly defeated his own general — left solitary and dependent on the very soviet democracy he had sought to escape. At Bykhov the prisoners lived under guard, watched by Stavka and the government, while the investigative commission of Shablovsky examined the "Kornilov program" and the plans for the future. Through September and October the empire drifted: Kerensky's military reforms under the war minister Verkhovsky, the German occupation of the Moonsund islands, and the gathering strength of the soviets. When the [[october-revolution-1917]] came, the Bykhov prisoners were set free amid the collapse of the old Stavka, the murder of the last supreme commander Dukhonin, and made their way to the Don, where [[mikhail-alexeyev]] and the volunteers were already gathering. The idea that had moved Kornilov — a military dictatorship to save Russia — did not die at Bykhov; it passed into the [[white-movement]] that would take the field against the Bolsheviks in the following year.
