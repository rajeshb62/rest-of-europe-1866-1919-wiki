# The Luxembourg Crisis (1867)

**January – May 1867**

The abortive attempt of [[napoleon-iii]] to buy the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg from the Netherlands, and the war scare that followed until the London Conference of May 1867 neutralised the duchy and ordered the dismantling of its fortress. It was the first great collision between France and the new [[north-german-confederation]] after the war of 1866, and — like the Hohenzollern candidature three years later — it sprang from the same unsettled question of French compensation on the Rhine.

## Background: France and the compensations of 1866

In the months after the [[austro-prussian-war-1866]] the French Emperor, who had expected to profit from the German war, found himself confronting a Prussia grown strong beyond the Main. Sybel records how Napoleon's diplomacy sought to recover the lost balance by proposing a grand exchange: a famous triple alliance by which France would receive Luxembourg and, further on, Belgium, Prussia the South German states, with Italy joining as a third partner — a scheme by which, in Sybel's words, the glory of the Napoleonic name would be restored without the shedding of blood. The Prussian negotiation over this plan dragged on. In December suspicion seized the Emperor that Prussia would not merely refuse the alliance but confront him as an open adversary over Luxembourg; he ordered his war minister to prepare a reform of the army that would double its strength. In January 1867 the failure of the Prussian plan and of the understanding with Italy was finally clear. "I have no ally in Europe," Napoleon told his ministers; "I can rely only on my own people." His resolve was taken to enter the liberal path — to win the gratitude of the people by free concessions and so to secure the throne and the succession. It was in these very days, as Sybel notes, that the Luxembourg affair entangled itself ever more dangerously.

## The purchase and its denouement

The crisis turned on the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. Though the duchy belonged to the King of the Netherlands, its fortress had been garrisoned since the Congress of 1815 by Prussian troops, as a stronghold of the German Confederation; in 1866 the Confederation had dissolved and the question of the garrison stood open. Napoleon proposed to buy Luxembourg from the King of the Netherlands. For a time his policy found a degree of favour in Berlin, and Bismarck encouraged the purchase — the transaction, from the German standpoint, would detach from Germany a point of which Prussia had no further need. The plan then miscarried. When the sale became known, German national feeling rose against the cession of a German fortress to France, and Bismarck, confronted with the storm, denounced the project he had at first countenanced. The bargain collapsed; the purchase was abandoned; and the two powers stood face to face over a question of honour and security, each convinced the other had sought to humiliate it.

## The London Conference and the neutralisation

The quarrel mounted until the mediation of the powers was invoked, and the crisis was referred to a conference of the European states which met in London in May 1867. There the solution was found in the neutralisation of Luxembourg. By the arrangement the Grand Duchy was placed under the collective guarantee of the powers as a permanently neutral state; the Prussian garrison was to be withdrawn and the fortress, the object of so much contention, was to be dismantled. The conference thus removed the immediate danger of war, but at the cost of satisfying neither party. France gained no territory and no compensation on the Rhine; Germany kept Luxembourg out of French hands, but at the price of abandoning a German fortress that Prussia had garrisoned for half a century. The settlement proved, in the estimate of the French sources of the corpus, a lasting grievance — the Luxembourg affair joined the stock of injuries which France laid to Prussia's account, to be reopened at the crisis of 1870.

## Consequences

The Luxembourg crisis set the pattern of the following years. It taught Napoleon the isolation of France: the failure of the triple-alliance scheme left him without a continental ally, and the events of 1867 confirmed his turn toward internal liberal reform — the beginning of the transformation toward the [[liberal-empire]] which Ollivier records from within. For Prussia the crisis revealed both the strength and the limits of the new [[north-german-confederation]]: German national sentiment had rallied against the cession, but the Bund had not been able to keep the fortress without yielding to a European conference. The affront of the neutralisation rankled in France, where the loss of Luxembourg was felt as a humiliation, and the memory of 1867 fed the bellicose current that in 1870 seized upon the [[hohenzollern-candidature-1870]] and the [[ems-dispatch-1870]] as the occasion for settling the account with Prussia in the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]]. In that sense the Luxembourg crisis was a first rehearsal of the war — the same powers, the same grievance of compensation, the same appeal to a conference — that ended, three years later, very differently.
