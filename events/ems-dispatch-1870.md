# The Ems Dispatch (1870)

**13 July 1870**

The telegram sent by King Wilhelm I of Prussia from the spa town of Ems to his chancellor Bismarck on **13 July 1870**, and — above all — the shortened, sharpened version in which Bismarck published it. In the French reading of the corpus it is the **Emser Depesche** as reworked by Bismarck — the *soufflet*, the "slap in the face" — that transformed a settled incident into an unbearable national insult and precipitated the French declaration of war.

## Background: the King at Ems and the Hohenzollern affair

After the withdrawal of the Hohenzollern candidacy on 12 July (see [[hohenzollern-candidature-1870]]), the French government, at the Emperor's prompting, had decided to demand guarantees for the future. The French ambassador Benedetti was sent to the King at Ems, where Wilhelm was taking the waters, to press the demand. In Ollivier's account the King, a constitutional monarch, would not *order* Prince Leopold's renunciation but had sought to obtain it by hint; the French instructions throughout asked only that the withdrawal be secured. Benedetti handled the negotiation at Ems with flexibility, and the King, though irritated at being pressed on his promenade, remained courteous. The demand of guarantees, however, was the work of the French Right, lacking serious grounds, and Ollivier himself advised confining it to the present rather than hardening it for the future.

## The King's refusal and the report to Berlin

On 13 July, after Benedetti's interview, the King refused to give the guarantees demanded and, in Ollivier's account, had already resolved to receive the French ambassador no more. The King caused two reports of the interview to be drawn up and sent the official account to Berlin through the councillor Abeken, who was with him at Ems. Ollivier insists that it was not the report of the ambassador Werther, and not any genuine French provocation, that set Bismarck to work — it was the poisoned narrative of Abeken's telegram, which he says authorises Bismarck's "perfidious disclosure." Sybel, writing from the German side, gives the same facts in the national frame: the King's correct but firm refusal, and the dispatch of the report to the chancellor.

## Bismarck's reworking of the telegram

Bismarck, who had earlier despaired at the renunciation and even offered the King his resignation, now searched in vain in Werther's report and in the French declaration for a pretext for war, and found it in the telegram from Ems. Ollivier describes how Bismarck **reworked Abeken's dispatch** until the falsified telegram became an unbearable outrage for France — a *soufflet*. The essence of the change was compression: from a fuller account of a polite but firm refusal, Bismarck produced a terse text in which the King was made to declare that he would receive the French ambassador no more and had nothing further to communicate — a form that read as a deliberate rebuff of France. He had the text posted and published. All Germany, in Ollivier's words, treated war as inevitable from that moment.

## The "soufflet" and its effect in Paris

The published dispatch reached Paris and fell upon the French government like a blow. Ollivier, who on the morning of 14 July believed peace secure, learned from Gramont of Bismarck's *soufflet*; a council met at the Tuileries, and the ministers, who had been seeking a pacific issue, were confronted with a public insult that could not be borne. In Ollivier's framing the grievance that finally decided the war was precisely the public outrage of the posted telegram — not Prague, not Luxembourg, not the refusal of future guarantees as such. The demand of guarantees had made war possible; the *soufflet* made it inevitable. On **15 July 1870** Ollivier read the declaration of war to the Chamber of Deputies, and the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]] began.

## The controversy over the 13th

The Ems dispatch is at the heart of the historiographical war that the sources preserve. Ollivier argues that historians who make Bismarck innocent of the war diminish him, and that those who restore his premeditation enlarge him; he cites Lorenz, Delbrück, Scherr and others for the responsibility of Bismarck, and attacks the German thesis that Prussia needed no war because France was herself preparing one. He is at pains to correct what he calls the *erreur des mémoires d'Abeken sur le 13 juillet* — the claim, which he rejects, that Abeken's account of the 13th exculpates the chancellor or changes the sequence of responsibility. For Ollivier, Bismarck alone organised the Hohenzollern candidacy and falsified the Ems dispatch, and without that falsification there would have been no war. Sybel, by contrast, presents the affair within the Prussian national story as the correct response of the King and the necessary concentration of German energies against France. Between these two, the whole question of the war's guilt is fought out.
