# The Siege of Paris (1870–71)

**19 September 1870 – 28 January 1871**

The investment of Paris by the German armies during the autumn and winter of 1870–71, in which the capital of France was cut off, reduced to famine, and finally compelled to capitulate. It was the last act of the war after the catastrophe of [[battle-of-sedan-1870]], and its grim months of hunger and humiliation prepared the bitterness that exploded in the [[paris-commune-1871]] the following spring.

## Background: the war after Sedan

After the fall of the Empire and the proclamation of the [[third-republic-france]] on 4 September 1870, the Government of National Defence resolved to continue the war. Hanotaux records the paradox of that decision: the country, in its patriotic anger at the disaster, refused to accept the loss of territory, yet the army was destroyed and the capital exposed. The German armies, having taken Sedan and forced the capitulation of Metz, closed upon Paris. The investment of the capital began in mid-September 1870, and by the end of the month Paris was surrounded, its fortifications ringed by the besieging armies and its communications with the rest of France cut.

## The famine of the capital

The siege became a contest of endurance. Hanotaux describes how Paris, isolated, "let itself be pushed to famine" — *Paris se laisse acculer à la famine*. The provisioning of a city of two million was impossible over a closed ring; food ran short through the autumn and the winter cold of 1870–71, and the population endured the slow starvation of the capital. Meanwhile the armies of the provinces, animated by the ardor of Gambetta, attempted to break the ring — Hanotaux records the "admirable ardor" that revived courage in the provinces and threw every available force against the blockade, but the armies of the Loire and of the East were successively beaten and driven back. The hope of relief faded as the winter wore on.

## The surrender of Paris

With famine at its last extremity and the provincial relief defeated, Paris was reduced to surrender. Hanotaux gives the summing judgment: *"Paris, enfin, réduit à la famine, doit se rendre. Sa chute est celle de la France"* — Paris, finally reduced to famine, must surrender; its fall is the fall of France. The capitulation of the capital was signed at the end of January 1871, and with it came the armistice that ended the fighting. The siege was the effective conclusion of the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]]: once Paris had fallen, Hanotaux writes, the opinion spread that peace was inevitable, and the country turned from war to the elections and the negotiation of peace.

## Aftermath: capitulation, elections, and the Commune

The surrender of Paris was followed by the national elections of **8 February 1871**, in which the departments, exhausted and desiring peace, returned an Assembly that met at Bordeaux and entrusted the peace to **[[adolphe-thiers]]**. Thiers was at pains, as Hanotaux records, to keep the negotiation of the peace separate from the capitulation of Paris — "It is Paris that is reduced, not France" — the better to save what could be saved. But the capital itself, humiliated by the siege, outraged by the surrender and by the entry of the German armies into a city that had held out so long, and radicalised by the working-class agitation of the war years, would not accept the Assembly's peace. The distrust between armed Paris and the conservative, royalist-leaning Assembly ripened through the spring of 1871 into the insurrection of **18 March** and the [[paris-commune-1871]]. The second siege of Paris that followed — the siege of the Commune by the Versailles army under Mac-Mahon — was fought in the streets the German armies had just abandoned, and the capital that had resisted the invader was taken by its own government in the Bloody Week of May 1871. The four months of the German siege thus fed directly into the civil war that dismembered France after the peace.
