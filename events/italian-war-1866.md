# The Italian War of 1866

**June – August 1866**

The Italian theatre of the third war of independence, fought in alliance with Prussia against Austria in the summer of 1866 — the campaign that closed the [[austro-prussian-war-1866]] on the Italian side. It was a war in which Italy lost every pitched engagement — on land at Custoza and on sea at Lissa — and yet, because the Prussian victory in the north decided the whole struggle, ended by acquiring the Veneto. The contrast between the glory of [[giuseppe-garibaldi]]'s volunteers in the Trentino and the humiliation of the army and fleet left a bitter mark on the new kingdom.

## Background: the Prussian alliance and the quest for the Veneto

Since 1859 the national programme of the *Risorgimento* had held Venice and Rome as the two goals still to be won. The war of 1866 offered the occasion to take the first of them. When Prussia prepared to strike at Austria over the German question, Italy, under the ministry of General Lamarmora, concluded a treaty of alliance with Prussia: the two powers were to make war together, and Italy, in return for joining, was to obtain the Veneto from Austria. Tivaroni's account records the negotiations that attended the treaty — the Austrian diplomacy, even on the eve of war, still probing whether the Veneto could be ceded peacefully; the efforts of the Prussian alliance to gain a better price; and the flat refusal to admit the Trentino into the bargain, Bismarck answering that he could not do it because the Trentino was not his to give. In the end Italy entered the war bound to Prussia, and the fate of the Veneto — as the sequel showed — was to depend less on Italian arms than on Prussian.

## The battle of Custoza

War was declared in June 1866, and the Italian army crossed the Mincio to take the offensive against the Austrians in the Veneto. On **24 June** the two armies met at **Custoza**, on the hilly ground about the Garda. Tivaroni gives a searching account of the battle and its mismanagement. The Italian plan, which looked to roll up the Austrian centre, failed in execution; the divisions were thrown separately upon the enemy, the movements of the several corps were not co-ordinated, and while the Austrian plan under the Archduke Albert did not succeed completely, the Italians were compelled to recross the Mincio with two divisions broken and two more disordered. It was, Tivaroni observes, the first time in fifteen hundred years that the soldiers of all Italy had stood united against a common enemy — and the irony was that their commander, General Lamarmora, did not know how to use them. The defeat was not a rout, but it was a reverse, and its effect on the army and on public spirit was deep.

## Inaction and the Trentino: Garibaldi's volunteers

After Custoza the Italian army stood still. Tivaroni records the long inaction between 25 June and mid-July — the recriminations among the generals, the failure to press the war, the government's growing despair — while the Prussians advanced in the north. In the mountain theatre, however, the war took another shape. The corps of volunteers, some 38,000 nominal under [[giuseppe-garibaldi]], had been posted on the extreme left about the Garda, charged with distracting the Austrians and cutting the line of retreat through the Trentino. Ill-armed and ill-supplied, Garibaldi himself crippled by rheumatism and compelled to move about in a carriage, the volunteers nevertheless carried the war into the mountains, forcing the passes of the Chiese and the Ledro, storming the forts one by one and pressing upon the line toward Trento. At **Bezzecce** they won the victory that, in Tivaroni's words, softened the grief of the nation for the outcome of Custoza and Lissa. The whole Trentino lay occupied, and Trento itself was about to fall, when the armistice arrived: Lamarmora's order to evacuate the Trentino reached Garibaldi on 25 July, and the general, against every entreaty of his men, obeyed it with the single word *obbedisco* — "I obey."

## The battle of Lissa

At sea the war was decided on **20 July**. The Italian fleet, commanded by Admiral Persano, had been built at heavy cost — Tivaroni reckons it at 127 million lire by the end of 1866 — and was the stronger of the two, with more ironclads and more guns than the Austrian fleet under Admiral Tegethoff. Yet the campaign had been one of hesitation: the fleet lay idle at Ancona, refused battle when the Austrians appeared, and finally, urged on by the minister Depretis, sailed not against the enemy fleet but against the island of **Lissa** in the Adriatic. There the Austrian squadron came out to meet it. In the battle that followed, Tegethoff, driving his ships in a compact mass, rammed and sank the ironclad *Re d'Italia* with most of its crew, while the *Palestro* blew up with three hundred men after her commander, Cappellini, refused to abandon her. The Italian fleet, though it remained on the waters after the enemy withdrew, had been defeated; Persano, who had transferred his flag during the action, was afterwards tried before the Senate and condemned for incapacity and negligence. Lissa cost Italy two ships and eight hundred men, and, coming after Custoza, completed the military humiliation of the war.

## The *cadeau du Veneto* and the Peace of Vienna

The decisive events were passing in the north. The Prussian victory over Austria at Königgrätz in July decided the war, and in the armistice of Nikolsburg Austria conceded the German settlement. Italy, which had sought to obtain the Veneto by its own arms and had failed, now received it as a gift. Tivaroni describes the bitter comedy of the *cadeau du Veneto*: the preliminary peace transferred the Veneto to the Emperor [[napoleon-iii]], and France then handed it over to Italy. For the Italian government and army the thing was a humiliation — Lamarmora wrote that to receive the Veneto as a present from France was dishonouring and that all the world would think Italy had betrayed Prussia; the romantic Ricasoli would rather have gone on fighting alone than accept the province as a charity. But to continue the war was impossible: the whole Austrian army of Sadowa was now free to descend upon Italy, and the government, against the advice of Ricasoli, accepted the armistice and evacuated the Trentino. On 12 August the armistice was signed at Cormons; Austria ceded the Veneto to France in the last days of August, and the transfer was completed through Napoleon's commissioner. The **Peace of Vienna**, closing the Italian war, registered the acquisition of the Veneto by the Kingdom of Italy — the completion of the union of the peninsula except for Rome and the irredentist lands of the Trentino and the eastern shore.

## Consequences

Italy emerged from 1866 with the Veneto — the object for which it had entered the war — but without military honour. Custoza and Lissa had shown the army and the fleet unequal to their task, and the province had been won not on the Mincio or the Adriatic but by Prussia at Königgrätz and by the good offices of the French Emperor. The government of Ricasoli, which had so fiercely desired to fight on, was discredited; a rising at Palermo in September 1866 showed the discontent of the south; and the very success of the Veneto purchase left the Roman question untouched, for France still garrisoned Rome. The Trentino, evacuated by order at the very moment of victory, remained in Austrian hands and entered the stock of Italian [[irredentism]] — the claim to the unredeemed lands of the north-east which was to recur again and again down to the Great War. The war of 1866 thus closed the chapter of the Veneto while opening that of Rome, and it is the humiliated rather than the triumphant Italy of 1866 that the sources of the *Risorgimento* carry forward into the struggle for the capital, which ended at [[capture-of-rome-1870]].
