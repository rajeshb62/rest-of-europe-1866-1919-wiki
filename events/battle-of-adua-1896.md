# The Battle of Adua (1 March 1896)

**1 March 1896**

The battle of Adua (Adwa) on 1 March 1896, in which an Italian army was annihilated by the forces of the Ethiopian emperor Menelik II, the greatest disaster inflicted on a European army by an African power in the age of empire. The defeat brought down the government of [[francesco-crispi]], whose African adventure had carried Italy into the Ethiopian highlands, and it turned Italian colonial policy toward the withdrawal and prudence that the leaders of the liberal opposition, above all [[giustino-fortunato]], had long urged. In this corpus the event closes the African chapter of the second monarchical period that Oriani narrates from the conquest of Eritrea, and it marks the great check to Italian expansionism in the years before the [[italy-ww1-entry-1915|entry into the world war]].

## Background

Italy's entry into the African scramble followed hard upon the formation of the kingdom, and by the 1880s the young state had established a colony on the Red Sea coast around Massawa — the nucleus of Eritrea. Oriani, in the *La lotta politica in Italia* chapter on the African conquest, frames the enterprise as the attraction of Italian ambition into the general European movement of imperial expansion. Under [[francesco-crispi]] the colonial project grew more ambitious, seeking to extend Italian dominion from the coast into the highlands of Abyssinia. The first great reverse had come on 24 January 1887, when an Italian column was cut to pieces at Dogali; but the colonial policy pressed on, and in the mid-1890s a larger war broke out with the Ethiopian empire of Menelik II, who had consolidated his power and refused to accept the Italian protectorate that the treaty of Uccialli had seemed to confer.

## The disaster

The decisive campaign of 1896 ended in catastrophe at Adua on 1 March 1896. The Italian commander, marching to force a decision against the Ethiopian host, was overwhelmed: the Italian army was surrounded and destroyed on the field, and the survivors, and the colony itself, fell into the hands of the enemy. Fortunato, writing in the months that followed, evokes the disaster as *l'immane disastro di Adua* — "the immense disaster of Adua" — a blow that had at a stroke made the country, in his words, ready for a great reaction. The scale of the loss was without precedent in the colonial record: thousands of Italian and colonial troops perished in the valley that the survivors, and the mothers of the fallen, would remember as the *triste conca di Adua* — "the sad hollow of Adua." The event struck the country with a shock that resounded through its public life for years, and tablets to the fallen of Adua were later raised, and commemorated, across Italy.

## The fall of Crispi

The political consequence was immediate. The disaster of Adua brought down the government of Francesco Crispi, the statesman whose African policy had staked the prestige of the country upon the enterprise, and who now fell with it. The national reaction against the colonial adventure delivered power to his opponents and made the disaster the decisive condemnation of the policy that had caused it. Fortunato's address to the electors of Melfi in March 1897, delivered in the Nineteenth Legislature after the disaster and the fall of Crispi, turned directly upon this point: the country had been brought to grief by a colonial policy of expansion and prestige, and the lesson, in his judgment, was prudence and withdrawal — an Eritrean policy that would hold what could be held and renounce the conquest of the Abyssinian highlands. In his later commemorations of the fallen of Adua, he returns again and again to the same themes of the folly of imperial adventure and the burden it imposed upon the people.

## Consequences

Adua fixed the limits of Italian empire in the Horn of Africa for a generation. The colony was saved only by the prompt abandonment of the war against Menelik, and the disaster chastened the colonial enthusiasm that had carried Italy forward in the 1880s and 1890s; the ambitions of the second monarchical period were forced back, and Italian expansion looked elsewhere. For the internal life of the country, the disaster discredited the statesman who had embodied the adventurous and authoritative style of the period, and it gave strength to the liberal, anti-imperial and southern voices — above all [[giustino-fortunato]] and the school of the Southern Question — who drew from it the argument that the Italian state should attend to its own misery at home rather than to glory abroad. It would not be until the twentieth century, and the wars of a later age, that Italy returned in force to the African empire that Adua had denied it.
