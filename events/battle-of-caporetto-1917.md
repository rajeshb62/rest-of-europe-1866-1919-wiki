# The Battle of Caporetto (24 October – 12 November 1917)

**24 October – 12 November 1917**

The battle of Caporetto (Karfreit), the Austro-German offensive that broke the Italian front on 24 October 1917 and drove the Italian army in a catastrophic retreat from the Isonzo across the Tagliamento to the Piave, before the line was at last arrested in November 1917. The greatest defeat suffered by the Italian army in the war, it cost the loss of the territory won in two years of the [[isonzo-battles-1915-17|Isonzo battles]], overthrew the chief of staff [[luigi-cadorna]], and threw Italy into the gravest crisis of the war — the point from which the recovery under [[armando-diaz]] would culminate in [[battle-of-vittorio-veneto-1918|Vittorio Veneto]].

## Background

After the Italian offensive of August–September 1917, the eleventh battle of the Isonzo, had carried the Italian line to the very edge of the plain, the Austrian command resolved to end the attrition of the Isonzo by a decisive stroke, and obtained German divisions for a combined Austro-German offensive. The blow was aimed at the junction of the Italian forces in the Julian Alps, in the sector about Caporetto (Karfreit) on the upper Isonzo, where the mountain front was held by troops weakened by the long summer battles. Cadorna, in his official account, describes the dispositions he had prepared to meet an Austro-German attack; but the sector was thin, the autumn of the third year of war had worn the army down, and the command underrated the force about to be flung upon it.

## The breakthrough

On 24 October 1917 the Austro-German forces struck at Caporetto. The attack, launched with the overwhelming local superiority that the concentration of German and Austrian divisions supplied, broke through the Italian line at its weakest point; the use of new infiltration tactics and of the storm troops overwhelmed the defenders, and the front gave way in a widening gap. The Italian army, cut off from its bases in the mountains and taken in the flank, fell into confusion. Cadorna's account, which gives the dispositions and the battle, records how the rupture spread faster than it could be contained: the units in the mountains, their rear turned, were compelled to abandon the positions won at such cost, and the whole front along the Isonzo collapsed. The retreat that followed was as much a rout as a withdrawal, the army streaming westward with the enemy at its heels.

## The retreat to the Piave

Cadorna's narrative traces the retreat stage by stage. Falling back from the Isonzo, the Italian army abandoned the whole of the Friulian plain and sought to rally on the line of the Tagliamento; but the enemy pressed on, crossed the Tagliamento in its turn, and the retreat had to be continued — *la ritirata dal Tagliamento alla Piave*, the retreat from the Tagliamento to the Piave. Behind the army, the loss of the eastern provinces and of immense stores was total, and the moral blow was even greater than the material. It was in the course of this retreat that the question of French help arose, and Cadorna records the mission of Marshal Foch to Italy in October–November 1917, rebutting the view that Foch came as the adviser and inspirer of the retreat and insisting, on the contrary, that Foch opposed it. The army was finally halted only on the line of the Piave and the Monte Grappa, where the exhausted forces and the torrent of the autumn-swollen river gave a defensible barrier, and where the retreat was at last arrested in November 1917.

## Consequences

Caporetto was the greatest disaster in Italian military history up to that time — the loss of a whole frontier, of two years' conquests, and of a hundred thousand prisoners and enormous material, with the frontier of the war moved from the Isonzo to the Piave within a few weeks. It brought the fall of [[luigi-cadorna]], who was relieved of the command in November 1917 and replaced by [[armando-diaz]]; and it threw Italy, its army shattered and its people demoralised, into the gravest internal crisis of the war. Yet the arrest on the Piave proved to be the turning point: the defence of the river line, restored and rearmed by the new command with the help of the Allies, held, and the lesson of Caporetto — the need to husband the army for a decisive battle — shaped the strategy that would end the war. A year later the Italian army, rebuilt, would break the same Austrian front at [[battle-of-vittorio-veneto-1918|Vittorio Veneto]], and the humiliation of Caporetto would be reversed in the greatest Italian victory of the war.
