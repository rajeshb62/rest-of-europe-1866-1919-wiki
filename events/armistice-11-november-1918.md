# The Armistice of 11 November 1918

**11 November 1918**

The armistice concluded at Rethondes in the forest of Compiègne on the morning of 11 November 1918, by which Germany accepted the conditions of [[marshal-foch]] and the Allied commands and the fighting ceased on all fronts at eleven o'clock. Recouly's account carries the event through the German request for an armistice, the reception of the German plenipotentiaries, the signature at five in the morning, and the ending of operations six hours later. The armistice sealed the defeat of the Central Powers that the [[hundred-days-offensive-1918|Allied offensives of 1918]] and the collapse of the German allies had made complete.

## Background

By the autumn of 1918 the war was lost for the Central Powers. The [[hundred-days-offensive-1918]] on the western front had broken the German army; Ludendorff, after the day of 8 August, had concluded that the war could no longer be won militarily and passed the burden of peace to the civilians he had so long despised. The German allies collapsed in turn: the Bulgarian front fell in September, and the [[battle-of-vittorio-veneto-1918|Italian offensive of Vittorio Veneto]] in October put the Austro-Hungarian army out of action, isolating Germany militarily — in the words of the Italian Comando Supremo, the Austrian army "was thus annihilated" and Germany forced to ask for an armistice. With the Quadruple Alliance in ruins, Germany sued for peace.

## The German request

Germany made known the names of its plenipotentiaries and asked for the place where they might enter the French lines. Foch, having received on 7 November the radio asking him to let the German plenipotentiaries penetrate the lines, replied that same night, ordering them to present themselves at the outposts on the great road of Maubeuge–La Capelle–Guise. He left Senlis himself in his special train and arrived at 6.30 in the evening at the little station of Rethondes, between Compiègne and Soissons, in the forest of Laigle. The German delegates, delayed by the extraordinary congestion of the roads, were several hours late; they reached Haudroy, two kilometres from La Capelle, at nine o'clock. There an officer of the staff brought them to the army headquarters, and a special train conducted them to Rethondes, where they arrived about seven in the morning.

## The signature

The German delegation, headed by a member of the government, Erzberger, comprised a plenipotentiary minister, General Winterfeld, formerly military attaché in Paris, a naval captain, and some officers. Foch received them at nine o'clock in his train and communicated the conditions of the armistice. The time allowed for acceptance was three clear days; the Germans were to make known their answer before 11 November at eleven o'clock. During those days events in Germany had precipitated: Prince Max of Baden, head of the government, had resigned, and the revolution had broken out in Germany (see [[wilhelm-ii|the flight of the Kaiser]]). In the night of the 10th to the 11th, the German delegates, having received from their government the authorisation to sign, conferred in a memorable session with Foch, his staff, and Admiral Wemyss, representing the English government. The signatures were exchanged at five o'clock in the morning. Six hours later, at eleven o'clock, the operations were stopped on the whole front.

## The armistice and the end of the war

In Paris, toward the end of the morning, the news that the armistice was signed ran through the capital like an electric spark — the ringing of bells, salvos of artillery, flags appearing at all the windows, and a crowd flooding the streets. The armistice of 11 November ended the fighting that had begun with the mobilisations and declarations of the summer of 1914 (see [[july-crisis-1914]] and [[sarajevo-assassination-1914]]). For the Allies it was victory; for Germany it was the beginning of the political catastrophe in which the army sought to disown the defeat — the seed of the [[stab-in-the-back-legend]] by which the undefeated-in-the-field army claimed it had been betrayed from within. Recouly, writing sixteen years later, closes on the sombre note that the treaty that followed was "badly conceived and still worse executed," and that the tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Berlin bore the inscription *INVICTIS VICTI VICTURI* — "to the unconquered soldiers, today's conquered who will be tomorrow's conquerors."
