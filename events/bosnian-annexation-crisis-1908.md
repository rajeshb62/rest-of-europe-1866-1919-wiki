# The Bosnian Annexation Crisis (1908–1909)

**5–6 October 1908 – spring 1909**

The annexation crisis of 1908–09 was the greatest international storm of the Habsburg monarchy's Balkan policy in the decade before the First World War. On 5–6 October 1908 the foreign minister [[alois-aehrenthal|Count Aehrenthal]] announced the annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina, tearing up Article 25 of the Treaty of Berlin that had left the two provinces under the nominal sovereignty of the Sultan; the stroke threw Serbia and Montenegro into fury, brought Austria-Hungary and Russia to the brink, and was resolved only when the monarchy, backed by Germany, compelled the powers to accept the accomplished fact. The crisis was the sharpest manifestation of the monarchy's new assertiveness in the East and the immediate predecessor of the [[balkan-wars-1912-13|Balkan wars]].

## Background: the thirty-year ambiguity

Since the [[occupation-of-bosnia-1878|occupation of 1878]], Austria-Hungary had administered Bosnia-Herzegovina under the mandate of the [[congress-of-berlin-1878|Congress of Berlin]], but the two provinces remained legally under the sovereignty of the Ottoman Sultan, and the occupation had been declared provisional. Thirty years of administration had built the monarchy's position in the country and bought, as the annexation proclamation was to put it, successes bought with heavy sacrifices; but the legal ambiguity stood in the way of the constitutional institutions that the country's needs demanded. In the years before 1908 the projected railway through the Sandjak of Novi Pazar — the *Sandschakbahn* — and the reform questions had kept the Eastern Question alive, and the ground of the [[eastern-question|Eastern Question]] was everywhere contested. It was Aehrenthal's design to end the ambiguity by a bold stroke: to annex the provinces outright, secure their legal standing, and stake the monarchy's prestige on the acceptance of the act by the powers.

## The act of annexation

The annexation was proclaimed on 6 October 1908 — a day, Sosnosky notes, of ominous association in Austrian history, since it was the date of the murder of Field Marshal Count Latour by the Viennese mob in 1848. The Kaiser's hand-written letter to Aehrenthal, dated at Budapest on 5 October 1908, declared that the cultural and political purposes for which the monarchy had undertaken the occupation and administration of Bosnia-Herzegovina, and the successes bought with heavy sacrifices, could be durably secured only by the granting of constitutional institutions, for whose enactment the creation of a clear and unambiguous legal standing of the two lands was the indispensable prerequisite; he therefore extended the rights of his sovereignty over Bosnia and the Herzegovina, set the succession order in force for them, and, in token of his peaceful intentions, ordered the evacuation of the Sandjak of Novi Pazar. A proclamation to the inhabitants followed, promising constitutional institutions and a voice in the decisions over the affairs of their homeland. The formal announcement appeared in the *Wiener Zeitung* of 7 October, while the daily papers printed the text in their morning editions of the preceding day.

## The international crisis

The reaction was immediate and violent. In Belgrade the extra sheet of the *Politika* announced the annexation, and the Serbian foreign ministry, as [[conrad-von-hotzendorf|Conrad]] recorded from his post on the general staff, demanded immediate mobilisation and war to the death against the monarchy. That mood, Conrad insisted, was not first called forth by the annexation question but was the permanent keynote, breaking out spontaneously at the least occasion. Russia, humiliated by the sudden reversal of the arrangements it had helped prepare, protested; England, France and Italy took up the cry against the violation of the Berlin treaty; and the monarchy stood almost alone against the European concert, its only sure support being Germany.

The crisis developed through the autumn and winter of 1908 into the spring of 1909. The monarchy, having asserted the annexation, refused to retreat; the powers demanded a European conference that Aehrenthal refused to attend on terms that would reopen the annexation; and the tension mounted until the brink of war between Austria-Hungary and Serbia, with Russia behind Serbia, was reached. The military preparations of the monarchy for 1909 were completed, the war-case against Serbia was studied on the general staff, and Conrad, who had all along urged a preventive war, saw the moment approach for which he had prepared.

## The settlement

The crisis was resolved in the spring of 1909 when Germany threw its weight decisively behind the monarchy. Backed by Berlin — a support that belonged to the working of the [[triple-alliance|alliance]] — Austria-Hungary compelled the powers to accept the annexation as an accomplished fact, and Russia, unwilling to face a war it could not win, counselled Serbia to yield. Serbia was forced to abandon its demand for the restoration of the status quo, the European conference was dropped, and the annexation stood. The monarchy's assertiveness had triumphed; but the settlement left Serbia embittered and unreconciled, and the Russo-Serbian humiliation of 1909 became a permanent memory that poisoned the relations of the powers down to the catastrophe of 1914.

The crisis was not confined to foreign affairs. At home it produced the Friedjung trial, in which the historian Dr. Heinrich Friedjung, who had used documents supplied from Aehrenthal's circle in an attack on the South-Slav leaders, was confronted with the proof that they were forgeries, and the libel proceedings that followed exposed the falsity of the material and damaged the moral standing of the annexationist cause. The crisis also completed the rupture between Aehrenthal and [[conrad-von-hotzendorf|Conrad]], who had pressed for war against Serbia and regarded the peaceable settlement as a wasted opportunity — a divergence that was to end in Conrad's dismissal in 1911 and his reinstatement in 1912, when the Balkan wars vindicated, in his eyes, his warnings.

## Consequences

The annexation crisis redrew the political map of the monarchy's relations. It destroyed the Austro-Russian understanding in the Balkans and pushed the monarchy into almost complete dependence on Germany; it left Serbia, Russia's protégé, humiliated and determined on revenge; and it showed how far the powers would go to preserve the Berlin settlement — a settlement the monarchy itself had now torn up. The crisis was the direct predecessor of the [[balkan-wars-1912-13|Balkan wars]] of 1912–13, in which the Balkan states, no longer restrained, fell upon Turkey and redrew the peninsula, and it set the stage for the [[sarajevo-assassination-1914|Sarajevo assassination]] of 1914 and the world war. The road from the annexation to Sarajevo runs through the whole of the remaining history of the period, and the crisis stands as the moment when the monarchy committed its fortunes to the Balkans.
