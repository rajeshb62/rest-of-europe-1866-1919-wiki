# Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871)

**10 May 1871**

The definitive peace that closed the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]], signed at Frankfurt, by which France ceded Alsace and part of Lorraine and undertook to pay an indemnity of five milliards of francs. The Treaty of Frankfurt fixed the humiliation of defeat in permanent form — the loss of the eastern provinces and the burden of the indemnity that France would spend years and a national effort to discharge. It was the foundation upon which the government of [[adolphe-thiers]] built its authority, and the wound from which the French sense of revanche would spring.

## Background

The armistice concluded during the [[siege-of-paris-1870-71]] had been followed by the election of the National Assembly on 8 February 1871, returned under the shadow of defeat to make peace. At the preliminaries of Versailles, Thiers sat with Bismarck (21 February) and bargained under the double pressure of a Paris still armed and already insurgent and of a country resolved on peace. The questions of Metz and Belfort divided the negotiators; the fate of Alsace and Lorraine, and the size of the indemnity, were the crux of the whole settlement.

## The negotiation

Thiers's negotiation was conducted in the shadow of the coming [[paris-commune-1871]] — the capital was in open insurrection from 18 March even as the peace was being completed. Hanotaux records the debate over whether Metz might have been saved and whether the London conference was neglected; in the event Belfort was retained for France and a milliard struck from the original demand. The definitive treaty was signed at Frankfurt on 10 May 1871. Its territorial clauses ceded Alsace and part of Lorraine; its financial clauses fixed the indemnity at five milliards, with a schedule of payment and occupation; commercial and transit stipulations bound the two countries. It was, Hanotaux writes, the Germanic *wehrgeld* — the war-price exacted from French savings and French future.

## The indemnity and the liberation

The Treaty of Frankfurt thus set France two tasks: to pay the five milliards and to recover its territory from the occupying German armies. Thiers refused Bismarck's offer to bring in German financiers and staked the payment on France itself; the loan of two milliards of June 1871, subscribed 381,906 times, began the discharge. The conventions of 12 October 1871 began the evacuation of the German troops, and the negotiations for anticipated evacuation — Gontaut-Biron, Saint-Vallier, Arnim, Bismarck — bore fruit in the convention of liberation of 15 March 1873. When the last payment was completed and the evacuation finished (16 September 1873), Thiers had already been toppled, but the *libérateur du territoire* had done his work.

## Consequences

The Treaty of Frankfurt shaped the Europe of the following half-century. It left France dismembered and diminished in European weight, while Germany, triumphant, became the arbiter of the continent under [[otto-von-bismarck]]. The loss of Alsace-Lorraine created a permanent grievance — the protest of the annexed provinces against German rule, the pressure of revanche on French politics — and the indemnity, paid so surprisingly fast, testified both to French wealth and to the efficiency of the new Republic. The treaty of 10 May 1871 was the point of departure for the Republic's recovery and for the armed peace that dominated Europe until 1914.
