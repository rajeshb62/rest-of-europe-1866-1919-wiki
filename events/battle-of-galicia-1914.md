# The Battle of Galicia (1914)

**August – September 1914**

The opening campaign of the Austro-Hungarian army against Russia in the first months of the world war: the great battles of Krasnik, Komarów and Lemberg–Rawa Ruska, fought in the plains of Galicia, which ended in the defeat of the Monarchy's forces, the loss of Lemberg, and the retreat to the Carpathians. It was the military catastrophe that the [[military-dictatorship-conrad]] of the chief of the general staff had both predicted and laboured to forestall, and it shaped the whole eastern war. In his own account, the campaign was the first fruit of the [[july-crisis-1914]] and the test of the [[war-plans-1914]] agreed with Germany.

## Background: the two-front deployment

The July crisis committed the Monarchy at once to war with Serbia and with Russia. Under Conrad's direction the concentration against Russia was prepared in Galicia: the 1st Army at Niemirow–Sieniawa with parts at the lower San, the 4th Army about Lemberg, the 3rd Army about Zloczów and Zborów, the 2nd Army along the Strypa and south of the Dniester, with eleven cavalry divisions on the frontier. Of the forty infantry and eleven cavalry divisions mobilised against Russia, the whole weight was flung into the Galician theatre, while only a fraction — eight infantry divisions and some brigades — was left to the Serbian and Montenegrin front.

Conrad's plan was offensive from the first. The 1st and 4th Armies were to advance east between the Bug and the Vistula to win freedom of manoeuvre and a tactical success, while the 3rd and 2nd Armies met the enemy coming from the Rovno–Dubno region and across the Zbrucz. He held to the offensive as the only way to break the enemy's plans and not be crushed by numbers — a defensive stand, he was convinced, would lead to catastrophe against an enemy of Russia's strength. The plan rested on the expectation of the German advance in East Prussia and on the support of Italy and Rumania; both supports were already doubtful. Conrad had never trusted Italy, and he scarcely hoped for Rumania, even reckoning with its hostility.

## The battles: Krasnik and Komarów

The opening battles in the north-west of Galicia went in the Monarchy's favour. At **Krasnik** and **Komarów** the Austro-Hungarian armies, attacking with assembled forces, beat the Russian armies opposed to them and won the tactical successes Conrad had sought. These victories, won against a brave and energetically led enemy, are among the episodes Conrad's closing word recalls with pride, as a monument to the valour of the old k. u. k. army. But they were local triumphs on the right of the line; the decisive struggle lay farther east, about Lemberg, where the balance of the campaign turned.

## The struggle about Lemberg and the loss of the city

The decisive action was fought by the two southern Russian armies under the generals Roussky and Brusilov, whom Recouly calls the two best generals of the Russian army — the one cold, severe, a professor's air behind his spectacles, a stratagemist who knew how to command and be feared; the other an ardent, impetuous cavalry general who carried audacity into all ranks. This immense Russian offensive against the Austro-Hungarian armies was conducted with skill and at the same time with fury; borne on by the consciousness of superiority, the Russians attacked everywhere with vigour. The campaign became a great battle of encounter — Recouly dates the battle of Lemberg from 25 August to 15 September.

The Russians had the advantage not only in numbers but in the cohesion of the Monarchy's polyglot army. Recouly observes the cracks already sounding in the Austrian army, the faithful image of the crumbling empire it served: while the Hungarians and some of the German-Austrian elements fought well, everything Slav fought without conviction, some not far from wishing for the defeat that would mean their liberation; whole regiments went over to the enemy. In the battle about Lemberg the Austro-Hungarian forces, though they held out to the utmost, were beaten. **Lemberg**, the capital of Galicia, fell to the Russians; **Przemysl** was invested; the army abandoned four hundred guns and a hundred thousand prisoners and withdrew upon the Carpathians, while Russian detachments, crossing the passes, came into view of the vast Hungarian plain.

## Consequences: the collapse of the agreed first action

The defeat in Galicia was, in Conrad's own judgment, the decisive event of the war's beginning. In the preface to his fourth volume he wrote that the decision of the whole war lay in the failure of the plan agreed with Germany for the decisive first action; what followed was only a stubborn struggle to make good the consequences of that failure, and it failed before the superiority of the enemies and the failure of the means. The defeat on the Marne in the west, coming in the same month, was the signal for Italy, Rumania and the other opponents of Germany and Austria-Hungary to seek the Entente, giving the enemy side an overwhelming superiority.

For the Monarchy the loss of Galicia meant the loss of its one great ally's confidence. Recouly notes that Germany was obliged to come quickly to the rescue of Austria, to prop its tottering ally — condemned, he writes, to carry Austria "literally at arm's length" for the four years of the war. From the autumn of 1914 the eastern war became a struggle fought largely with German support on Austrian soil, and the defeat of Galicia was the first of the disasters that would bring the Dual Monarchy to [[treaty-of-brest-litovsk-1918]] and to its dissolution. The battle thus marks, in the military history of the period, the collapse of the Habsburg power in the east at the very opening of the war.
