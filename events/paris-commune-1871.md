# Paris Commune (18 March – 28 May 1871)

**18 March – 28 May 1871**

The insurrectionary government of Paris that defied the National Assembly of Versailles after the defeats of 1870–71, and that was suppressed in the Bloody Week of May 1871. The Commune — at once a municipal revolt, a social revolution, and a reassertion of the revolutionary tradition of Paris — posed the most dangerous challenge to the nascent [[third-republic-france]] and was crushed with a ferocity that left a deep mark on French politics. Hanotaux recounts it as a civil war within the defeat.

## Background

Paris, which had endured the [[siege-of-paris-1870-71]] and had been starved and bombarded by the Germans, found the peace of the new National Assembly intolerable. The Assembly, elected by a rural France resolved on peace, sat at Versailles and treated the republican capital with suspicion; the city was garrisoned by a National Guard armed and enraged, and the war's suffering had radicalized the working districts. Hanotaux traces the explosion to the Assembly's measures and to the attempt to seize the guns of Montmartre on 18 March 1871. The attempt to disarm the capital detonated the insurrection; the government and the regular army withdrew to Versailles, and the Commune proclaimed itself master of Paris.

## The Commune

For two months there existed a dual power: Versailles and the Commune. Hanotaux describes the composition and factions of the revolutionary government — the Central Committee, the International, the Blanquists and Jacobins, the old revolutionary currents revived in a new context. The Commune was a municipal and social experiment, a government of the working city asserting its autonomy and its revolutionary heritage against a conservative Assembly. Meanwhile the national government under [[adolphe-thiers]] regrouped the regular army at Versailles and prepared to retake the capital.

## The suppression and the Bloody Week

The military operations of the civil war followed: the siege of Paris by the Versailles army under Marshal [[patrice-mac-mahon]], the provincial risings put down one by one, and finally the assault of May 1871. The Bloody Week (la semaine sanglante), the last days of the Commune's resistance and its crushing, was one of the bloodiest episodes in modern French history. The communards, their barricades overwhelmed, were executed in large numbers as the army reoccupied the city; the vengeance of the victorious Versailles troops matched the fury of the insurrection. Hanotaux records the repression and its legal and political aftermath — the courts-martial, the transportations, the years of mourning that followed.

## Consequences

The Commune fixed the terms of French politics for a generation. For the conservative Assembly and for Thiers it justified the restoration of order; the suppression confirmed Versailles as the seat of national authority and made possible the completion of the peace and the payment of the [[treaty-of-frankfurt-1871]] indemnity. For the French Left the Commune became a founding martyrdom — the proof of the Republic's capacity for slaughter and the seed of the revolutionary socialist tradition. The blood of the Bloody Week separated the bourgeois Republic from the workers of Paris and fed the socialism that would return, in different forms, through the Boulanger years, the [[dreyfus-affair-1894-1906]], and the twentieth century. In the immediate present, the defeat of the Commune allowed the National Assembly to turn from civil war to the constitutional struggle over the form of the state.
