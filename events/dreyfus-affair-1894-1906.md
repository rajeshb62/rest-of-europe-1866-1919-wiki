# Dreyfus Affair (1894–1906)

**1894–1906**

The Dreyfus affair was the crisis that convulsed the Third Republic for a decade — the 1894 treason conviction of Captain Alfred Dreyfus, the long campaign to prove his innocence, and the eventual vindication and rehabilitation that divided France between the defenders of the army and the state and the defenders of a single man's justice. It was the defining moral-political crisis of the Republic, the last surge of the republican *mystique* in Péguy's phrase, and the event that reshaped the alignment of French politics, religion, and socialism.

## Background

The affair arose from the espionage climate of the 1890s, when France, defeated in 1870, lived in suspicion of Germany and of internal betrayal. Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish officer of the general staff, was convicted in 1894 of treason — of delivering military secrets to the Germans — by a court-martial on evidence that was, in reality, thin and partly forged. He was degraded and deported to Devil's Island. For the army and for much of public opinion the verdict was a settled fact; to question it was to question the honour of the institution that embodied the nation.

## Narrative

The campaign for revision began almost at once. Péguy, in *Notre jeunesse*, honours **Bernard-Lazare** — "born at Nîmes, 14 June 1865; died at Paris, 1 September 1903" — as the prophet of the affair, "the first to defend Dreyfus," the one whose word he calls "the decisive word of the affair." Even as some Dreyfusards grumbled at the returned captain's imperfections, Bernard-Lazare defended him "with that admirable voluntary blindness of those who truly love": "He is innocent, that is already much."

The affair was transformed into a national crisis by the intervention of the intellectuals. **Émile Zola's** open letter *J'accuse* (1898) threw the question into the open, accusing the army's leaders of a judicial crime; his prosecution and conviction turned the Dreyfusards into a movement. The affair divided France into two irreconcilable camps: the anti-Dreyfusards, who held that the honour of the army and the reason of state must not be sacrificed for one man, and the Dreyfusards, who held that "a single injustice, a single crime suffices to break the whole social pact." The Dreyfusard cause enlisted the new intellectuals and the republican left; the anti-Dreyfusard cause drew together the army, the clerical and nationalist right, and much of the Catholic hierarchy. The passions it unleashed — including anti-Semitic agitation — convulsed French society.

For Péguy, the affair was above all a spiritual event. The Dreyfusards, he writes, spoke "in a deep Christian movement," refusing that France should be "constituted in a state of mortal sin"; the true division of the affair was between "the men of eternal salvation" and "the men of temporal salvation." He calls it "the last of the operations of the republican mystique," the supreme surge of the heroism of his generation. Sabatier, likewise, treats "the true significance of the Dreyfus affair" as one of the keys to the religious orientation of contemporary France. The affair, in this reading, was the moment when the Republic's conscience was tested — and the moment that redefined what the Republic and its institutions stood for.

## Consequences

The affair was resolved by the eventual vindication of Dreyfus. A second court-martial at Rennes in 1899 again found him guilty but with extenuating circumstances, an outcome that satisfied no one and prolonged the struggle; it was only in 1906 that the verdict was finally quashed and Dreyfus fully rehabilitated. By then the affair had permanently reshaped French politics. It discredited the anti-Dreyfusard alliance of army, Church, and nationalist right and delivered a decisive reinforcement to the republican and anticlerical cause — clearing the way for the republican governments that followed to complete the laic programme. For the socialists, as Lagardelle records, the affair and its aftermath tested their relation to the Republic: the ministry of Waldeck-Rousseau, with Millerand as the first socialist minister, marked the entanglement of socialism in parliamentary politics that the syndicalists would denounce.

Péguy's own conclusion was darker. The affair, he held, had begun in *mystique* and ended in *politique*: the Dreyfusard faith degenerated into Dreyfusard calculation, and the party men of both sides turned against those who had fought from conviction. The degradation of the mystique into politique, the triumph of the "modern world" that believes in nothing, and the loss of the republican and Christian faiths in a single movement of "demystification" — these, for Péguy, were the enduring legacy of the affair, which proved to be the last surge of the heroism of his generation.
