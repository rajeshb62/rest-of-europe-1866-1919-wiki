# The Battle of Wörth (1870)

**6 August 1870**

The defeat of Marshal Mac-Mahon's French army at **Wörth** — called by the French also Frœschwiller or Reichshoffen — on **6 August 1870**, the second great engagement of the opening days of the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]]. Fought in Alsace two days after the action at Wissembourg, it broke the French right wing, opened the road into Lorraine and, together with the simultaneous defeat at Forbach, decided the first act of the war in favour of Prussia and the German armies.

## Background: the invasion of Alsace

War had been declared in July 1870, and the German armies, mobilised on the machinery of the [[north-german-confederation]] and its military conventions with the southern states, had crossed the Rhine into France within days. The French forces, divided into two great bodies, had taken position to receive them: on the left Marshal Bazaine about Metz, and on the right Marshal Mac-Mahon in Alsace, with his headquarters at Strasbourg. Ollivier's account of the campaign records the dispositions of these first days — how Mac-Mahon, his army extended about the northern frontier, hurried reinforcements toward the line of the Wissembourg positions, where the division of General Abel Douay was posted, and how the German advance fell upon it. On 4 August the action at **Wissembourg** gave the Germans their first victory of the war and left the French position in Alsace uncovered.

## The battle of 6 August

Two days later the collision came in full. Mac-Mahon had drawn his army up to meet the German advance, and the two forces met on the field of Wörth on the heights of Frœschwiller. The French held their ground with the tenacity for which Ollivier's sources give them credit, and the battle was among the severest of the opening campaign — an engagement in which the Marshal himself was wounded and his army, pressed in front and turned upon its flanks, was at length broken. The issue was a decisive Prussian and German victory. Sybel, writing from the German side, records the battle and its aftermath in the phrase that passed from hand to hand — the "dissolved flight of Wörth," the rout of Mac-Mahon's army in disorder across the field. The defeat was total: the corps of the French right was shattered, and Mac-Mahon's army, no longer able to hold Alsace, fell back in retreat, abandoning the province to the invader.

## The consequences of the defeat

The effect of Wörth reached far beyond the field. It completed, in the first week of the war, the destruction of the French line in the east: Mac-Mahon's army, driven out of Alsace, could no longer join hands with Bazaine's army in Lorraine, and the German advance into the heart of France was left open. The news of the double defeat of 6 August — Wörth and Forbach on the same day — fell upon France as a calamity. Sybel's narrative shows how the intelligence of the "Schlacht von Wörth" reverberated through the courts of Europe: it reached the King of Italy, [[victor-emmanuel-ii]], in the night of 6–7 August and finally resolved his hesitation over the alliance with France, convincing him that the French had no generals and that nothing was to be done for the Emperor; the September Convention, the compact that had bound Italy to France, was thereby rendered void, and the road to Rome lay open. Within France the defeat of Mac-Mahon threw the government into crisis and set the stage for the disasters that followed — the fall of the [[second-empire-france]] and, two days after Sedan, the collapse of the imperial régime in the [[battle-of-sedan-1870]].
