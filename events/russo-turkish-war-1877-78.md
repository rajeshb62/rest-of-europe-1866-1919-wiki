# Russo-Turkish War (1877–1878)

**1877–1878**

## Background

The war of 1877–1878 was the crisis in which the [[eastern-question]] — the long-standing question
of the fate of the Ottoman Empire's European provinces and of the position of its Balkan Slavs —
finally brought Russia into open conflict with the Porte. In the account of A. A. Kornilov's lecture
course, the ground had been laid over the preceding years by a policy that pressed outward on every
side. Government policy in the borderlands tightened: there were pressures in Little Russia and
Poland, and a foreign policy of expansion that set Russian and English interests at odds in Asia,
where the conquest of the Caucasus and of the Central Asian khanates extended the empire far beyond
its old frontiers.

Within Turkey itself troubles multiplied. The movement of the Balkan Slavs grew into insurrection,
and the Serbian war and the Bulgarian massacres turned the grievances of the South Slavs into a
cause that excited Russian society. The great powers entered a round of negotiations over the
condition of the Christian provinces of European Turkey, but these repeatedly failed to arrest the
drift toward war. The revolt of the South Slavs gave the Russian government both an opportunity and
a pretext for intervention in the affairs of the Ottoman Empire, and when the diplomatic path failed
the empire of [[alexander-ii]] resolved upon war.

Kornilov treats the war as one of the decisive episodes of Alexander's reign, bound up with the
reaction at home and with the disillusionment that was soon to turn society against the government.
The war itself, and still more the peace that followed it, became a turning-point in the internal
history of Russia no less than in the Eastern Question.

## The state of preparation

Kornilov is scathing about the military preparation. When war actually began, it emerged that the
state was not ready in any respect. The government was obliged to make mass issues of paper money,
which destroyed the plans of Finance Minister Reutern for restoring the exchange rate of the paper
ruble and undermined the careful financial edifice he had built through the reform era. The reforms
of War Minister Miliutin — above all the replacement of conscription by universal military service,
carried out only in 1874, two years before the mobilization of 1876 — were so new and had so
overturned the old structure of the army that carrying out a full mobilization proved far from easy.
The administrative authorities on whom the correctness and speed of mobilization depended proved to
be beneath all criticism, so that in the course of six months only an inadequate body of troops
could be brought to the Turkish frontiers.

Part of the blame Kornilov lays on Count Ignatiev, the Russian ambassador at Constantinople, who
asserted that the Turks could be beaten very easily, that Turkey was decaying, and that only very
small forces were needed to strike her a decisive blow. In reality there were not only too few
troops but an extremely badly chosen army staff. The commander-in-chief was the emperor's brother,
Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich, a man entirely without the necessary strategic talents. As chief of
staff he chose General Nepokojchitsky, who in youth might have been capable, especially as a writer
on military questions, but was now quite out of date, distinguished by complete lack of
resourcefulness, and had no campaign plan whatever.

## The first campaign and Plevna

Immediately after the brilliantly executed crossing of the Danube came complete disorganization. In
the absence of any general plan, the commanders of the separate detachments began to undertake
highly risky actions on their own initiative. The enterprising and brave General Gurko rushed
straight beyond the Balkans and, meeting no significant obstacles, was carried almost as far as
Adrianople. Meanwhile Osman Pasha, commanding several tens of thousands of Turkish troops, occupied
the unassailable position of Plevna in the rear of the Russian forces that had crossed the Balkans.
The storming of Plevna was repulsed, and it soon became clear that this was a place so unassailable
that Osman could not be driven out, and one had to think of a prolonged siege — for which there were
not enough troops to invest Plevna from all sides.

The position was dire. Had Suleiman Pasha, commanding the southern Turkish army then on the far side
of the Balkans, at once crossed the mountains as ordered and joined Osman, Gurko and the other
advanced detachments would have been cut off and destroyed. Only because Suleiman, apparently
competing with Osman, went to drive the Russians out of the Shipka Pass held by Radetsky instead of
crossing by a free pass, were the advanced detachments saved. Shipka was held, Suleiman was beaten
off by Radetsky, and Gurko was able to retreat safely while fresh Russian troops came up.

Plevna nevertheless had to be besieged for several months. The first attempt to take the Plevna
heights came in July 1877, and Osman Pasha could be compelled to surrender only in December, and
then only because the whole Guard, which could mobilize and be transported quickly, was summoned
from Petersburg to the theatre of war. It was also necessary to turn to Prince Karl of Romania, who
agreed to give his — small but well-trained and well-armed — army of thirty-five thousand only on
condition that he himself be named commander of the whole siege corps. Only with the arrival of the
engineer-general Totleben, summoned from Petersburg, did the siege of Plevna go properly, and Osman
Pasha at last had to lay down his arms after an unsuccessful attempt to break out.

## The advance and the Adrianople armistice

The campaign thus dragged on through all of 1877 and part of 1878. After the capture of Plevna the
Russians crossed the Balkans once more, occupied Adrianople, which was not then a fortress, and
approached Constantinople in January 1878. At this point [[alexander-ii]] received a telegram from
Queen Victoria begging him to stop and conclude an armistice. Although the emperor had promised
England before the war that he would not seek to occupy Constantinople, Lord Beaconsfield had in
support of this telegram already got Parliament to vote six million pounds for war preparations, and
war with England seemed almost inevitable.

Turkey, however, was utterly exhausted and was compelled to sue for peace without waiting for
English support. In mid-January 1878 (new style) the Adrianople armistice was concluded, based on
the sultan's promise to satisfy the demands of the great powers and to give a proper order — partly
in the form of semi-independent principalities, partly in the form of territories with Christian
governors-general — to the Christian provinces of European Turkey.

## San Stefano and the Treaty of Peace

Shortly after the armistice diplomatic negotiations opened at San Stefano, conducted on the Russian
side by Ignatiev with complete success. In March the peace treaty was signed, satisfying all
Russia's demands. Not only were Serbia and Montenegro enlarged, but Bulgaria became a
semi-independent principality with territory reaching to the Aegean Sea. In Asia the Russians had
waged war with far greater success than on the Balkan peninsula, taking Kars, Erzerum, and Batum. By
the peace treaty it was established that, in exchange for part of the war indemnity of 1,400 million
rubles which Turkey was to pay, she would cede to Russia, in the region of Asiatic Turkey and out of
the occupied territory, Kars and Batum with their districts. As an indispensable condition of peace,
the emperor Alexander also demanded the return of that strip of Bessarabia which in 1856 had been
separated from Russia and given to Romania; Romania, which had fought in alliance with Russia, was
greatly offended by this, and in compensation was given Dobruja.

## The Congress of Berlin

No sooner did England learn of these conditions than Lord Beaconsfield protested against any
alteration of Turkish territory without the participation of the great powers that had taken part in
the Paris Congress of 1856. Alexander II therefore, in the end, under threat of a heavy war with
England and Austria, had to agree to a congress of representatives of the great powers at Berlin,
under the presidency of Bismarck. At this congress the conditions of peace were substantially
altered: the acquisitions of Serbia, Montenegro, and especially Bulgaria were cut down. From the
latter a whole region was detached south of the Balkans — Eastern Rumelia — which remained a Turkish
province with a Christian governor-general. Beaconsfield also protested against Russia's territorial
acquisitions; he could not destroy them, but he succeeded in insisting that Batum be changed from a
military port into a peaceful harbor, open to all states.

In this way the conditions of peace were changed to Russia's disadvantage. This, together with the
conduct of the war — which had produced a series of failures — and with the thievery that once again
was exposed in the supply of provisions (for the investigation of which a special committee was
appointed), created extraordinary indignation and embitterment of feeling in wide circles of Russian
society. As Kornilov emphasizes, it was not only the radical and revolutionary strata who were
indignant, but even the most loyal circles, with the Slavophiles at their head. When the rumors of
the concessions made at the [[congress-of-berlin-1878]] reached Moscow, Ivan Aksakov spoke at a
public meeting of the Slavonic Society in thunderous terms of the "shameful tidings" of Russian
concessions carried daily from Berlin by the press into the knowledge of the whole people, and
declared that he refused to believe such acts of Russian diplomacy could be approved and
acknowledged by the "supreme authority." The reputation of the empire among the Balkan Slavs, the
position of the southern part of Bulgaria, the independence of the remaining Slav peoples, the
political preponderance of the hated Austria — all, Aksakov insisted, had been given away.

## Consequences

The war had serious economic and financial consequences. The mass issue of paper money undermined
Reutern's financial programme, and the finance minister resigned. The war exhausted the treasury at
the very moment when the government had been struggling, in Reutern's system, to raise the country's
general productivity and especially its export trade and to build the railways. The
[[eastern-question]] was not settled but merely deferred; the settlement at Berlin left Russia's
prestige among the Balkan Slavs diminished and Austria's influence correspondingly enhanced.

At home the disillusionment of the war, the discovery of corruption, and the anger over Berlin fed
the growing gulf between the government and society. The [[zemstvo-great-reforms|zemstvo]] movement
gathered strength, the government appealed to society for support, and the zemstvos responded with
addresses that pressed for participation in government. The revolutionary movement, hardened by the
years of reaction, turned toward systematic terrorism, and the party of the
[[narodnaya-volya|People's Will]] took shape in the aftermath of the war. The war and the peace that
followed thus marked a step on the road that led to the attempts on the life of [[alexander-ii]] and
to the catastrophe of 1881 (see [[assassination-alexander-ii-1881]]).

## Key participants

- [[alexander-ii]] — Emperor of Russia, who imposed the Bessarabian condition on the peace.
- The Ottoman commanders — Osman Pasha (defender of Plevna) and Suleiman Pasha (the southern front).
- Russian commanders — Grand Duke Nikolai Nikolaevich (commander-in-chief), Generals Gurko, Radetsky, and the engineer-general Totleben.
- Count Ignatiev — Russian ambassador at Constantinople, negotiator of the San Stefano treaty.
- Bismarck — president of the [[congress-of-berlin-1878]].
