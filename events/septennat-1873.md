# The Septennat (20 November 1873)

**20 November 1873**

The law by which the National Assembly conferred on Marshal Mac-Mahon a personal seven-year presidency, adopted on 20 November 1873 after the failure of the monarchical restoration. The Septennat was the monarchical majority's fallback when [[comte-de-chambord]] wrecked the restoration with his letter of 27 October 1873 — a provisional expedient intended to keep a king possible that in fact cleared the way for the Republic.

## Background

The National Assembly elected in February 1871 had made peace, repressed the [[paris-commune-1871]], and overthrown [[adolphe-thiers]] on 24 May 1873, but it still could not agree on a form of government. Its majority was monarchical, but divided among legitimists, Orléanists, and Bonapartists — a monarchical majority without a single candidate. The hope was "fusion," the union of the two branches of the house of Bourbon under the comte de Chambord; the campaign of autumn 1873 was staked on the pretender's acceptance.

## The failure of the restoration

The decisive blow was Chambord's letter of 27 October 1873, refusing the tricolour flag and clinging to the white flag of the Bourbons. The monarchical chiefs — the Nine, the commission that had delegated Charles Chesnelong to Salzburg — abandoned the campaign; the comte de Paris had already sketched the "line of retreat" toward the Marshal. With the restoration impossible, the right needed a way to keep power without a king and to postpone the question of the form of the state. Their answer was to prolong the presidency of the soldier they had installed at the Élysée.

## The vote of 20 November

The Assembly, on the proposal of the duc de Broglie and the governmental right, voted on 20 November 1873 to confer on Marshal Mac-Mahon the exercise of the presidential power for a term of seven years — the *septennat*. Hanotaux records the embarrassment of the extreme right, the attitude of the Orléans princes, the proposals of Changarnier, and the comte de Chambord's presence at Versailles, where Mac-Mahon, through Blacas, refused to receive him. The vote established a provisional regime: the septennat was "personal" to the Marshal, a tenure that left the future open. It was, Hanotaux writes, the expectant policy once more profiting the Republic.

## Consequences

The Septennat shaped the whole settlement of the next years. It gave France a president for seven years without deciding whether the regime was a monarchy or a republic; Broglie's second ministry and the *ordre moral* governed under it, and Broglie would declare the septennat "incommutable." But a personal, provisional presidency was a holding position that could not last: it prepared the constitutional settlement of 1875, which the Assembly would be forced to make as the septennat expired into permanence. By keeping the throne empty and the Marshal seated, the Septennat — designed to preserve the monarchy's chances — in fact made the Republic inevitable. It stands with the [[french-constitution-1875]] as the hinge between the monarchical majority and the parliamentary Republic.
