# The Battle of Mentana (3 November 1867)

**3 November 1867**

The battle of Mentana on 3 November 1867, in which the Garibaldian volunteers marching on Rome were crushed at the gates of the Papal city by the Papal troops and the French reinforcements, equipped with the new *chassepots*. The defeat ended the last great revolutionary attempt to seize Rome by the force of the volunteers, and it demonstrated once more that the Roman question could not be solved by the *partito d'azione* but only, in the event, by the monarchy and the collapse of the French protectorate. It stands in this corpus as the decisive reverse that closed the revolutionary road to Rome, which the [[capture-of-rome-1870|annexation of 1870]] would then achieve by other means.

## Background

Since the establishment of the Kingdom of Italy in 1861, Rome had been the great unfinished business of the [[risorgimento]]. The September Convention of 1864 between Italy and [[napoleon-iii|France]] had arranged the withdrawal of the French garrison from Rome while pledging Italy not to attack the Papal territory; but the city and the remnant of the Papal States remained under the Pope, guarded by the Papal army and by the French protection that the convention preserved. The revolutionary democrats, above all the followers of Mazzini, would not wait on diplomacy, and their restless pressure for the seizure of Rome was embodied in the person of [[giuseppe-garibaldi]]. The ministry of Urbano Rattazzi, in office from 11 April to 19 October 1867, found itself caught between the republican agitation for the attack and the French guarantee that forbade it.

## The march on Rome

In the autumn of 1867 the Garibaldian movement resolved on a new attempt, determined to enter Rome in defiance of the Papal and French power. The Italian government could not sanction the expedition, any more than it had been able to sanction the march of 1862 that ended at Aspromonte; yet the volunteers gathered and advanced toward the Papal frontier, and Garibaldi himself, breaking from the restraint his cautious supporters urged, placed himself at their head. The enterprise was undertaken against the grain of the convention and of the monarchy's whole policy of awaiting the diplomatic solution. The Papal forces, summoned to defend the Patrimony, went out to meet the intruders, and from the French side reinforcements were hurried to the scene.

## The battle

The clash came at Mentana, a small town northeast of Rome, on 3 November 1867. The Papal troops, stiffened by the French reinforcements sent in support of the Pope, fell upon the Garibaldians, and the superiority of their arms told heavily: the French soldiers carried the *chassepot*, the new breech-loading rifle that had already given the French army its pre-eminence among the powers. The fire of the chassepots broke the thin and ill-armed volunteer columns, which had no answer to a weapon so rapid and so deadly. In Garibaldi's own retrospective, written in his *Memorie*, the campaign of Mentana was the disastrous ending of his years of struggle for the city — the attempt to seize Rome crushed at the gates of the Papal capital by the Papal troops and the French reinforcements. The survivors were scattered or taken, and the revolutionary road to Rome was closed.

## Consequences

Mentana was a double defeat. Militarily it destroyed the last great volunteer army that the revolutionary Risorgimento could throw against Rome, and it confirmed that the French protection of the Pope was still effective enough, while it lasted, to bar the city. Politically it discredited the revolutionary solution: Oriani, in his account of the last revolutionary revival, places Mentana at the centre of the failure of the *partito d'azione* and the parliamentary reaction that followed, and it forced the ministry of Rattazzi from office — the Menabrea ministry succeeding it on 27 October 1867, immediately in the wake of the disaster. The lesson of the defeat, that Rome could not be won by the volunteers against France, left the field to the monarchical and diplomatic road, which would be vindicated three years later when the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71|defeat of France]] withdrew the protector and opened the way to the [[capture-of-rome-1870|breach of Porta Pia]]. For Garibaldi, though he lived to see Rome Italian, the failure at Mentana stood as the last frustration of his revolutionary design upon the city.
