# The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (1918)

**3 March 1918**

The peace concluded at Brest-Litovsk between the Central Powers and the Soviet Republic, signed on 3 March 1918, by which Russia withdrew from the world war at the price of enormous territorial and economic concessions in the west and south. It was the first great settlement of the war, the fruit of the German eastern victory, and — from the standpoint of the Austrian minister [[ottokar-czernin]], who negotiated it, and of the Russian generals who refused it — a document of deeply contested character: for one the deliverance of the starving Central Powers, for the other a "burning shame."

## Background: the collapse of the Russian front

The treaty grew out of the ruin of the Russian front after the [[february-revolution-1917]]. Czernin, the Austro-Hungarian foreign minister, dated the possibility of peace with Russia to the summer of 1917, when reports from the neutral world described the disintegration of the Russian army and the bitter struggle within the Russian parties over the Allied-urged offensive. The army at the front was no longer willing to fight; the infantry in particular refused the offensive, while only the officers, the cavalry and the Cossacks remained for the war. The soldiers' committees had paralysed the command, and the front was dissolving into the countryside. Kerensky's June offensive collapsed, and the front dissolved into revolutionary chaos.

After the [[october-revolution-1917]] the Bolsheviks of [[vladimir-lenin]], who had promised peace and land, took power and at once sued for an armistice. On 22 December 1917 negotiations opened at Brest-Litovsk between the Central Powers and the delegation of the Soviet government. For the Central Powers, exhausted by three and a half years of blockade, the prospect of the eastern peace was the prospect of survival; for the Allies it was a betrayal of the common war. The German high command, confident of victory in the east, saw in the negotiations the means of detaching Russia and of turning the mass of the army against the west.

## The negotiations

Czernin himself went to Brest-Litovsk and left a vivid record of the proceedings. The German negotiator was the state secretary Kühlmann, supported by the soldier-diplomat General Hoffmann; the first Russian delegations were led by Joffe, with Kamenev and others, and later by [[leon-trotsky]]. The Central Powers professed an "annexationless peace" (*annexionsloser Friede*) — peace without forcible annexations, on the basis of the self-determination of peoples — while the Germans in fact pressed for the detachment of the border provinces of Poland, Courland and Lithuania.

Through January the talks dragged on, complicated by the Ukrainians, who claimed a separate voice and concluded their own peace, and by the looming famine of the Central Powers, above all of Austria. Czernin was harassed by the German high command — Hindenburg's furious telegrams over any "renunciation," Ludendorff's hourly telephone calls — and at one point demanded a free hand for himself in case the Germans broke with the Russians. The Bulgarians threatened, the Germans feared that the Entente might accept a general peace, and the Russian delegation, for its part, tried to play for time, counting on the revolution to spread. The whole was a tangle of ambitions, suspicions and necessities in which the fate of millions was decided.

## Trotsky's defiance and the ultimatum

In January [[leon-trotsky]] arrived to lead the Soviet delegation, and the tone changed. He was greeted with demonstrations for peace at the stations on the way; but he proved an extremely skilled and at first courteous negotiator, who sought to use the proceedings as a platform for world revolution. He was, in Czernin's phrase, the leading spirit of the "Internationalists," those who wished to restore the International and to refuse a separate peace with the imperialists. Czernin, under the pressure of the Austrian famine and the demands of the allies, concluded that the peace must be had at once.

The Germans finally presented an ultimatum, backed by the threat of a renewed advance; Hoffmann, the old staff officer, wished to "give the Russians a good beating." On 10 February Trotsky, refusing both peace and war, broke off the negotiations and returned to Petrograd, declaring "no war, no peace" and counting on a German revolution. The German high command answered by resuming the offensive, which met almost no resistance from the dissolving Russian army. The Soviet government, under Lenin's insistence on peace at any cost, bowed to the ultimatum, and the treaty was signed at Brest-Litovsk on 3 March 1918. A separate peace with the Ukraine had already been concluded in February — the *Brotfriede* ("bread peace") on which Austria, starving, had pinned its hopes of grain.

## The terms

By the treaty Russia renounced Poland, Courland, Lithuania and Livonia and Estland, whose future Germany was to determine, withdrew from Finland and the Ukraine, ceded the districts of the west, and conceded to Germany and Austria-Hungary economic and financial advantages. In the south the Ukraine passed under German influence, and Russia lost the line of the old front. From the standpoint of the [[white-movement]], Denikin condemned the settlement as the "bargaining between the Central Powers and their soviet agents," a memory evoking "burning shame and pain": never, he wrote, had European statesmen thrown off with such shamelessness every covering of honour and justice. He noted that the Sovnarkom, linked by financial relations to the German staff, yet observed the outward decorum, and that even the Bolsheviks' own military council had voted against signing, with only Lenin, the commander-in-chief Krylenko and a minority for peace at any cost. The separate peace took Russia out of the war and released the German armies for the final offensives in the west, one of the critical events of 1918.

## Consequences

For the Central Powers the treaty was a triumph — the fulfilment of the eastern war aims and the prospect of relief from the blockade — but its fruits were soon poisoned. The expected grain and oil of the Ukraine did not end the Austrian famine, and the *Brotfriede* proved a delusion; Czernin, who had staked his policy on the eastern settlement, resigned in April 1918 amid the failure of his peace hopes in the west. For Russia the treaty was the price of the survival of the soviet regime, won at the cost of vast territories and of the moral authority of the revolution; it confirmed the Bolsheviks in power while the [[white-movement]] and the Allies denounced them as the betrayers of Russia.

The collapse of the Central Powers in November 1918 annulled the treaty in fact, but its legacy endured: the borderlands of the west and south passed through years of war and reorganisation, and the German occupation of the Ukraine and the Baltic drew into the conflict both the volunteers of the south and the forces of the new states. The Brest settlement stands as the first great act of the peacemaking that the war's end would complete at Versailles, and as the demonstration, for all of Europe, of what a peace of force could mean. For the [[ice-march-1918]], unfolding in the same weeks in the south, it was the shadow in which the White resistance began.
