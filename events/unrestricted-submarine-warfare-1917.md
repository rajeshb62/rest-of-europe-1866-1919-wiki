# Unrestricted Submarine Warfare (February 1917)

**February 1917 – 1918**

Germany's declaration of unrestricted submarine warfare in February 1917, by which the German navy was authorised to sink without warning the merchant shipping of neutrals as well as of enemies in the war zones, and which brought the United States into the war on the side of the Entente. Recouly numbers the decision among "the grave blunders of its military chiefs — ignorant of politics" that defeated Germany: the political defection of Russia brought victory within two fingers of Germany, but the unrestricted submarine war, by provoking American intervention, added one more enemy to the list. Czernin, who as Austrian foreign minister opposed it, records the decision and its fatal consequences in detail.

## Background

The war had reached the point where Germany, unable to break the Entente on land, turned to the submarine as the weapon that could sever the sea-borne supplies of Britain. The temptation was immense: the U-boat offered the prospect of strangling the island power that was the heart of the blockade. But the instrument could only be used ruthlessly, against all shipping in the war zones, and that meant sinking the vessels of neutrals — above all of the United States, whose trade and lives would be caught in the net. Recouly sets the decision in the frame of the German command's fatal over-confidence: the German military chiefs, convinced of their superiority over all adversaries, resolved to conduct the war henceforth "impitoyably, with the maximum of violence," regardless of the neutrals they might drive into the enemy camp. Ludendorff had no care for the risk of bringing the United States into the conflict.

## The decision

Czernin, newly elevated to the Austrian foreign ministry, treats the decision as the capital error of the war. He records that he and Prince Hohenlohe warned against it; that the German leaders, State Secretary Zimmermann and Admiral Holtzendorff, came to Vienna to press it; and that Holtzendorff gave a guarantee of success. The German leaders still in April 1918 remained optimistically convinced that the war would be ended before America had time "to come." Czernin records the judgment that the calculation was false — "that we all know today." The German generals' psychology was governed by a "victory psychosis": convinced that their army was invincible, they believed the submarine war would bring Britain to her knees in a few months. The decision was taken against the warnings of the political factors; Czernin insists that had the political men of Berlin been heard, there would have been no unrestricted submarine war, and that this one omission would have saved the Central Powers. Wilhelm II, "a prisoner of his generals," let the Supreme Command have its way. Hindenburg and Ludendorff — "gods for the German people" — were far more powerful than the Kaiser, and he less than ever able to oppose them.

## The American entry

The consequences followed with the swiftness the warning had predicted. The unrestricted submarine war, sinking American ships and citizens, drove the United States into the war on the side of the Entente in the spring of 1917. Recouly marks the entry of America as the decisive addition of one more enemy that made German victory impossible: it was a measure that risked displeasing the neutrals and making them enter the conflict, and Ludendorff "has no care for it." Where the Russian revolution had seemed to save Germany by removing the eastern enemy, the American intervention replaced the balance on the other side. Czernin records how England, at least from the summer of 1917, was resolved to shatter Germany, and how the fiasco of the submarine war revealed the false calculation of its authors: the construction of further U-boats had to be restricted in the course of the war, and Germany misjudged the whole situation.

## Consequences

The unrestricted submarine war thus stands as one of the turning points of the war — the decision that brought the manpower and wealth of the United States to the Entente just as the collapse of Russia (see [[february-revolution-1917]] and [[october-revolution-1917]]) had freed the Central Powers in the East. For Recouly it is the capital illustration of his thesis: Germany, too sure of its warlike superiority and committing its habitual sin of pride, ranged too many enemies against itself. The submarines, far from ending the war, helped to condemn the Central Powers to the defeat that the [[armistice-11-november-1918|armistice of 1918]] sealed. Czernin, writing from the Austrian side, sums up the decision as a grave blunder of the military leaders "ignorant of politics" — the blunder that added the decisive weight against the Central Powers.
