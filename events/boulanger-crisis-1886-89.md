# Boulanger Crisis (1886–1889)

**1886–1889**

The Boulanger crisis was a wave of populist, militarist agitation around General Georges Boulanger that threatened the parliamentary Republic in the later 1880s before collapsing. This corpus's assigned sources treat it only at the edges — the volumes of Hanotaux's history end at the death of Gambetta in 1882, before the movement reached its height — and the event is here recorded lightly, as the crisis of the maturing Republic that Grévy's resignation closed.

## Background

The Republic that entered the 1880s had passed through its founding "heroic age" and settled into parliamentary routine. Gambetta's death in 1882, followed by the fall of Ferry's ministry, closed the generation of the founders and left the regime to the play of shifting parliamentary majorities. Hanotaux's fourth volume suspends the narrative at Gambetta's death, treating it as a brutal conclusion after which "figures and the aspect of things change." Into this unsettled scene — a Republic of ministries rising and falling, of *opportunist* governments without a commanding leader — the Boulanger agitation erupted as a challenge to the parliamentary system itself.

## Narrative

The sources available here do not trace the movement's rise in detail. What they establish is that the agitation came to a head in the later 1880s, that it was bound up with the political storms and scandals of the era, and that it forced the resignation of President Jules Grévy in 1887 — the victim, as the record of his presidency puts it, of "the corruption affair that touched his entourage and of the political storms of the Boulanger era." The movement combined a glorification of the army and of national revenge with a populist appeal to the discontents of the parliamentary regime, and it momentarily seemed capable of overturning the Republic.

## Consequences

The crisis passed. The Republic withstood the Boulanger agitation, and the regime's consolidation survived the collapse of the movement. Grévy's departure, coming after the death of Gambetta and the fall of Ferry, closed the generation of the founders: the parliamentary Republic had shown that it could survive without a powerful president, and the presidency had been correspondingly weakened. The Boulanger episode belongs to the class of crises by which the maturing Third Republic tested and hardened its parliamentary institutions before the moral-political ordeal of the Dreyfus affair that followed in the next decade.
