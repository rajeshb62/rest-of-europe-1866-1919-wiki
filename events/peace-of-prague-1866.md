# Peace of Prague (23 August 1866)

**August 23, 1866**

## Background

The Peace of Prague was the treaty that ended the Austro-Prussian War. It was the culmination of a
rapid sequence of negotiations that began immediately after the decisive Prussian victory at
Königgrätz on 3 July 1866. Vienna, its army broken and the road to its capital laid open, sought
peace at once; the preliminary terms were agreed at Nikolsburg, where the negotiations opened in the
days after the battle, and the definitive treaty was then concluded and signed in Prague on 23
August 1866.

Throughout these weeks Napoleon III hovered over the settlement as a would-be mediator. He had
proposed a European congress in the spring and now sought to limit Prussia's gains and to win
compensation for France, but his intervention proved ineffective. Sybel observes of France's
passivity that by declining to take part in the settlement, Paris surrendered any right to object to
changes in a treaty whose conclusion it had not shared — a pointed comment on the French position,
and a warning of the resentment that was already gathering in Paris at the sudden consolidation of
Prussian power.

## The Terms

The Peace of Prague settled the German question decisively in Prussia's favour. The German
Confederation was dissolved and never revived; Austria was excluded from the political life of
Germany, ending centuries of Habsburg primacy in German affairs and leaving the future of Germany to
be shaped by Prussia alone. The terms, as Sybel's final book, *Grundlagen des neuen Reichs*
("Foundations of the new Reich"), records them, also provided for Prussia's annexation of Hanover,
Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, and the free city of Frankfurt, sweeping away the medium states that had stood
in the path of Prussian hegemony.

The settlement was, in essence, the Kleindeutsch programme realised by force of arms. Prussia had
fought the war, won it, and now imposed on Germany a structure from which Austria was excluded
entirely. The older vision of a Germany that might include the Habsburg monarchy in a Grossdeutsch
union was dead, buried at Königgrätz and Prague.

## The Italian Settlement

Italy, Prussia's ally, also made peace. Though beaten at Custoza and Lissa, Italy received Venetia —
occupied "without a struggle" once Prussia had won — and an uneasy Italian claim to Welschtirol
(Trentino) was refused. The Italian government, irritated at the five-day armistice that Bismarck
had concluded without its consent and disappointed at being denied Trentino, chafed under the
settlement, but had little choice but to accept what Prussia had won for it.

Bismarck, unruffled by the Italian complaints, held firm that Prussia had neither made armistice nor
peace without Italy's being consulted, only granting a cessation of hostilities in order to
negotiate; and if Prussian troops rested five days, he observed, they did no more than the Italian
army had done for weeks after Custoza. Italy, in the end, took Venetia and made its peace, and the
Italian theatre was closed.

## Consequences

The Peace of Prague transformed the map and constitution of Germany. With Austria excluded and the
Confederation gone, Prussia proceeded to construct the North German Confederation, the Prussian-led
federal state that became the scaffolding of the eventual German Empire. The foundations of the new
Reich, as Sybel titles his final book, were laid directly upon the peace, and the work of Prussian
unification that Bismarck had begun in 1862 reached its constitutional fulfilment.

The peace also left Austria humiliated but intact. Excluded from Germany and stripped of Venetia,
the Habsburg monarchy turned inward to rebuild its internal structure, and the task of reconciling
the empire to its expulsion fell to the *Ausgleich* of the following year — the Compromise with
Hungary that rebuilt the monarchy as Austria-Hungary. The settlement of 1866, moreover, created the
very conditions of the next great war: Prussia's new pre-eminence in Germany, sealed at Prague, was
the challenge that brought the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 and, with it, the proclamation of the
German Empire at Versailles.
