# Battle of Custoza (24 June 1866)

**June 24, 1866**

## Background

The Battle of Custoza was the great Italian reverse of the Austro-Prussian War. Italy had entered
the war in April 1866 through the alliance that Otto von Bismarck negotiated with the Kingdom of
Italy, bound by the expectation of winning Venetia from the Habsburgs. When war came in June, the
Italian army advanced into the Venetian provinces against the Austrian forces in Italy, which were
commanded by the Archduke Albrecht, a capable and aggressive Habsburg field commander who had been
given a free hand to defend the Venetian frontier.

The Italian army was under General Alfonso La Marmora, a veteran of the Risorgimento campaigns.
Sybel's account of the campaign is unsparing about the Italian operational failures: the army was
cumbersome in its movements, uncertain in its purpose, and poorly coordinated across its advancing
columns. The plan of operations called for the Italians to seize the initiative and overwhelm the
smaller Austrian forces in Venetia, but the execution was hesitant and the columns became separated
as they pushed toward the frontier. Against the Archduke Albrecht's concentrated and aggressive
Austrians, these weaknesses were to prove fatal.

## The Battle of 24 June

On 24 June 1866 the two armies met at Custoza, in the hills near the Venetian frontier. The
Austrians, seizing the initiative, fell upon the separated Italian columns and defeated them
piecemeal, striking each in turn before it could be supported. The Archduke Albrecht's victory was
thorough and well-earned; Sybel records it as a decisive Austrian success that vindicated Austrian
arms in the Italian theatre even while the main war was going badly elsewhere on the map of Europe.

La Marmora's army was thrown back with heavy losses, and for the moment it could no longer press the
invasion of Venetia. The defeat was a humiliation for the young Italian kingdom, and it exposed the
gulf between Italian national aspirations and Italian military performance. The army that had been
expected to overrun the Venetian provinces was instead routed on the first major encounter of the
war, and the Italian campaign collapsed into passivity.

## Aftermath: An Army That Did Nothing

The consequences of Custoza were felt for the rest of the war. Defeated, the Italian army fell back
and did not seriously renew its offensive against the Austrians in Venetia. Sybel later underlined
the point through the mouth of Bismarck: when Italy complained that Prussia had granted Austria a
five-day armistice without consulting her, Bismarck retorted that if Prussian troops rested for five
days, they did no more than the Italian army had done for weeks after Custoza. The barb was sharp
and it was deserved; Italy had entered the war expecting to win Venetia cheaply, and instead had
been beaten in the field.

The failure fed a lasting bitterness within Italy. The Italian command had promised a swift victory
over the Austrians and had delivered a defeat, and the contrast with the Prussian triumphs in
Bohemia was galling. National pride, wounded at Custoza, would be wounded again a month later at sea
off Lissa, and the double reverse left an enduring scar on the self-image of the new kingdom.

## Consequences

Custoza did not, in the end, deny Italy its prize. The great Prussian victories in Bohemia —
culminating at Königgrätz on 3 July — decided the war on the northern front, and Austria, crushed by
Prussia, was in no position to hold Venetia against Italy in the south. Italy occupied the province
"without a struggle," as Sybel put it, crossing its frontiers once Prussia had won, and the peace
settlement that followed the war ceded Venetia to Italy. In the final distribution of territory,
Custoza proved a defeat without consequences.

Yet the memory of Custoza outlasted the war. The Risorgimento, which had reached its greatest
heights under the leadership of Victor Emmanuel II and the patriots who had united Italy, now
carried the stain of a major military humiliation at the very moment the kingdom seemed about to
complete its work. The defeat sharpened the discontent with the performance of Italian arms that
would colour the country's politics for decades, and it fed the irredentist resentment against an
Austria that, though beaten by Prussia, had twice beaten Italy in the field and on the water.
