# The Battle of Sedan (1870)

**1 September 1870**

The decisive battle of the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]], fought on **1 September 1870** near the town of Sedan on the Belgian frontier. In it the French army of Châlons was encircled and destroyed, Napoleon III surrendered in person, and the defeat opened the "vacance du pouvoir" that brought down the [[second-empire-france]] and made way for the [[third-republic-france]].

## Background: Mac-Mahon's march to relieve Bazaine

After the defeats at [[battle-of-woerth-1870]] and Forbach in early August, the Emperor, Marshal Bazaine and the Army of the Rhine had been shut into Metz, besieged by the German armies. Marshal Mac-Mahon assembled a new army at Châlons to go to its relief. Hanotaux records the fateful decision: a telegram from Marshal Bazaine announcing a sortie toward Montmédy decided Mac-Mahon to march to the northeast and effect his junction. "The union of the two forces would have been formidable," he writes; "but the Prussian armies gained on Mac-Mahon." The German march was the faster, and the army of Châlons, instead of joining Bazaine, was driven toward the frontier and "went to be engulfed in the funnel of Sedan," where the Belgian border closed off retreat on one side and the surrounding heights on the others.

## The encirclement of 1 September

At Sedan Mac-Mahon's army, with the Emperor accompanying it, found itself caught in a pocket hemmed by the Meuse and the Belgian frontier. The German armies closed the ring during the night of 31 August and the morning of 1 September, and the battle that followed was less a battle than a siege. Ollivier, who had left office, records the event from the distance of his memoir-history: the hopelessness of the position, the heroic resistance, and the grim certainty that the whole army would have to await its fate. Hanotaux speaks of the army "écrasée" — crushed — "after a heroic resistance." Mac-Mahon was wounded and his command passed on; the army, cut off from Bazaine, from Paris and from the south, had no route of escape.

## The capitulation of Napoleon III

Seeing that further resistance could only end in useless slaughter, Napoleon III ordered the white flag raised and surrendered in person to the King of Prussia. The capitulation of the emperor and of an entire army was without precedent in the century: never had a reigning sovereign of France been taken on the field. Hanotaux draws the political meaning sharply — "A Sedan, Napoléon III ne rendit pas seulement lui-même prisonnier": at Sedan Napoleon III did not only make himself prisoner. The whole imperial edifice, which had rested on the glory and the person of the dynasty, was swept away with its sovereign. The capitulation handed the emperor into captivity at Wilhelmshöhe, from which he would pass into English exile.

## Consequences: the "vacance du pouvoir" and the fall of the Empire

The news of Sedan reached Paris on 2–3 September; Palikao, president of the council, announced the catastrophe and the captivity of the emperor to the Corps législatif. The defeat created, in Thiers's phrase which Hanotaux records, a "vacance du pouvoir" — a void of power. The regency conferred on the Empress had delegated only part of the authority, and the sovereign himself was a prisoner; of the assent of all, the catastrophe opened a governmental crisis. On **4 September 1870** the Republic was proclaimed in Paris and a Government of National Defence established, ending the Second Empire. The war, however, went on: Paris would be besieged through the winter, and the peace that followed — the [[treaty-of-frankfurt-1871]] — would exact the loss of Alsace and Lorraine. Sedan thus stands as the hinge of the whole period: the single battle that destroyed a dynasty, created the Third Republic, and made possible the proclamation of the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]].
