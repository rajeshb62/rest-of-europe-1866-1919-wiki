# The October Manifesto (1905)

**17 October 1905**

The manifesto "On the Improvement of the State Order" (*Об усовершенствовании государственного порядка*), issued by [[nicholas-ii]] on 17 October 1905, which granted the empire's population the unshakable foundations of civil liberty and promised the broad participation of the people in the work of the State Duma. For all that was to follow, Witte judged it a document that "will constitute an epoch in the history of Russia," and it marks the hinge between the old [[autocracy-russia]] of the Romanovs and the experiment of [[duma-constitutionalism]] that ran, through every limit and evasion, to the eve of the world war.

## Background: the year of troubles

The manifesto was the culminating act of the [[revolution-1905]] that had convulsed the empire through the year. In Witte's account the sovereign and his government were pushed step by step from one concession to the next. The catastrophe of "Bloody Sunday" on 9 January, when workers marching to the Winter Palace to present a petition were fired upon, had broken the tsar's hold on the loyalty of the labouring masses. The defeats of the [[russo-japanese-war-1904-05]] and the annihilation of the fleet at Tsushima had discredited the state before the army and the nation. The assassination of the interior ministers, the attempt on the life of Bogolepov and then of Sipyagin, and the harsh course of Plehve had left the government without able men. After Plehve's death the "spring" of Svyatopolk-Mirsky promised concessions, and the *ukase* of 12 December 1904 spoke of the establishment of legality. In February 1905 came a manifesto on disorders and troubles and the *ukase* to Bulygin on drawing in "the best people" to a consultative assembly.

The autumn of 1905 was the breaking point. The peace of Portsmouth, concluded with the mediation of the American president in September, had been Witte's own triumph, and he returned to Petersburg at the height of his prestige. But the war's end did not quiet the country; instead the revolutionary movement surged forward, crowned by the great political strike of October that paralysed the railways, the factories and the universities. The government had to choose between the fullest repression, which threatened civil war, and a grant of constitutional liberties. Witte, who had for years urged the necessity of a constitutional order as the only alternative to revolution, carried the day. It was in this atmosphere of strike and uncertainty that the decision for the manifesto was made.

## The eve and the drafting

Witte's own account dwells on the confused and half-secret preparation. On the eve of 17 October he conferred with the moderate figures of Petersburg society — the conversations of the Naryshkina circle and others — sounding out the ground for a constitutional reform. The sovereign hesitated at Peterhof, weighing the competing counsels of the conservatives, who saw in any concession the end of the monarchy, and of those, like Witte, who insisted that a constitution alone could save the dynasty and the state. Witte prepared a report as chairman of the Committee of Ministers, setting out the necessity of civil liberty and of a legislative assembly, and pressed it upon the emperor. The report was submitted with the imperial inscription "accept for guidance," and the manifesto was drawn in its spirit. In Witte's telling the whole thing moved with extraordinary speed: the decision once taken, the text of the manifesto was issued on 17 October, and the report was published alongside it.

## The text of 17 October

The manifesto opened with the sorrow of the sovereign before the *смута* — "the disorders and troubles in the capitals and in many parts of the empire fill our heart with great and heavy grief." The good of the Russian sovereign, it declared, was inseparable from the good of the people, and the people's grief was his own. From the troubles that had arisen might come a deep disorder of the nation and a threat to the integrity and unity of the all-Russian realm; the great vow of the tsar's service commanded the sovereign to strive with all the forces of reason and power toward the speedy ending of so dangerous a disorder. It then laid down three imperatives upon the government:

1. To grant the population the unshakable foundations of civil liberty, "on the basis of real inviolability of the person, freedom of conscience, speech, assembly and association."
2. Without halting the projected elections to the State Duma, to draw into participation in the Duma those classes of the population which were then wholly deprived of electoral rights, leaving the further development of universal suffrage to the newly established legislative order.
3. To establish as an unshakable rule that no law could take force without the approval of the State Duma, and that the people's representatives should be secured a real share in supervising the legality of the acts of the authorities.

The manifesto closed by calling on all faithful sons of Russia to remember their duty to the fatherland and to help end the unheard-of *смута*, straining with the sovereign to restore tranquillity and peace to the native land. It was a document that acknowledged the reality of revolution while invoking the sacred bond of tsar and people — the voice of autocracy trying to speak in the language of the constitutional state.

## The new government

The manifesto alone did not establish the new order; that required the machinery of a responsible government, and here Witte took the decisive step. On 19 October, two days after the manifesto, was published the law "On Measures to Strengthen the Unity of the Activity of the Ministers and Chief Administrations," which created a true Council of Ministers, and on the same day Witte was appointed its first prime minister — a cabinet premier with a right of influence over all the ministers, save only the war, naval and, in some degree, the interior ministries. The published report set out the programme of the new cabinet: the consolidation of the empire, the conduct of elections, the establishment of the Duma and of the civil liberties promised. For a half-year Witte was, in effect, the first constitutional prime minister of Russia, the man called upon to reconcile the promise of 17 October with the survival of the monarchy.

## Consequences: the half-year premiership

The manifesto split the liberal and revolutionary camps. For the Constitutional Democrats and the moderates it was the threshold of a constitutional order, the "constitution" they had demanded; for the socialists and much of the street it was an evasion to be carried further by force. The revolutionary movement, far from disarming, redoubled in the weeks that followed: Witte governed amid "complete confusion" of the empire, the disorders of the countryside and the punitive expeditions of the winter of 1905–06, while the Social Democrats and the Socialist-Revolutionaries held to the call for a constituent assembly and the abolition of private property in land. The government answered with both repression and concession: the amnesty, the electoral law, and the preparation of the Fundamental Laws of 1906, which defined the powers of the new Duma within the framework of an autocracy that still claimed the title of "supreme self-rule."

To liquidate the costs of the war and to restore the finances shaken by the revolution, Witte raised the great foreign loan of 1906 — the loan that, in his own estimate and that of his critics, enabled the monarchy to survive its first constitutional crisis. He also presided over the resumption of orderly government: the settlement of the Finnish question, the beginnings of the electoral machinery. In April 1906, worn down by the right's hostility and the left's intransigence, he resigned. The First Duma met that spring under his successors as a "Duma of popular retribution" or of inexperience, and was dissolved within months; after the Second Duma came the state coup of 3 June 1907, which altered the electoral law under [[pyotr-stolypin]] and narrowed the franchise. Yet the structure the manifesto had set — an elected legislature with a voice over the law and the budget — outlived Witte's fall. Strained, limited, and repeatedly evaded, it endured through the Stolypin years as the fragile constitutionalism that the world war would finally break.
