# The July Crisis (1914)

**28 June – early August 1914**

The five weeks between the shots at Sarajevo and the outbreak of the world war — the diplomatic storm in which Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, the German assurance of support, Russia's mobilisation, and the cascade of declarations of war carried Europe from a Balkan dispute to a general conflagration. It is the culminating event of the period's eastern crisis, the point at which the [[balkan-policy-austria]] of the Habsburg Monarchy and the [[eastern-question]] as a whole detonated into the [[battle-of-galicia-1914]], the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]] and four years of war. Both the diplomat [[ottokar-czernin]] and the chief of the general staff [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]] left retrospective accounts, written from the inside and at close distance.

## Background: the ultimatum and the German assurance

The murder of [[franz-ferdinand]] on 28 June supplied the Monarchy with the occasion it had long sought. The conviction at Vienna, voiced by Conrad on the very day, was that the crime was the war-declaration of Serbia and could only be answered by war; the decision to press Serbia to account was taken, and Count [[leopold-berchtold]] prepared an ultimatum. Behind it stood the assurance of Germany. Czernin records that Berchtold was fortified through the German ambassador, Heinrich von Tschirschky, in the belief that a war against France and Russia would end victoriously — the blank cheque of Berlin, as the phrase ran — and that Berchtold himself, as Czernin judged, hoped to the last to win a diplomatic success without war, "to win the victory with the pen," while reckoning on Germany's backing if Serbia refused. The Hungarian minister-president, Count [[stephen-tisza]], opposed the sharp ultimatum outright, foreseeing a war he did not want; but the current ran against him.

## The ultimatum and the partial acceptance

The ultimatum was delivered to Belgrade in the last week of July, demanding satisfaction for the Sarajevo crime in terms no sovereign state could accept in full. Serbia's reply, in Czernin's summary, accepted the ultimatum only incompletely. On **25 July** — the date Conrad gives for the outbreak against Serbia — the Monarchy broke off diplomatic relations and prepared to move; the declaration of war against Serbia followed, and the Serbian war had begun. Russia, as everyone had foreseen, did not stand aside. Czernin insists that Berchtold can have had no doubt that a Serbian war would draw in Russia: his own brother's reports from Petersburg had left none. With the Serbian war declared, Russia mobilised and intervened by arms.

## Grey's mediation and the failure of the last hour

In the final days of July the Powers struggled to arrest the avalanche. On 30 July the German ambassador laid before Berchtold a telegram from the German ambassador in London, Lichnowsky, conveying Sir Edward Grey's proposal of a four-power mediation, on the assumption that Austria-Hungary, after occupying Belgrade, would state its terms. Grey warned privately that an Austro-Russian war would permit England's neutrality, but that the situation would change if Germany and France were drawn in; England's public opinion, so favourable to Austria after the murder, was beginning to turn at Austria's obstinacy. On the 31st came the telegram of King George V to Prince Henry of Prussia, urging that Austria be persuaded to content itself with the occupation of Belgrade and neighbouring territory as a hostage for a satisfactory settlement. Vienna accepted these proposals only on conditions of military caution, and that conditional acceptance did not satisfy London. The hours slipped by unused; events outstripped the diplomats.

## Russia's mobilisation and the outbreak of the war

The turning point, in Czernin's account, was Russia. A powerful party in Petersburg was at work to force the war at any price; the Russian entry created a *fait accompli*, and Vienna, in the last moment, dared not stop its own mobilisation for fear of arriving too late in its own defence. Hostilities, Czernin notes, had begun without the will of the Tsar, and Russia, having created the *Zwangslage*, the compulsion, had "unleashed the world war." On the evening of 30 July the order for the general mobilisation of the Monarchy was to be laid before the Emperor; once Russia had moved, Austria's own mobilisation and the German entry followed as a chain. Czernin, writing after the catastrophe, held that the war was unleashed by Russia, and counted Austria-Hungary's greatest misfortune the German invasion of Belgium, which gave England its ground to enter — the decision that on the night of 4 August settled England's part in the war.

For the general staff the crisis was above all the hour of decision against Russia. Conrad records the extension of the Danube line as a fortified central zone, the deployment of the army against Serbia and Russia, and the farewell audience with [[franz-joseph]] on 15 August before the army high command left Vienna for the Russian theatre. The war that had been dreaded and prepared for a generation had come, and the machinery of the [[war-plans-1914]] — the Austro-German deployment, the expected decision in the west — now began to move.

## Consequences: the two-front war

The July crisis ended in the general war. The Monarchy's forces were committed at once on two fronts — against Serbia, where the opening operations were launched, and against Russia in Galicia, where Conrad's armies marched to the disastrous [[battle-of-galicia-1914]]. In the west the German advance met the French and British at the [[battle-of-the-marne-1914]], whose failure, as Conrad argued in his *Vorwort*, was the true decision of the war: it was the signal for Italy and Rumania to seek the Entente. The failure to localise the conflict, which [[franz-joseph]] had so anxiously hoped for, had plunged the Monarchy into the struggle that would end in its dissolution. The July crisis thus marks the transition from the age of Balkan crises to the age of world war.
