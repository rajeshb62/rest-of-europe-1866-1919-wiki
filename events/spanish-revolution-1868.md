# The Spanish Revolution (1868)

**September 1868**

The revolution that, in the third week of September 1868, drove Queen Isabella II from the Spanish throne, and thereby opened the vacancy which the courts of Europe spent the next two years trying to fill. It is in the frame of this corpus the decisive European event of 1868: the flight of the Bourbons at Madrid left a throne without a king, and the search for a successor — pursued across Portugal, France and finally the German houses — led directly to the [[hohenzollern-candidature-1870]] and the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]].

## Background: the unpopular Queen and the generals

Ollivier's memoir-history gives a full portrait of Isabella II and of the causes of her ruin. A queen whose reign had been a long alternation of courts and pronunciamientos, she had worn out the loyalty of the army and of the political factions alike. The two great liberal leaders, Marshal Prim and Marshal O'Donnell, had successively raised their revolts under the conservative governments of Narváez and O'Donnell; the death of O'Donnell, the reactionary ministry of González Bravo, the arrest of Marshal Serrano and the exile of the duc de Montpensier completed the alienation. Prim's ambition — a republican of convenience who had served every government in turn — was to place the country under a new régime of which he should be the master. By 1868 the conspiracy against the Queen had been long in the making.

## The September rising and the battle of Alcolea

The revolution broke out in the fleet and spread to the land. Ollivier recounts how the conspiracy of the admiral Topete, joined by Prim, raised the navy at Cadiz in September 1868; the rising, once begun, carried the revolutionary generals with it, and on the march toward Madrid the forces of the Queen were met and beaten at the battle of **Alcolea**. Isabella, abandoned, fled across the frontier into France. Marshal Serrano entered Madrid at the head of the revolution, and Prim, in the words of the cry that gave the movement its temper, raised the slogan *¡Abajo los Borbones!* ("Down with the Bourbons!"). The dynasty was dethroned; the throne stood empty.

## The search for a king

The revolutionaries, though united against Isabella, were divided over what should replace her. As Ollivier records, the democrats would not give up their republic, while the Unionists and the Progressives insisted on a monarchy — but of whom? The Unionists had a candidate ready in the duc de Montpensier, husband of the Infanta Luisa and himself a liberal prince in disgrace at the court; the Progressives looked elsewhere. The search ranged over the princes of Europe. Ferdinand of Portugal was first approached and refused; the duc de Montpensier was pressed but could not carry the unanimity of the movement; princes of Italy were explored. As the months passed without a king, the provisional governments of Marshal Serrano and of Marshal Prim, both monarchist, grew more importunate. It was in the course of this hunt that the first hints of a Hohenzollern candidacy appeared — a suggestion which Prince Anthony of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen at first rejected, because, as he is reported to have said, France would not tolerate it. Prim's patience was running out.

## France, Napoleon and the vacant throne

From Paris the revolution was watched with uneasiness and then with a policy of wait-and-see. Ollivier records Napoleon III's declared abstention: the Emperor would recognise any government born of popular election — even one dominated by Montpensier — and inclined rather to the restoration of Alfonso, the Queen's son, as the reasonable solution. But the abiding French anxiety was that a hostile prince on the throne of Madrid would place a foreign influence on both flanks of France. Europe, Ollivier notes, attributed a hidden hand in the Spanish revolution to [[otto-von-bismarck]] — a suspicion which the Prussian statesman, in a conversation with the English statesman Clarendon, disclaimed. Prussia had drawn the line at the Main, and Napoleon had reiterated that Prussia would not be allowed to cross it; but the Spanish vacancy offered a field in which that line could be turned. The ultimate irony of the whole affair, which the sources preserve from opposite sides, is that the candidate who finally emerged — a prince of the Hohenzollern house, seated at Madrid through Prussian influence — was precisely the danger that French policy had feared from the start.

## Consequences

The Spanish revolution left a monarchy without a king, and that vacancy became the fuse of the greatest war of the century. The search which Prim and the provisional governments pursued so impatiently ended, in the summer of 1870, in the acceptance of Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen — the [[hohenzollern-candidature-1870]] — whose revelation in July 1870 precipitated the French declaration of war and the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]]. What began as the fall of an unpopular queen in September 1868 thus issued, through the empty throne of Spain, in the destruction of the [[second-empire-france]] and the proclamation of the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]]. In the Spanish frame itself the revolution opened the interregnum that ended only with the election of King Amadeo of Savoy in 1870 and, after his abdication, the short-lived First Republic — but the decisive consequence for the rest of Europe lay in the candidacy to which the vacant throne gave rise.
