# The October Revolution (1917)

**25 October 1917**

The seizure of power in Petrograd by the Bolsheviks of [[vladimir-lenin]] on the night of 25 October (7 November) 1917, the overthrow of the Provisional Government of [[alexander-kerensky]], and the establishment of the government of the Soviets — followed within months by the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly and the beginnings of one-party rule. Denikin, a prisoner at Bykhov through these weeks, recorded both the strange ease of the coup and the fatal consequences of the "non-resistance" that had preceded it.

## Background: the drift to the coup

The [[kornilov-affair-1917]] had been the turning point. In destroying the Supreme Commander and the command, Kerensky had disarmed the state; Denikin's verdict was that the "victory" over Kornilov was the "prelude to Bolshevism." By the autumn the government was left solitary — "an army without leaders, administration without an army," he wrote — while the soviets, step by step, gathered the real power into their hands. The failure of the summer, the collapse of the front before the German occupation of the Moonsund islands, the starvation of the cities and the ruin of transport, all discredited the Provisional Government. The condition of the army in September and October, undermined by the soldiers' committees and by the war ministry's "adaptation to soviet moods," left no force on which the government could rely.

The Bolsheviks, alone among the parties, had drawn the lesson of the summer. In the first period of the revolution, to October, their aim had been to break the "bourgeois order" and to disorganise the army; now they turned to the seizure of power itself. Their two great slogans — peace and land — and their demand for the transfer of all power to the soviets carried them into the ranks of the garrison, the workers and the peasantry. Lenin, returned from exile in April, had set the party on this course; his theses on arrival, quoted by Denikin, marked the break with the moderate socialists. The soviet democracy, which had declined power in February, found itself outflanked by a party that would take it.

## The coup

The coup itself was almost bloodless. In the early hours of 25 October the Bolshevik committees, the Red Guards and the revolutionary garrison seized the key points of the capital — the stations, the bridges, the telegraph, the banks; the Winter Palace, where the Provisional Government sat, was taken that night with the arrest of the ministers. Kerensky fled Petrograd to raise troops, but the garrison refused to fight, and the "Gatchina" episode — the last attempt of his dictatorship — ended in his flight abroad and the final collapse of his authority. Denikin stressed the causes of the astonishing "non-resistance": the universal fatigue, the seduction of the slogans of land and peace, and the discipline of a small, organised minority against a disorganised majority. Lenin himself, Denikin recalled, had earlier quoted the 1905 lesson of the impossibility of immediate socialism; yet now the party seized the state. At Stavka and at Bykhov, where the Kornilov officers lay under arrest, the reaction was one of fatalism and disarray; the command, like the government, had no answer to a coup born of the very disorder it had been powerless to cure.

## The new regime and the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly

In the first days the new government of the Soviets — the Sovnarkom, the Council of People's Commissars, under Lenin — issued a torrent of decrees: the armistice and the call for peace, the nationalisation of the land, workers' control, and the self-determination of peoples. Many, even among its own opponents, believed the regime would not last two weeks; Denikin recorded that general expectation of ephemerality. But the soviets, and the committees, proved unexpectedly tenacious. The old army dissolved into the countryside as the soldiers answered the land decree and the call for peace, and the regime, far from collapsing, consolidated its hold on the capital and then on the provinces.

The decisive test came with the Constituent Assembly, for which elections had been held in November and which was to decide the constitution of Russia. The Bolsheviks had won only a minority of the seats; rather than surrender power to a body dominated by the Socialist-Revolutionaries, they dispersed the Assembly by force when it met in January 1918 — the suppression of the one body in which the democratic revolution had placed its hopes, and the moment that made clear the coup was not a revolution of the people but the rule of a party. The assassination of the former supreme commander Dukhonin, murdered by the mob when the new government turned against the old Stavka, marked the same abandonment of the forms of the fallen order. From Bykhov the imprisoned officers watched these events with a growing conviction that the regime would neither fall of itself nor be removed by any legal means.

## Consequences

The October coup transformed the character of the Russian revolution and of the whole war. In foreign policy it led directly to the separate peace of the [[treaty-of-brest-litovsk-1918]], which took Russia out of the world war and released German divisions for the west; in internal affairs it began the suppression of all rival parties and the concentration of power in the Bolshevik Central Committee. The decrees on land, on workers' control and on national self-determination dissolved the old bonds of the empire and set the borderlands into motion, beginning the civil conflicts of the south and the west.

For the officers imprisoned at Bykhov, the coup was the signal for escape: released amid the collapse of the old Stavka, Kornilov, Denikin and their comrades made their way to the Don, where [[mikhail-alexeyev]] had already begun the "Alekseev organisation" that would become the [[white-movement]]'s Volunteer Army. The February revolution's promise of a democratic [[duma-constitutionalism]] had ended; in its place arose the dictatorship of the proletariat, and with it the civil war that was to consume Russia for three years. The [[ice-march-1918]] was the first fruit of that struggle, and the whole of the White resistance its prolongation.
