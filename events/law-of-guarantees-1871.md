# The Law of Guarantees (13 May 1871)

**13 May 1871**

The Italian law of guarantees — *la legge delle guarentigie* — enacted on 13 May 1871 to regulate the position of the Pope after the loss of Rome, defining the status of the Holy See and of the Papacy within a city that had become the capital of the Kingdom of Italy. The measure answered the problem that the [[capture-of-rome-1870|occupation of Rome]] had raised: how the spiritual head of the Catholic Church could live and govern under Italian sovereignty. It was framed unilaterally by the Italian parliament and never accepted by the Pontiff, whose refusal to treat with the new order kept the [[roman-question]] alive for another half-century.

## Background

The taking of Rome on 20 September 1870 had stripped the Papacy of its temporal dominion and left the Pope resident in a city under the authority of the Italian crown. The problem was acute: the Holy See had enjoyed, as a temporal sovereign, the independence that a head of a universal Church requires, and the new kingdom had to decide what standing the Pope should hold within its own territory. The ministry of Giovanni Lanza and Quintino Sella, which had carried out the occupation, was charged with the settlement. Oriani treats the measure as the necessary sequel of the annexation — the completion, in law, of the monarchical solution of the Roman problem that he narrates in *La lotta politica in Italia* from the September Convention onward.

## The terms of the law

The Law of Guarantees, enacted on 13 May 1871, was a unilateral law of the Italian state, not a treaty with the Holy See. It conceded to the Pope the inviolability of his person, the honours of a sovereign, and the right to maintain his own diplomatic representatives, while reserving to the kingdom the general law of the land. It granted the Pope an annual allowance in compensation for the revenues of the Papal States, and secured the extraterritoriality of the Vatican, the Lateran and the papal residence at Castel Gandolfo — the buildings of the Holy See left outside the ordinary operation of Italian law. The idea was to preserve for the Papacy the freedom and independence of a sovereign power without the territorial dominion it had lost, in a kind of personal and spiritual sovereignty over a limited, guaranteed space within the capital.

## The refusal of the Pope

The law was never accepted by the Holy See. The Pope refused to recognise the Italian seizure of Rome and to treat the unilateral Italian statute as a sufficient or legitimate guarantee of his independence, holding that the loss of temporal sovereignty could not be repaired by a concession of the very state that had despoiled him. In the years that followed this refusal took institutional form in the Pope's rule of *Non expedit* — "it is not expedient" — by which Italian Catholics were counselled against participation in the political life of the kingdom that had taken Rome. The practical effect was that the [[roman-question]], though solved as a territorial matter by the capture of the city, remained an open wound in the relations between the Italian state and the Church throughout the period that this corpus covers, down to the very threshold of the twentieth century.

## Consequences

The Law of Guarantees settled in law the Pope's position inside Italy but left the moral and religious question unresolved, for the Pope's refusal made the statute a dead letter in the eyes of the Holy See. The division it embodied would persist as a standing feature of Italian public life — a church standing apart from the state, an anti-clerical current in politics, and a population whose devout loyalty to the Pope was set against the claims of the kingdom. In the longer run the guarantees prepared the ground on which a later settlement would have to be built, but within the years of this wiki the law stands as the Italian state's answer to the [[roman-question]] — an answer that the Pope, by his *Non expedit*, declined to accept.
