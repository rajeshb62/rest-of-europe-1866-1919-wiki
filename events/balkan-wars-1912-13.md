# The Balkan Wars (1912–13)

**October 1912 – August 1913**

The two wars that redrew the map of the Balkans on the eve of the Great War: the war of the Balkan League against the Ottoman Empire in the autumn and winter of 1912–13, and the inter-allied war of the summer of 1913 in which the victors turned upon one another over the spoils. Together they destroyed the Turkish dominion in Europe almost at a stroke and doubled the size of Serbia — the very change that set Austria-Hungary upon the road to Sarajevo and the July crisis of 1914. Sosnosky, closing his two-volume account of the Habsburg Balkan policy at the Peace of Bukarest, gave the wars their name in the historical literature of the Monarchy: *die Balkan-Katastrophe*, the Balkan catastrophe.

## Background: the Eastern Question comes to a head

The wars were the eruption of the [[eastern-question]] in its most violent form yet. Through the nineteenth century the Powers had agreed only to keep the sick man of Europe on his feet; by 1911–12 that concert had broken down. The Italo-Turkish war over Tripoli (1911) taught the small Balkan states that the Porte could be attacked with impunity, and the Austro-Russian *Mürzsteg* agreement over Macedonia, long since dead in practice, had left the peninsula without a policeman. Under the chancellorship of Count [[leopold-berchtold]] the Monarchy still clung to the doctrine of the status quo — that nothing on the Balkan should be changed without the agreement of the Great Powers — while Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro prepared to change everything. Sosnosky describes the league that now took shape under the title "strange allies": Bulgaria, the most powerful of the four, joined with its hereditary rivals Serbia and Greece, and with Montenegro, in a compact whose only real cement, as he judged it, was the appetite for the Turkish inheritance. The watchword raised was *"Der Balkan den Balkanvölkern"* — "the Balkans for the Balkan peoples" — the slogan under which the league resolved to exclude both the Porte and the tutelage of the Powers.

## The first war: the league against Turkey

In October 1912 the Balkan League opened war upon the Ottoman Empire. The Turkish armies, shaken by the defeat in Tripoli and commanded by generals who had lost their nerve, collapsed with astonishing speed. Sosnosky records how the allied successes exceeded every expectation: the Turks were driven out of Macedonia, Thrace and Albania; Adrianople and Scutari were besieged and taken. Within a few weeks the whole Turkish position in Europe was overthrown, and only the Gallipoli peninsula and the line *Enos–Midia* — the line the Powers strove to impose as the limit of the allied conquest — remained to the Sultan. The Porte, whose empire had once ended at the gates of Vienna, was glad to keep a scrap of the Europe that had once trembled before it.

For Austria-Hungary the war was a catastrophe in prospect. Berchtold's status-quo policy was struck dead by the speed of the allied victories. The Monarchy had to reckon with a vastly enlarged Serbia at its frontier and, behind Serbia, Russia; the general staff under [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]] prepared for the possibility of warlike complications. Sosnosky is at pains to defend Berchtold's reserve in the crisis: his restraint over the Sandjak of Novi Pazar and over Albania was not, he argues, the paralysis it appeared, but a policy bound hand and foot by the alliance with Italy — which held the treaty right to demand the port of Valona as compensation should the Monarchy advance into Turkish territory — and by the reluctance of Berlin to be drawn into a Balkan war. The Monarchy, in Sosnosky's image, walked like a somnambulist along an abyss, assured by its own press that all was well with its allies while Italy's irredentist fever burned unquenched.

## The road to the peace of London

Through the winter of 1912–13 the Powers, meeting in the ambassadors' conference at London, strove to confine the war. The allies' demands were so severe — the cession of all Turkish territory northwest of the *Rodosto–Malatra* line, the Aegean islands, Crete, a war indemnity — that the Powers refused them and countered with their own terms, leaving the disposal of Albania and the islands to the Great Powers. Berchtold pressed unremittingly for a prompt peace, and Sir Edward Grey at last lost patience, declaring that only the delegates ready to sign at once could count on England's support. On the last days of May 1913 the preliminary peace of London was signed; the Ottoman dominion in Europe was formally ended, and the fate of Albania and the Aegean islands was reserved to the concert of the Powers.

## The second war: the allies fall out

The peace of London solved nothing. The only bond that had held the Balkan allies together was, as Sosnosky put it, *die Habsucht* — greed — and greed turned within the camp as soon as the Turkish lands were won. Serbia, denied Albania by the Monarchy's insistence, demanded compensation in Macedonia at Bulgaria's expense; Greece and Serbia, distrusting Bulgaria, arranged the partition of the conquered territory behind its back; the Serbo-Bulgarian quarrel over the partition of Macedonia, and the Greco-Bulgarian collisions at Saloniki, mounted through the spring of 1913 toward a second war. The Tsar, the nominal arbiter of the alliance, pleaded for peace; his telegram of 8 June to the kings of Bulgaria and Serbia failed to reconcile them. In the last days of June 1913 Bulgaria, whether by accident or design, attacked its former allies. The second Balkan War had begun.

The second war went against Bulgaria from the outset. Serbia and Greece, joined by the Turks who reconquered Adrianople, fell upon the exhausted Bulgarians; in July Rumania, which had held back on the advice of the Monarchy at the start of the first war, at last intervened and crossed the Danube into the Dobrudscha without firing a shot. Isolated and beaten on all sides, Bulgaria sued for peace. The treaty was signed at Bukarest on **10 August 1913**: Bulgaria lost the Dobrudscha to Rumania and much of Macedonia to Serbia and Greece, while the separate treaty of Constantinople of 29 September restored Adrianople and Kirkilisse to the Turks. The peace of Bukarest, in Sosnosky's judgment, laid not the foundation of a lasting settlement but the seeds of new conflict — "a dragons-seed that would come up sooner or later as evil harvest."

## Consequences: the enlarged Serbia and the road to 1914

The Balkan Wars transformed the position that had sustained the Monarchy since the Congress of Berlin. Serbia was nearly doubled in size and territory and had won enormous prestige among the South Slavs; the Habsburg monarchy, by contrast, emerged humiliated, isolated even within its own alliance, its treaty of Bukarest unrevised despite its protest. Austria-Hungary had created, out of the wreck of the Ottoman power, the independent state of Albania — an act Berchtold had felt compelled to perform because neither he nor Italy would suffer the other to possess it — and had thereby barred Serbia from the Adriatic, earning its hatred. King Franz Ferdinand's last attempts to secure Rumania's adherence, and the general staff's war preparations, came to nothing.

The consequence, as both Sosnosky and the diplomat [[ottokar-czernin]] record, was that the peace of Bukarest was generally recognised to be no peace at all. The aged [[franz-joseph]] told Czernin before he left for Bukarest that the treaty was untenable and that the Monarchy was going to a new war, hoping only that it would remain localised. The enlarged Serbia was far from satisfied and aspired to become a Great Power; the Monarchy, its prestige broken, was far from resigned. The two Balkan Wars had set the stage on which the shots of Sarajevo would fire the world war. They are the immediate prelude, in time and in temper, to the [[july-crisis-1914]], and the decisive demonstration of the failure of the Habsburg [[balkan-policy-austria]].
