# Hohenzollern Candidature (1870)

**1 July – 12 July 1870**

The offer of the vacant Spanish throne to Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, and the diplomatic crisis it provoked between France and Prussia in the first days of July 1870. It was the match that lit the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]], and the two great national histories of the corpus — Ollivier's French memoir and Sybel's German chronicle — recount it from opposite sides of the same events.

## Background: the vacant throne of Spain

The Spanish revolution of 1868 had driven Isabella II from the throne and left Spain in search of a king. Sybel recounts how the regency of Marshal Serrano and the government of Marshal Prim, both monarchists, sought a candidate across the courts of Europe; Prim successively explored a Portuguese candidate, the duc de Montpensier of the House of Orléans, and princes of Italy before turning, with growing impatience, to the German houses. Ollivier records the same search from Paris, where every rumour of a candidate hostile to French interests was watched with alarm. The prize was the strategic fact that a prince of the Prussian royal house seated at Madrid would place a Hohenzollern on both flanks of France. See [[spanish-revolution-1868]].

## Prim and the German negotiations

The decisive turn came when the Spanish emissary Salazar, with Prim's permission, carried the offer to Germany and, as Sybel narrates in detail, the candidacy of Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen took shape under the influence of Bismarck and the Prussian King. Sybel stresses the secrecy of the whole plan: it was arranged that the Cortes should be packed for an immediate vote so that France should be confronted with an accomplished fact. Leopold's acceptance was telegraphed from Madrid late in June 1870, and Prim, who had judged the plan already compromised by the delay, tried to recover it by diplomacy.

## The revelation and the French reaction

The secret broke in the last days of June and the first of July 1870. Ollivier describes the news bursting on Paris "like a bomb" on **3 July**: the acceptance was revealed in Madrid through Zorrilla to *la Época* before the Cortes could vote, and the French ambassador Mercier telegraphed the intelligence to Paris. The foreign minister Gramont informed the Emperor, and the government, in Ollivier's account, resolved at once to turn aside the candidacy. Indignation was universal in France — Ollivier lists Doudan, Jules Favre, Jules Simon, Thiers and Gambetta among those who condemned the Prussian move — and mirrored among foreign statesmen. The ministry, whose head Ollivier was, decided to confine the quarrel strictly to the Hohenzollern candidacy and to seek its withdrawal by diplomacy, rather than to reopen the older grievances of the Luxembourg and Rhine questions.

## The crisis unfolds

Against Prim's plan to have the Cortes elect Leopold on 20 July, the French cabinet acted on several fronts: a ministerial declaration read from the tribune on **6 July**, the negotiation at Ems with King Wilhelm through the ambassador Benedetti, approaches to the powers and to the Spanish regency, and finally the intervention of the prince's own family. On **11 July** Leopold's father, Prince Anthony of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen, renounced the candidacy on his son's behalf, and on **12 July** Leopold himself, after violent scenes with his father and under pressure from Prim's envoy, handed his own renunciation to the admiral. The candidacy was withdrawn. For Ollivier and the French ministry the affair appeared at that moment settled: the cause of quarrel had been removed, and the incident declared finished.

## Consequences

The withdrawal did not end the crisis but set the stage for its climax. In Paris the war party, discontented at having been robbed of its pretext, pressed for guarantees against the repetition of such an affair; the Emperor and Gramont, without Ollivier, decided on **12 July** to demand them, and on **13 July** Bismarck's falsification of the Ems telegram — the [[ems-dispatch-1870]] — turned the retreat of the candidacy into a public affront to France. The demand of guarantees and the *soufflet* together made war inevitable, and on 15 July France declared it. The Hohenzollern candidature thus ended where it had begun: not in the quiet withdrawal of a prince, but in the [[franco-prussian-war-1870-71]] that destroyed the [[second-empire-france]] and proclaimed the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]]. On the question of who had staged the affair, the sources divide — Ollivier holds Bismarck personally responsible for organising the candidacy; Sybel presents it as the convergence of Spanish search, Prussian dynastic interest and Bismarck's policy.
