# The February Revolution (1917)

**February – March 1917**

The fall of the Russian monarchy in the winter of 1917: the disorders of the Petrograd streets, the mutiny of the garrison, the abdication of [[nicholas-ii]], and the establishment of the double authority — *двоевластие* ("dual power") — of the Provisional Government and the Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies. Denikin, writing from the Staff of the Supreme Commander, saw in it the "inevitable historical process" that "led to the collapse of Russian statehood"; no one, he judged, had expected the elemental force to sweep away so quickly the supreme power, the intelligentsia, and the ten-million-strong army.

## Background: the collapse of authority

Denikin traced the ruin of the old order to its roots in the preceding decades. The [[autocracy-russia]] of the Romanovs rested on "faith, tsar and fatherland," but the defeat in the [[russo-japanese-war-1904-05]] had left a bitterness among the military youth and a paralysis at the top of the War Ministry and the General Staff. The world war that began in 1914 had then made the state's frailties mortal. In August 1915 [[nicholas-ii]] assumed the supreme command — in Denikin's judgment under the influence of the Empress's circles and of [[grigori-rasputin]], over the fruitless representations of eight ministers. From then the sovereign dwelt at Stavka in Mogilev "alone, without family, without intimates," while the capital slipped into the grip of ministers seen as captives of the Duma and the press.

Zhevakhov, writing from within the Holy Synod, registers the same decomposition from the church's side. He describes the corruption of the "mechanism" of power at the top, ministers in captivity to the Duma and the newspapers, and the contrast with the still-holding lower police. The murder of [[grigori-rasputin]] in December 1916 — the subject of his long excursus — he treats as the break toward catastrophe, the act that removed the last personal support of the imperial family while doing nothing to restore its authority. By the winter of 1916–17 the state presented the paradox of a government that could neither govern nor be changed by ordinary means: the Duma's speeches, above all Miliukov's famous attack of 1 November 1916 with its refrain "stupidity or treason," had exposed the court to the educated public, while the war's burdens had turned the mass of the people against the regime.

## The February days

The collapse came quickly and, to the great majority, unexpectedly. The winter of 1916–17 brought a food crisis to Petrograd: the supply of the capital broke down, the factories stopped, and the queues lengthened in the streets. On 23 February (8 March) strikes and demonstrations, prompted above all by the women's day and the bread queues, swelled into a general movement; by 26–27 February the garrison, called upon to fire on the crowds, instead refused and went over to them. The government of the aged interior minister Protopopov at first treated the movement as "popular disturbances" to be met by "decisive measures" — machine-guns were readied for Khabalov and a punitive detachment prepared under General Ivanov — but the troops of the capital melted into the revolution. The members of the State Council telegraphed on the night of 28 February of the disruption of transport, the factory stoppages, the food crisis, and the hatred of the government.

Zhevakhov, returned from a journey to the Caucasus on 24 February, records the "first steps of the revolution" — "the next day was still more terrible than the previous." On 26 February the Holy Synod held its memorable sitting at the very moment of the street's breaking; on 27 February came the round-ups, "the genuine picture of the revolution"; on 28 February "the triumph of the boor," as the author calls it, with the cabinet almost in full caught by events. On that day Zhevakhov himself was arrested and confined in the Ministerial Pavilion of the Duma with the other captured dignitaries of the fallen power. He describes the cross-section of the overthrown authority gathered there — ministers, senators, churchmen — and the rising fear of the prisoners as the revolution ran beyond all limits.

## The abdication

The fall of the dynasty was sealed at Pskov. The generals and the political leaders alike had concluded that the abdication was inevitable — "in all, a clearly conscious inevitability of abdication." Through the night the telegraph wires carried the conversations that decided the fate of the country: Ruzsky with Rodzianko and Alekseev, Stavka with the commanders-in-chief, Lukomsky with Danilov. On the morning of 2 March General Ruzsky laid before the sovereign the opinions of Rodzianko, the chairman of the Duma, and of the military commanders; the emperor listened calmly, his face as if frozen, and declared that the act of abdication in favour of his son was already signed, handing over the telegram. He had first hesitated on the report that the Duma delegates Guchkov and Shulgin were coming to Pskov, and then, in the evening, he changed the terms.

Guchkov, arriving among deep silence, drew the picture of the abyss toward which the country was moving and pointed to the only way out — abdication. The sovereign replied that he had made his decision, that he had been ready that afternoon to abdicate in favour of his son, "but then I understood that I am incapable of parting with my son. You will understand that, I hope. Therefore I have decided to abdicate in favour of my brother." The delegates, taken by surprise, did not protest: Guchkov out of the feelings of a father, unwilling to press the tsar to part with his son, and Shulgin on political grounds, doubting whether a regent could swear fidelity to a constitution on behalf of a minor emperor. Denikin dwelt on the thirty minutes that changed the course of events — a prosaic, everyday episode had altered the fate of the dynasty. The act was signed, and with it the three hundred years of the Romanovs and the whole frame of [[autocracy-russia]] came to an end.

## Dual power: the Provisional Government and the Soviet

In the place of the monarchy rose a divided authority. The Duma, seeing itself as an institution of the old order, did not formally reconvene; from its committees emerged the Provisional Government, headed by the liberal Prince Lvov, while the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies, born in the same days in the Tauride Palace, claimed the allegiance of the workers and of the mutinous garrison. Denikin quoted the characterisation of the Duma committee itself: the seizure of the rights of state power by irresponsible organisations, the creation of "dual power in the centre and anarchy in the country."

The Soviet, through its leading socialists, explicitly declined to take power itself. Its spokesman Tseretelli insisted that the moment had not come for the proletariat, that this was a bourgeois revolution whose final tasks were not yet to be attempted; rather than seize the state, the democratic leaders chose to press the government by constant organised pressure. Yet the Soviet would not relinquish its hold on the army and the workers, and from the first days it exercised a power that overlapped and undercut the government's. This unresolved contradiction, the *двоевластие*, defined the whole interval between the fall of the monarchy and the Bolshevik seizure in the autumn: no decree of the Provisional Government could command obedience against the will of the Soviet, and no power of the Soviet could govern the country.

## Consequences: the army and the road to October

The most fateful consequence for Denikin, a commander at the front, was the dissolution of the army. The garrison's mutiny was generalised by **Order No. 1** of the Soviet, which subordinated the armed forces to the revolutionary committees and struck a blow at discipline from which the army never recovered. Denikin learned of the events far from the capital, on the Romanian Front where he commanded the 8th Army Corps, "absorbed in its local cares," preparing a winter offensive. The news of the revolution and the abdication filtered through to men who had not been prepared for such a sudden denouement; "only here and there in the ranks" was the abdication known. Order No. 1, with its committees and its elected authority over the officers, completed what the revolution had begun: the ten-million-strong army dissolved into a debating society, and the front with it.

The Provisional Government, torn between the Soviet and the command, proved unable to restore either authority or supply. By spring the cities and the army were starving — Denikin records the official norms of bread that were never met — and the soldiers' committees paralysed the officers. Prince Lvov's government, and then that of [[alexander-kerensky]], wavered between the Allies' demand for an offensive and the army's refusal to fight; the "rusty, notched sword," Denikin wrote, was still swinging, but it could no longer strike. The failure of the promised June offensive and the collapse of the front in the summer would discredit the government and open the way first to the [[kornilov-affair-1917]] and then to the [[october-revolution-1917]]. Zhevakhov, released from arrest by Kerensky after the abdication, carried away the verdict of the Synod's circle — that what had swept the throne away was not so much a revolution as a soldier's mutiny — but the monarchy was gone beyond recall, and with it the last formal anchor of the old order.
