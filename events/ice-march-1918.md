# The First Kuban (Ice) March (1918)

**February – April 1918**

The retreat and forced march of the Volunteer Army from the Don into the Kuban in the winter–spring of 1918, the "first Kuban campaign" — the *Ледяной поход* ("Ice March") — that carried [[lavr-kornilov]]'s little army through snow and mud to the assault on Ekaterinodar, cost the life of its leader, and passed the command to [[anton-denikin]]. It was the founding ordeal of the [[white-movement]] in the south, and the act by which the volunteers first rose "in defence of Russia against the red scourge."

## Background: the army and the Don

After the [[october-revolution-1917]] the southern generals and the officers imprisoned at Bykhov had made their way to the Don. On 2 November [[mikhail-alexeyev]] reached Novocherkassk and began an armed force "destined to play so considerable a role"; there followed the arrival of Kornilov, the organisation of the Volunteer Army, and the attempt of the "triumvirate" of Alekseev, Kornilov and the ataman Kaledin to establish a Russian authority in the south. The volunteers were few — a handful of generals, officers and junkers, joined by some Cossacks and civilians — but they carried with them the whole moral weight of the officers' refusal to accept the revolution.

But the winter brought the collapse of the Don. The end of the old army and the beginning of the Red Guard transformed the region; the peasants and many of the Cossacks turned against the volunteer cause, and the Soviet armed forces pressed upon Rostov and Novocherkassk. The treaty of [[treaty-of-brest-litovsk-1918]] and the German advance in the west had drawn away the attention of the world, leaving the south to its own war. Denikin described the madness left in the wake of the retreat — the plunder, the murder of the wounded, the terror of the families — and the "new tragedy of the Russian officer corps" who had staked everything on the struggle. Kaledin, despairing, shot himself as the Don collapsed; the volunteers, driven from their bases, chose to march into the unknown rather than surrender.

## The march into the Kuban

The decision was taken at the military council at the stanitsa of Olginskaya: the army would abandon the Don and march south into the Kuban, the "promised land" of the Cossacks. On 23 February the volunteers entered the Kuban region, greeted, Denikin wrote, with bread and salt; but the promised rising of the Kuban did not come as hoped. The march was a running fight through a country already taken by the Bolsheviks. They fought at Berezanka, Vyselki and Korenovskaya, learned of the fall of Ekaterinodar to the Reds, and turned south.

The army moved by night through the flooded steppe, its column strung out along the roads and through the mud, its rear-guard under Bogaevsky pressed by the Kuban Bolsheviks, its headquarters improvised on the march. In the night of 8 March they crossed the River Laba, the junkers wading in water up to their chests, "blowing bubbles" in the cold. Day after day they threaded the encirclement of the khutors, and Denikin confessed to the "shady sides" of an army's life in extremity. The fighting was continuous: the Red forces surrounded them on every side, and each day brought its skirmish or its stand. Yet the army held together, bound by the discipline of its officers and by the sheer necessity of survival, and through it all the news of the fate of Ekaterinodar and of the Kuban detachment coloured their hopes.

## The Ice March

The ordeal earned its name on 15 March, in the fight at Novo-Dmitrievskaya — the "Ice March," the "glory of Markov and the Officer Regiment, the pride of the Volunteer Army, and one of the most vivid memories of every veteran of the first campaign." All the night before the rain had poured, and it continued into the morning; the army marched through stretches of water and liquid mud, on roads and off them, lost in the thick fog that lay over the land. The cold water soaked every garment and ran in sharp, piercing streams down the collars; the men went slowly, shuddering with cold and dragging their feet in swollen, water-logged boots. Toward noon came thick flakes of sticky snow and a wind that filled the eyes and nose and ears and took the breath.

Ahead of them a river barred the road, its far bank held by the Bolshevik outposts; the bridge was broken, and horsemen went to seek fords, some plunging with their horses into the icy water. The column crowded to the bank; the huts of a small farmstead beckoned with the smoke of their chimneys, and the men pressed into the steaming, crowded rooms to warm their frozen limbs. The crossing secured, the army pressed on, and at Novo-Dmitrievskaya the Officer Regiment under Markov won the fight that gave the whole march its name. There the treaty was concluded with the Kuban volunteer detachment, which joined its strength to the Volunteer Army, and the combined force turned on Ekaterinodar. The Ice March had become the badge of the first volunteers, the emblem of their endurance and their cause.

## The assault and the death of Kornilov

By 27 March the volunteers had reached the approaches of Ekaterinodar, where the Red defenders had gathered in force. Kornilov called a military council — the first since Olginskaya — and resolved on a decisive assault, though the enemy outnumbered them many times over and the army was exhausted, its units intermixed, its commander strength shattered by four days of battle, its shells and cartridges gone, and more than fifteen hundred wounded in the lazaret. At the council the generals drew a sad picture of the position; only Markov, weary from two sleepless days, dozed through it. Kornilov did not try to soften the picture, and was unmoved by the argument for withdrawing: "There is no other way out, Anton Ivanovich. If we do not take Ekaterinodar, I will have to put a bullet in my head."

On the morning of 31 March, while the army prepared the assault, the farmstead where the headquarters lay came under artillery fire. Kornilov was urged to move; "It is not worth it now; tomorrow the assault," he replied. In the shelling he was struck down. Denikin wrote in the volume's dedication: "On 31 March 1918 a Russian grenade, directed by the hand of a Russian man, struck down a great Russian patriot." The army that had lost its leader had also lost the chance of the city; the assault, launched without him, failed.

## Consequences: Denikin's command

The death of Kornilov left the army in crisis, but the struggle did not end. The chief of staff asked Denikin whether he would take command of the army, and he answered yes. He lifted the siege of Ekaterinodar and led the volunteers away through the fights at Gnachbau and Medvedovskaya, where the feat of General Markov — a sudden, bold counter-stroke that saved the column — passed into legend. Turning north and east, the army marched from Dyadkovskaya to Uspenskaya, bearing its wounded with it, living on the bread and salt of the stanitsa, before swinging back toward the Don amid the risings that had begun to break out against the Bolsheviks in the Cossack country.

By late April, with the arrival of the detachment of Colonel Drozdovsky and the appearance of the Germans on the Don, the volunteers were back in the Don country, the siege of Novocherkassk relieved and the army given a breathing-space. The struggle was not over but had been given a new form: the little army that had marched through the ice had become the nucleus of a movement. Denikin closed the account with the scale of what the first march had meant: "Two and a half years more their struggle lasted," and the survivors were scattered across the Slavic lands, the Allied camps, and the night shelters of the Old and New World — but the memory of those "who first rose in defence of Russia against the red scourge" would be kept while peace rested over the country.
