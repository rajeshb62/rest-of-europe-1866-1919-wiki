# The Sarajevo Assassination (1914)

**28 June 1914**

The murder of the heir to the Habsburg throne, Archduke [[franz-ferdinand]], and his wife Sophie, Duchess of Hohenberg, by the young Bosnian Serb Gavrilo Princip on the streets of Sarajevo — the pistol shots that, within five weeks, set Europe alight. The act itself was a single day's violence in a Bosnian capital; its consequences were the [[july-crisis-1914]] and the outbreak of the world war, and it is the hinge upon which the whole period turns. Both the chief of the general staff, [[conrad-von-hotzendorf]], and the diplomat [[ottokar-czernin]], who knew the heir and the monarchy, left accounts of the crime and of its meaning.

## Background: the archduke's journey to Bosnia

The assassination grew out of the settlement of the [[balkan-wars-1912-13]]. The Monarchy had barred the enlarged Serbia from the Adriatic by creating the state of Albania, and the South-Slav agitation, fed from Belgrade, burned against the Habsburg rule in Bosnia-Herzegovina. Into this tinder walked the heir. Franz Ferdinand had announced his intention to travel to Bosnia in the spring or summer of 1914, to attend the army manoeuvres of the 15th and 16th corps and to visit the capital. It was a gesture of sovereignty over the annexed province, and it was timed, as contemporaries noted, for *Vidovdan*, the Serbian national day of mourning for the battle of Kosovo.

The archduke travelled by sea to Metković and then by rail through Mostar into the Herzegovina; the Duchess came overland by way of Bosnisch-Brod. The pair lodged at the spa of Ilidže, nine kilometres west of Sarajevo, where the general staff chief Conrad, with his officers Metzger and Kundmann, had arrived on 24 June for the manoeuvres. Conrad records the ease of those days — the informal suppers, the archduke's charm, the imperial couple's joy at news of their eldest son's examination results — and the extensive measures taken for the safety of the guests, an infantry detachment mounting the guard. The manoeuvres of 26 and 27 June passed without incident; on the evening of the 27th Conrad took his leave, the archduke bidding him "auf Wiedersehen bei den Manövern" — words, Conrad wrote, that were the last he was ever to address to him.

## The day: Appelkai and the Lateiner bridge

The 28th of June was set aside for the ceremonial visit to Sarajevo, the seat of the government of Bosnia. The archduke's motor car, with the Duchess, was followed by a second car carrying Count Harrach and other members of the suite, and by a third in which sat Colonel Bardolff and his officers; Field Marshal Potiorek, the governor and army inspector, rode with the imperial couple. As the procession drove along the Appelkai quay toward the town hall, a young man hurled a bomb at the archduke's car. It struck the folded roof, rolled off, and exploded beneath the following car, wounding Colonel von Merizii in the head and several other officers; the imperial couple, save for a graze on the Duchess's neck, were unhurt. The first assassin, Cabrinović, was seized across the Miljačka river.

The archduke was deeply shaken. At the town hall, receiving the mayor, he complained of the attack and asked Potiorek whether the drive should continue, and whether it would go on like this with bombs. Potiorek assured him nothing more would happen, and the party — against its own fixed decision to avoid the town centre and drive straight along the quay — was led by the leading cars into the Franz Joseph Street. As the archduke's car turned the corner, the youth Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb student from Belgrade, fired from point-blank range. One bullet struck the Duchess in the lower body, another the archduke in the throat. The car reversed over the Lateiner bridge and reached the Konak, the governor's residence, in two minutes, but the wounds were mortal. The archduke, streaming blood, lost consciousness and died within the quarter hour; the Duchess was already dead on arrival. The last rites were administered; Archbishop Stadler came with the clergy and prayed over the bodies.

## The aftermath: a burial without honour

The double murder threw the Monarchy into an agony of grief and indignation. Conrad, hearing the news at Agram on his way to a generals' journey, was summoned back to Vienna by the Emperor. He read the crime as no single fanatic's act but the work of an organised plot — "the war-declaration of Serbia against Austria-Hungary," he wrote, which could only be answered by war; the long chain of the Balkan antagonism had found its last link. Czernin, minister at Bukarest, saw the sudden turn of opinion: the whole world, its sympathies briefly with the Monarchy after the murder, was swept by a wave of hatred once the ultimatum followed.

The obsequies themselves were a study in the hierarchy of the court. The Emperor ordered that the imperial couple should not be separated even in death; but the etiquette of the Capuchin crypt, the burial place of the Habsburgs, would not admit the morganatic Duchess of Hohenberg, and so the heir to the throne was laid to rest at his own estate of Artstetten in Lower Austria, without the customary honours and without the attendance of foreign princes. The bodies were brought by rail from Sarajevo to Vienna and, in a dark and silent night procession, to the West station and thence to Artstetten. Czernin, who had known the archduke as a friend, judged him no warmonger but a man who would have reconstructed the Monarchy — a "more a patron than an adversary of the Serbs," in his phrase, and no partisan of a closer attachment to Germany. The shots of Sarajevo removed the one figure of the dynasty whose authority might have weighed against the war party in the weeks that followed.

## Consequences: the road to war

The assassination was the immediate cause of the [[july-crisis-1914]]. Within a month the Monarchy had addressed its ultimatum to Serbia, Russia had mobilised, and the localisation of the conflict — Franz Joseph's fondest hope, as Czernin recalled his words after Bukarest — had failed. The heir's death did not, of itself, make the war; but it supplied the pretext and the passion. It also removed, as Czernin insisted, the most powerful advocate of a different course, leaving the direction of the Monarchy in the hands of the aged [[franz-joseph]], the cautious Berchtold, and the generals who had long argued that the Serbian question must be settled by the sword. The murder of the heir of the house of Habsburg thus stands at the head of the world war of 1914.
