# The Hundred Days Offensive (8 August – 11 November 1918)

**8 August – 11 November 1918**

The final Allied offensive on the western front, conducted under the supreme command of [[marshal-foch]], which broke the German army after the failure of Ludendorff's offensives of the spring and forced Germany to ask for the [[armistice-11-november-1918]] of 11 November 1918. Recouly's chapter on the offensives of Foch opens with the observation, drawn from Casanova, that a man climbs only up to a certain moment; from that point he begins to descend, and in a descent the more rapid the nearer he approaches the end — *motus in fine velocior*. The failure of Ludendorff's 15 July offensive, followed three days later by a graver failure, marked for Ludendorff the beginning of that descent.

## Background

The winter of 1917–1918 had seen the military collapse of Russia and the defeat of Romania; by the peace treaties signed with Russia and Romania in February–March 1918, Germany obtained what it sought (see [[treaty-of-brest-litovsk-1918]]). Germany could be said to have won the war three-quarters of the way on the eastern front. Freed from the East, the German command under Ludendorff threw its last strength into the spring offensives of 1918 on the western front, seeking a decision before the growing weight of American intervention could be brought to bear. The first of these offensives carried the German armies deep toward Paris. But the effort exhausted the German army, and the Allies, compelled at last by the direct menace of a defeat and even of a disaster — "the march of the Germans on Paris, separating the French armies" — created the [[marshal-foch|single command]] under Foch.

## The turn of the tide

Recouly dates the turn to the second battle of the Marne. The failure of Ludendorff's 15 July offensive, followed three days later by a still graver failure, marked the beginning of the German descent. Foch, commanding the Allied forces, seized the initiative. His method, Recouly explains, consisted of multiple attacks harrying "the fragile facade" of the German front — attacks designed to shake the German army of occupation and overwork the German army of shock, in striking contrast with Ludendorff's single, concentrated blows. On 8 August the Allied offensive opened on the western front; Ludendorff himself called it the day of mourning of the German army, *« Der 8. August ist der Trauertag der deutschen Armee »* — the day on which German divisions let themselves be completely broken through, tanks surprising divisional staffs in their headquarters, six or seven of the most particularly resistant German divisions cut to pieces, and troops crying to a fresh division marching bravely to the attack: *« Streikbrecher und Kriegsverlängerer »* — "strike-breakers and war-prolongers!" The French offensive broadened under Foch and [[philippe-petain]]: the Third Army under Humbert attacked, Pétain threw the Tenth Army of Mangin into the attack between the Oise and the Aisne, and Foch invited the British commander to enlarge the struggle toward the north with Byng's army toward Bapaume.

## The collapse of the German army

The psychological effect on Ludendorff was sudden and total. After the events of 8 August, Recouly records, he lost all confidence in victory: the war, in his view, could no longer be won militarily. "Our combat army no longer has its full value," he said. He concluded that peace must be sought as quickly as possible, and the military handed over to the civilians they had so long treated with contempt. Ludendorff offered his resignation to the Kaiser, who naturally refused it; but the German command was broken. Recouly judged Ludendorff's sudden collapse a sign of a character neither very balanced nor very solid, and the resort to the diplomats — on whose shoulders the crushing burden was suddenly laid — an act of considerable naivety, since the first and only thing to attempt was to save the army, or what could still be saved of it, by drawing it back behind the Meuse, perhaps behind the Rhine.

## The pursuit and the armistice

The Hundred Days carried the Allies from the Marne across the old battlefields of 1914–1916 to the frontier. The offensive on the western front coincided with the collapse of the other fronts: the Bulgarian front fell in September (see the Italian account of the Salonica offensive in the [[comando-supremo-la-battaglia-di-vittorio-veneto-1918|Comando Supremo]] volume), and the [[battle-of-vittorio-veneto-1918|Italian offensive of Vittorio Veneto]] in October put the Austro-Hungarian army out of action, isolating Germany militarily. Germany, faced with the ruin of the Quadruple Alliance, asked for an armistice. Foch received the German plenipotentiaries at the little station of Rethondes in the forest of Compiègne on 7 November; the armistice of 11 November ended the fighting on all fronts. In Recouly's frame the Hundred Days were the descent *motus in fine velocior* — the swift, vertiginous fall of the German army once its last offensive had failed, completed by the collapse of its allies.
