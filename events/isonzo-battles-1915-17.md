# The Battles of the Isonzo (1915–1917)

**23 May 1915 – 1917**

The twelve battles of the Isonzo, the long series of offensives fought by the Italian army under [[luigi-cadorna]] against Austria-Hungary along the river Isonzo and on the barren plateau of the Carso, from Italy's entry into the [[italy-ww1-entry-1915|First World War]] in May 1915 until the catastrophe of [[battle-of-caporetto-1917|Caporetto]] in October 1917. The Isonzo front was the principal theatre of the Italian war, the road by which Italy sought to break through toward the "unredeemed" lands of Gorizia, Trieste and the Adriatic that [[irredentism]] demanded; and it was the scene of the most costly and least decisive warfare of the Italian front, a grinding struggle against a defensive line that Cadorna's repeated offensives could rarely move.

## Background

When Italy crossed the frontier on 24 May 1915, the line of battle ran along the eastern frontier of the Veneto, where the river Isonzo flows south out of the Julian Alps toward the Adriatic, and where the Austro-Hungarian army, its chief of staff [[conrad-von-hotzendorf|Conrad]] long having feared and expected Italy's defection, had fortified the passes and the high ground. The Italian war aim — the recovery of the Trentino, Gorizia, Trieste and the Adriatic coast — pointed directly at the Isonzo and the Carso, the barrier behind which lay the coveted coast. Cadorna's strategy, as he sets it out in his account, was the offensive: to break the enemy's line by repeated frontal assaults, forcing the river and carrying the heights that commanded the way to Trieste. The ground favoured the defence, and the first battles, in the summer and autumn of 1915, gained little at heavy cost.

## The years of the offensives

Through 1915 and 1916 the Italian army mounted a series of offensives against the same fortified sector, each numbered in the Italian reckoning as a battle of the Isonzo. In the summer of 1916 the front was violently shaken from another quarter when the Austrians, seeking to relieve the pressure, launched the offensive against the Trentino that Cadorna records as the *Strafexpedition* — the Austrian "punitive" counter-offensive on the Asiago plateau, which threatened the Italian rear and was only arrested by the concentration of the reserves. The autumn of 1916 brought a renewed Italian effort: Cadorna's seventh, eighth and ninth battles of the Isonzo, the offensive operations on the Carso which at last wrested Gorizia from the enemy and carried the Italian line to the threshold of the plateau. The struggles on the bare Carso, where every advance was bought in blood over a shattered limestone wilderness, became the emblem of the war's futility and cost.

## The climax of 1917

In the spring of 1917 the Italian command renewed the offensive: the tenth battle of the Isonzo, opened in May 1917 on the Julian front, pressed the enemy in the direction of the frontier, and was answered, as in previous years, by an Austrian counter-offensive and renewed action on the Trentino. Then, in August–September 1917, Cadorna fought his eleventh battle of the Isonzo — *la battaglia fra l'Idria e il Timavo*, the battle between the Idria and the Timavo, as he entitles it — the most powerful Italian effort of the whole war, in which the army at last broke across the last of the Carso and looked upon the edge of the plain. The twelfth battle was impending in October 1917 when the whole calculus was overturned: the Austrians, reinforced by German divisions, struck at the junction of the Italian line near Caporetto and broke it, and the eleven battles of the Isonzo were swallowed up in the great retreat to the Piave.

## Consequences

The Isonzo offensives exacted an enormous price — repeated months of frontal warfare against the strongest part of the Austrian line, yielding Gorizia but never Trieste, and exhausting the Italian army that had mounted them. They bled the Austro-Hungarian army too, whose repeated defence of the Isonzo and counter-strokes on the Trentino consumed the strength of the monarchy, and the very success of the eleventh battle prepared the Austro-German decision to end the stalemate by a decisive stroke at [[battle-of-caporetto-1917|Caporetto]]. That stroke, breaking the line that twelve battles had failed to move, threw the Italian army back across the Tagliamento to the Piave and brought the fall of Cadorna. The Isonzo thus stands as both the central ordeal of the Italian war and the prelude to its darkest hour — the point of departure for the recovery that would culminate, a year later, in [[battle-of-vittorio-veneto-1918|Vittorio Veneto]].
