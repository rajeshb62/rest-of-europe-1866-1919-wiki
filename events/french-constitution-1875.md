# Constitution of 1875 (February–July 1875)

**February – July 1875**

The three constitutional laws by which France at last organized the Republic, voted in the spring and summer of 1875 by a National Assembly that had spent four years refusing to choose a form of government. The Constitution of 1875 — the Senate law of 24 February, the law on the organization of the public powers of 25 February, and the law on the relations of the powers of 16 July — was the anonymous, improvised foundation of the [[third-republic-france]], adopted almost by accident, and yet it proved durable for sixty-five years.

## Background

Four years after Bordeaux the National Assembly had made peace, repressed the Commune, overthrown Thiers, and voted the [[septennat-1873]], but it still had given no constitution. Its monarchical majority was torn among the three royal houses; the Republic survived by their divisions. After the fall of the duc de Broglie in May 1874 and the failure of the Cissey cabinet, the question of the constitutional organization of the state could no longer be postponed. The republicans, growing with each by-election, demanded the Republic; the right, unable to crown a king, clung to the septennat and to delay.

## The Republic voted by one voice

The decisive session came in January 1875. The commission of the Trente, charged with preparing a constitutional project, worked under presidential pressure from the Marshal and the conferences at the Élysée. The debate turned on the form of the executive and the creation of a Senate. In the great sittings of 28–30 January the Assembly rejected the Laboulaye amendment after Louis Blanc's intervention and then came to the amendment of the deputy Wallon, which proposed that "the President of the Republic is elected by a plurality of the votes of the Senate and of the Chamber of Deputies." The Wallon amendment, the only one that pronounced the word "Republic," was carried by a majority of one voice. Hanotaux records the sense of the hour: the Republic had been founded almost by accident, on a single vote, in an Assembly hostile to it. The majority of one voice proved decisive — the monarchy, in effect, was never to return.

## The three laws

The consequence of the Wallon vote was the elaboration of the full constitutional settlement. The law on the organization of the Senate was voted (24 February), then the law on the organization of the public powers (25 February), and finally the law on the relations of the public powers (16 July), together with the organic laws on senatorial and legislative elections. The Constitution of 1875 established a President of the Republic, a Senate and a Chamber of Deputies, and a responsible ministry; it created a two-Chamber parliamentary system with a strong presidency — the Marshal, who had hoped for a conservative Senate, retained the power to dissolve the Chamber with the Senate's consent, and the seat of the powers remained at Versailles. Hanotaux stresses the improvised, unplanned character of the whole: three constitutional laws and two organic laws, viable despite the disdain of many of their authors, a work of equilibrium and transaction.

## Consequences

The Constitution of 1875 founded the Republic on the very hesitations of its enemies. The septennat had kept a king possible; the one-vote Republic made it impossible. The new institutions were immediately put to the test: the Senate's composition, the powers of the president, and the responsibility of the ministry became the ground of the struggle between Mac-Mahon and the republican majority that exploded in the [[seize-mai-1877]] crisis. Yet the settlement held: the parliamentary Republic, once voted, proved stronger than the monarchists who had voted it by accident. The Constitution of 1875 was the durable frame of the Third Republic, surviving until 1940 — the improvised work that the founders themselves had despised.
