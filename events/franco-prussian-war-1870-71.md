# The Franco-Prussian War (1870–71)

**15 July 1870 – 10 May 1871**

The war between the French Empire and the German states under Prussian leadership, opened by the French declaration of 15 July 1870, decided by the defeats of August and the capitulations of Sedan, Metz and Paris, and closed by the [[treaty-of-frankfurt-1871]] of 10 May 1871. It destroyed the [[second-empire-france]], proclaimed the [[german-empire-proclaimed-1871]], and ended with the birth of the [[third-republic-france]] under the shadow of defeat.

## Background

The war grew out of the [[hohenzollern-candidature-1870]] and the [[ems-dispatch-1870]]. France, whose liberal ministry under [[emile-ollivier]] had gone to the brink over the Spanish candidacy, declared war on Prussia on **15 July 1870** — in Ollivier's famous and subsequently condemned phrase, "le cœur léger," a war he believed forced upon France by Bismarck's provocation. Hanotaux, writing the national history, gives the deeper cause: the imperial policy of nationalities, which had humiliated Prussia's neighbours but now confronted Prussia itself, led France into a war for which her army and her diplomacy were unprepared, while Prussia enjoyed the military conventions that had placed the armies of the southern German states under her command.

## The opening battles, August 1870

The war opened disastrously for France. On **4 August** the Crown Prince's army beat the French at Wissembourg on the Lauter; on **6 August** came the double defeats of [[battle-of-woerth-1870]] in Alsace, where Marshal Mac-Mahon's army was broken after a heroic resistance, and of Forbach near the Saar, where Frossard's corps, though not beaten on the field, panicked and fled toward Sarreguemines. Ollivier's memoir of these days is a study in the confusion of the French command: Mac-Mahon had resolved on a retreat and was drawn back into battle by the cannon; Frossard "constituted himself as beaten"; and the Emperor, whose health had collapsed, lost effective direction of the war. On **9 August** the Chamber, turned against his ministry, forced Ollivier's resignation, and the government passed to General Cousin-Montauban, comte de Palikao.

## Sedan and the fall of the Empire

The decisive stroke of the campaign was the encirclement of Mac-Mahon's army of Châlons, marching to relieve Marshal Bazaine at Metz. At **[[battle-of-sedan-1870]]** on 1 September 1870 the whole army was caught in the funnel of the Belgian frontier and destroyed; Napoleon III, who had accompanied it, ordered the white flag raised and surrendered in person. Hanotaux stresses the political consequence: at Sedan "Napoleon III did not only make himself prisoner" — the fall of the emperor and of an army opened a void of power, the "vacance du pouvoir," filled on **4 September** by the proclamation of the Republic in Paris. The Empire was finished with its sovereign.

## The siege of Paris and the close of the war

The war continued under the Government of National Defence, with Paris [[siege-of-paris-1870-71|besieged from September 1870]] and the armies of Bazaine capitulating at Metz in October. Hanotaux records how Paris let itself be reduced to famine, how the ardor of Gambetta in the provinces prolonged a hopeless struggle, and how the armies of the East and the Loire successively collapsed. Paris, reduced to famine, surrendered; its fall, in Hanotaux's words, was the fall of France. An armistice was concluded, and the nation went to the polls.

## The elections, Bordeaux and the peace

The elections of **8 February 1871** — dominated by the great debate of War or Peace, with republicans calling for *guerre à outrance* and the departments voting for peace — returned a National Assembly that met at Bordeaux and turned to **[[adolphe-thiers]]**, elected in twenty-six departments, as "administrator of the national misfortune" and chief of the executive power. Thiers negotiated the preliminaries of peace with Bismarck at Versailles in February 1871, accepting the loss of Alsace and part of Lorraine while retaining Belfort and reducing the indemnity. The definitive **Treaty of Frankfurt of 10 May 1871** fixed the five-milliard indemnity and the occupation schedule (see [[treaty-of-frankfurt-1871]]). Even as the peace was being ratified, Paris rose against the Assembly in the **[[paris-commune-1871]]**, crushed by the Versailles army under Mac-Mahon in the Bloody Week of May 1871. The war ended with France dismembered, republican by accident of defeat, and with the German Empire proclaimed at Versailles in January 1871.
