# Battle of Lissa (20 July 1866)

**July 20, 1866**

## Background

The Battle of Lissa — named for the island in the Adriatic off the Dalmatian coast, rendered by the
Austrians as *Lissa* — was the one significant naval engagement of the Austro-Prussian War, and it
was an Austrian triumph. It came about because the Italian campaign, after the defeat at Custoza on
24 June, had settled into stalemate on land. With the army unable to make headway against the
Austrians in Venetia, the Italians turned to the sea, where their ironclad fleet was expected to
bring Austria to heel and to carry Italian ambitions across the Adriatic against the Dalmatian and
Istrian coasts that Italy coveted.

The opposing admirals were men whom Sybel judged to be of a kind: the Italian Admiral Carlo di
Persano and the Austrian Admiral Wilhelm von Tegetthoff are described as "völlig gleichwerthige und
geistesverwandte" — entirely equivalent and kindred spirits. Both commanded fleets built around the
new ironclad warships, the armoured steamers that had made the wooden navies of the sailing age
obsolete. Lissa would be one of the first great fleet actions of the ironclad age, a test of whether
the armoured ship had changed the nature of naval combat — and of whether the new machines could be
handled with the daring of the old.

## The Fleets and the Setting

The Italian fleet, numerically superior and strongly armed with ironclads, was engaged in operations
off the island of Lissa in mid-July, bombarding the Austrian garrison and preparing an assault on
the island's fortress. Persano's fleet lay at anchor or in loose formation off the heights before
Lissa, intent on its siege. Tegetthoff, at the head of the Austrian fleet, resolved to break up the
Italian operations and came down upon them with a plan of the simplest aggression: to close at
speed, ram the enemy line, and throw the Italians into disorder at close quarters.

The two forces were products of a transition in naval warfare. Wooden ships-of-the-line were giving
way to armoured steamers, and Lissa was one of the first battles in which these ironclads fought a
major fleet action against one another. Ramming, which had been a forgotten art in the age of sail,
was revived as the ironclads' chief offensive weapon, for the armoured ships had no turrets capable
of bringing heavy fire to bear ahead and astern. The battle would therefore be decided in the close,
crashing mêlée of hull against hull.

## The Battle of 20 July

On 20 July 1866 Tegetthoff's fleet came upon the Italians on the heights before Lissa, where Persano
was still engaged in the siege of the island. Tegetthoff did not hesitate: he launched a *kräftigen
Angriff* — a vigorous attack — falling upon the Italians with his armoured squadron in a wedge
formation that broke into their line. Sybel records the result as a thorough defeat for Persano, who
was "gründlich geschlagen" ("thoroughly beaten").

The Austrians rammed and broke the Italian line, sinking the ironclad *Re d'Italia*, which went down
with great loss of life, and disabling other Italian vessels; Persano's fleet was thrown into
confusion and compelled to withdraw. The victory was won against a numerically and materially
formidable enemy, and it was achieved by the sheer aggression of the Austrian attack and the
superior handling of the Austrian ironclads. Lissa was a stunning demonstration that Austrian arms,
humbled on land at Königgrätz a fortnight before, could still prevail at sea.

## A Victory Without Fruits

Lissa entrenched the Austrian reputation as the premier naval power of the Adriatic and established
Tegetthoff as one of the most celebrated admirals of his generation. It was the first great fleet
action of the ironclad age and set the pattern for the close-quarters, ramming tactics that would
characterise armoured naval combat in the decades that followed. For the Austrians it was a point of
pride in an otherwise disastrous year.

Yet, like Custoza, Lissa had no ultimate effect on the distribution of territory. Austria, crushed
by Prussia on land, was forced to cede Venetia to Italy in the peace, and the Adriatic settlements
were not altered by Tegetthoff's triumph at sea. Italy, though beaten twice, still received Venetia,
and the fleet's humiliation changed nothing in the final terms.

## Consequences

The battle's significance lay rather in what it signified for the future. It fixed the ironclad as
the decisive instrument of naval war, and the revival of ramming tactics that it demonstrated would
shape the construction and doctrine of the world's navies until the battleship and the torpedo came
to dominate. The Austrian victory at Lissa was studied by every naval power in Europe.

For Italy, the double reverse of Custoza and Lissa left a lasting scar on military and naval pride.
The navy that was meant to complete the work of the Risorgimento by extending Italian power across
the Adriatic had instead been driven off in confusion, and the resentment rankled. The defeats
sharpened the discontent with the performance of Italian arms that would colour the country's
politics for decades, and fed the irredentist longing for the lands Austria still held — a longing
that would one day draw Italy back into war against the Habsburg monarchy.
