# Battle of Königgrätz (3 July 1866)

**July 3, 1866**

## Background

The Battle of Königgrätz — known to the Austrians and to much of Europe as Sadowa, after the village
near the battlefield — was the decisive engagement of the Bohemian campaign and of the Austro-
Prussian War as a whole. Its prelude lay in the concentration of forces on either side through June
1866. Austria's Northern Army, under Feldzeugmeister Ludwig von Benedek, had gathered at Olmütz;
Sybel's account stresses the command controversies, organisational problems, and logistical
deficiencies that hampered it from the start. Benedek's army was large and veteran, hardened in the
Italian wars, but it was badly served from the top and crippled by uncertain direction and a
defensive-minded plan.

Prussia, by contrast, was served by a general staff whose plan was precise and audacious. The
Prussian forces crossed into Bohemia in three separate columns: the Elbe Army under Herwarth von
Bittenfeld, the First Army under Prince Friedrich Karl, and the Second Army under Crown Prince
Friedrich Wilhelm, heir to the throne of King Wilhelm I. The design of Helmut von Moltke, the
Prussian chief of staff, was that these three columns, advancing on divergent roads, should be drawn
together by a common objective and unite on the battlefield rather than in a preliminary manoeuvre.
The plan was risk-laden — the columns marched separately and might have been beaten in detail before
they could join — but it was vindicated at Königgrätz.

## The Advance into Bohemia

The road to the decisive battle ran through a series of preliminary engagements. The Prussians
pushed into Bohemia and met the Austrians and their allied Saxon and south German contingents at
Münchengrätz and then at Gitschin (Jičín). These actions, as Sybel recounts them, cleared the way
for the Prussian concentration and forced the Austrians back toward the fortress line along the
Elbe. Benedek's army, already wracked by the command controversies of the preceding weeks, fell back
in some confusion, while the three Prussian columns, still separated, pressed forward on a
converging course.

By the beginning of July Moltke's concentration was nearly complete. The Elbe and First Armies had
joined on the Prussian left, and the Crown Prince's Second Army was approaching from the northeast.
Benedek, choosing to give battle rather than retreat further, drew up his army before the fortress
of Königgrätz, near the village of Sadowa, with the little Bistritz stream in his front and the Elbe
at his back. The position was strong defensively but offered no easy line of retreat, and it
committed the Austrians to a decisive stand on ground of Benedek's own choosing.

## The Battle of 3 July

On the morning of 3 July 1866 the Prussian First and Elbe Armies opened the battle, crossing the
Bistritz and pressing against the Austrian centre and left. The fighting was heavy, and for much of
the day the Prussian attacks made only halting progress against the Austrian artillery and infantry
fire. The day hung in the balance. Moltke's whole conception depended on the arrival of the Crown
Prince's Second Army on the Austrian flank, and the longer the battle continued without it, the more
precarious the Prussian position became.

The arrival came at last, and it decided the day. The Second Army fell upon the Austrian right and
flank with crushing force, and Benedek's position collapsed under the converging pressure of two
fronts. The Austrian army was not annihilated — Benedek managed to extricate much of it across the
Elbe, sacrificing his rearguards to save the rest — but it was decisively beaten and could not
resume the campaign. The Prussians took tens of thousands of prisoners, along with guns and colours,
and the field was theirs.

## The Needle-Gun and the Reasons for Victory

The Prussian victory was owed in no small part to the needle-gun (*Zündnadelgewehr*), the Prussian
breech-loading rifle that could be loaded and fired lying down, and far more rapidly than the
Austrian muzzle-loaders. Sybel gives the weapon a central place in his tactical analysis of the day.
The Austrian infantry, still firing the older rifled muskets in the slow manner of the muzzle-
loading age, was mown down as it advanced or held in place, while the Prussian infantry poured out a
far heavier and more continuous fire. The needle-gun was not the only cause of the victory, but it
was the technological edge that turned a hard-fought day into a rout.

## Consequences

Königgrätz decided the war. Vienna at once sought an armistice, and the road lay open to the
negotiations that produced the preliminary Peace of Nikolsburg and, in August, the definitive Peace
of Prague. The victory transformed Prussia's standing in Germany: with the Confederation's leading
military power humbled, Prussia could annex Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, and Frankfurt and proceed
to found the North German Confederation under its own leadership.

For Europe, Sadowa was a shock. It announced a new great power at the heart of the continent and
redrew the map of Germany within weeks. France in particular was alarmed at the sudden consolidation
of Prussian power on its eastern frontier, and the humiliation of 1866 rankled in Paris — a
resentment that fed directly into the crisis and war of 1870. The way was prepared for the ultimate
reckoning with France, and for the proclamation of the German Empire four years after the guns fell
silent at Königgrätz.
