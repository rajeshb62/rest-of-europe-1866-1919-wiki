# The Occupation of Bosnia-Herzegovina (1878)

**Late July–November 1878**

The Austro-Hungarian military occupation of Bosnia-Herzegovina, undertaken in the late summer and autumn of 1878 under the mandate of the [[congress-of-berlin-1878|Congress of Berlin]], was the monarchy's first great act on the Balkan stage of the period. Under the terms of Article 25 of the Berlin treaty Austria-Hungary was to occupy and administer the two provinces, but the task proved to be a hard campaign rather than a peaceful entry: the Austro-Hungarian army, advancing in several columns, met armed resistance from the local population, fought a series of engagements, and took the provincial capital Sarajevo only after heavy fighting on 19 August 1878. The occupation gave the monarchy its principal Balkan acquisition, laid the foundations of thirty years of administration, and opened the road that led to the [[bosnian-annexation-crisis-1908|annexation of 1908]].

## Background: the mandate and the eve of the entry

The occupation followed directly upon the [[congress-of-berlin-1878|Berlin settlement]], which had given Austria-Hungary the mandate to occupy and administer Bosnia-Herzegovina while the Sultan's formal sovereignty was reserved. The diplomacy of [[gyula-andrassy]] had prepared the ground: the monarchy's Balkan programme, the agreement with Russia at Reichstadt, and the last-moment protocol at Berlin all pointed to the entry. The condition of the provinces strengthened the case — Bosnia and Herzegovina lay under Turkish rule in a state of anarchy, agrarian misery and confessional hatred, and the Austro-Hungarian claim rested on the responsibility of the monarchy to restore order and security on its eastern frontier.

Sosnosky's account of the eve of the entry (*Vor dem Einmarsch*) describes the military preparations of the monarchy and the theatre of operations. The task was not expected to be easy: the two provinces, mountainous and difficult of access, were peopled by a population that regarded the coming of foreign troops with mistrust, and the Austro-Hungarian command prepared for resistance. The occupation troops — whose order of battle Sosnosky appends for the beginning of August 1878 — were organised to advance into the country and to overcome whatever opposition the country might offer.

## The campaign

The campaign was fought as a series of columns converging on the interior. Sosnosky's account of the occupation divides the operations into the several columns — the central, the right and the left — and the separate theatres: the operations in Herzegovina, the march on Sarajevo, the operations in the Posavina and the Župa, the renewed fighting in Herzegovina, the operations in southern Bosnia and in northwestern Bosnia, and finally the return home. The resistance was more serious than the monarchy had allowed: the insurgent forces, the *Streitkräfte der Insurgenten*, met the Austro-Hungarian army with determined opposition, and the occupation required a real war rather than a ceremonial entry.

The decisive moment was the capture of Sarajevo on 19 August 1878. The march on the provincial capital and its storming were the climax of the campaign, and after the fall of the city the Austro-Hungarian forces regrouped, pursued the remaining resistance in the outlying districts, and gradually reduced the country to submission. The fighting in Herzegovina, in the Posavina and the Župa, and in the south and northwest of the country dragged on through the autumn, and the final return home of the occupation troops came only after the whole country had been brought under the monarchy's control.

## The cost and the meaning

Sosnosky closes his account of the occupation with a summing-up of the campaign, its cost and its meaning. The occupation of Bosnia-Herzegovina was bought with real losses — the fighting had been sharp and the country difficult to subdue — but it gave the monarchy the prize that its Balkan policy had sought since the crisis of 1875–78. The provinces became an Austro-Hungarian acquisition administered from Vienna, and the monarchy, which had retired from Germany after 1866, found in the East the field of its renewed greatness. The occupation was the origin of the Dual Monarchy's most lasting Balkan commitment: thirty years of administration followed, and the provisional character of the mandate — the Sultan's sovereignty reserved at Berlin — carried within it the ambiguity that [[alois-aehrenthal|Aehrenthal]] resolved by the annexation of 1908. The road from the occupation of 1878 to the [[bosnian-annexation-crisis-1908|annexation crisis]] is the central thread of the monarchy's Eastern policy in the period, and the occupation stands as its beginning.
